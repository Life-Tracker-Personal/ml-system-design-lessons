## TL;DR

Classification evaluation works because each input has one correct label, so a prediction is right or wrong and you can count. Generation breaks that: there are many acceptable outputs, no enumerable label set, and the same model produces different text on the same input. Every generative metric is an attempt to recover a countable quantity from that mess. Reference-based metrics do it by comparing against a written-down answer, which works when the answer space is genuinely narrow (extraction, SQL, code) and degrades to noise when it is not. n-gram overlap scores like BLEU and ROUGE are the weakest form of this and leading with them in 2026 dates you. What replaced them is task-grounded checks plus **LLM-as-judge**: a second model reading the output against an explicit criterion.

The move that separates a senior answer from a junior one is this: **the evaluator is itself a system that must be evaluated.** An LLM judge is a classifier with a TPR and a TNR, and you measure it against held-out human labels and report those two numbers separately, because raw agreement lies under class imbalance. A human panel is also a measurement instrument, and Cohen's kappa exists for exactly the same reason — raw agreement between two annotators is inflated by prevalence. This lesson derives both corrections with arithmetic, covers the three judge biases with real magnitudes, and ends with the observation that the metrics you actually ship are almost always task-specific and binary.

## What you'll be able to do after this lesson

- Say precisely why a classification metric does not transfer to open-ended generation, and name the two properties that break.
- Compute token-level F1 and explain in one sentence why n-gram overlap fails on paraphrase, using a worked example.
- Build an LLM judge from real traces instead of imagined failure modes, with one binary criterion per named failure.
- Name position, verbosity and self-preference bias, quote their measured magnitudes, and state the specific control for each.
- Choose pairwise vs pointwise judging based on what decision the number supports.
- Validate a judge against human labels, report TPR and TNR, and correct a measured failure rate for the judge's own error rates.
- Compute Cohen's kappa by hand and explain why 85% agreement can still be a bad panel.

## Part 1 — Why generation breaks the classification playbook

A spam classifier has a finite label set and one correct label per email. Two properties follow, and every classification metric leans on both. First, **the correct answer is unique and enumerable**, so equality is a valid comparison. Second, **the system is deterministic** at a fixed threshold, so one run is one measurement.

Generation has neither. "Summarize this ticket" has an unbounded set of good answers, and equality against any one of them is meaningless. Sampling at temperature above zero means the same input yields different output on Tuesday than Monday, so a single run is a draw from a distribution and one number is an estimate with variance. These two properties, not the model architecture, are what force every design choice in the rest of the chapter: you need a comparison operator that is not equality, and you need repeated sampling with an uncertainty interval instead of a point score.

There is a third property that is easy to miss. In classification the label is cheap and objective — a human looks at the email and says spam. In generation the label is often a *judgment* — is this summary faithful, is this tone right — which means the labeling process itself has error, and that error has to be measured. That is why half this lesson is about grading the graders.

The consequence people resist: **an aggregate "quality score" is not a metric.** A number is only a metric if it supports a specific decision — ship or don't, gate this PR or don't, roll back or don't. A 1–5 quality score supports nothing, because nobody can say what separates a 3 from a 4, and two annotators will not agree on it. Hold onto that; it comes back in Part 3.

## Part 2 — Reference-based metrics, and where they run out

A reference-based metric compares output to a written answer. They form a ladder of increasing tolerance.

**Exact match** is string equality after normalization (lowercase, strip punctuation and articles). It is the right metric more often than people admit — for structured extraction, classification-shaped generation, SQL results, tool-call arguments. If the answer space is narrow and you can normalize it, exact match is unambiguous, free, deterministic, and nobody argues with it in a review.

**Token F1** relaxes to bag-of-tokens overlap. Gold answer `the refund window is 30 days`, prediction `you have thirty days to request a refund`. Normalized tokens overlap on `refund` and `days` only. Precision is $2/7 \approx 0.29$ (7 prediction tokens after dropping articles), recall is $2/5 = 0.40$, so

$$
F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot 0.29 \cdot 0.40}{0.29 + 0.40} \approx 0.34
$$

The prediction is completely correct and scores 0.34. That is the whole indictment of surface overlap in one number.

**BLEU and ROUGE** are the n-gram versions of the same idea — BLEU is precision-weighted over n-grams with a brevity penalty, ROUGE is recall-weighted — and they inherit the same failure amplified, because at $n = 4$ the example above scores exactly zero. The one sentence worth keeping: *n-gram overlap measures surface form, so for open-ended output it penalizes correct paraphrase and rewards fluent copying of the reference.* Know that sentence and move on. Leading with BLEU as your answer to "how do you evaluate generation" is the single clearest dating signal in this domain.

**BERTScore** replaces token equality with embedding similarity: embed every token of the candidate and the reference with a contextual encoder, greedily match each candidate token to its most similar reference token, average the cosine similarities, and combine precision and recall into an F1. It genuinely fixes paraphrase — `thirty days` and `30 days` land close. It does not fix the deeper problem. It still needs one reference to be *the* answer, and it inherits the blindness of the embedding space it is built on, most sharply on negation: "the drug is safe" and "the drug is not safe" are topically near-identical and score high against each other. If you read c5.1, that is the same failure mode as retrieval, from the same cause.

So the ladder ends where it started. Reference-based metrics work when the answer space is narrow. When it is wide, no amount of softening the comparison operator saves you, because the premise — that a single written reference defines correctness — is what is wrong.

## Part 3 — Building an LLM judge, and its three biases

An LLM judge is a model prompted to grade an output. The prompt contains the criterion in one sentence, the inputs it needs (request, retrieved context, output), two or three few-shot exemplars chosen *near the decision boundary* rather than obvious cases, an instruction to state its reasoning before its verdict, and a structured output with a boolean field. Reasoning-before-verdict matters mechanically: the verdict token is conditioned on the reasoning tokens only if they come first.

Judges are biased in three specific ways, and the measured magnitudes are worth quoting because they are larger than people expect.

**Position bias.** In pairwise comparison, judges favor whichever response is in a particular slot. Measured rates: GPT-3.5 position-biased on roughly 50% of comparisons, Claude-v1 on roughly 70%. The control is to run every comparison twice with the order swapped, count only the comparisons where the verdict is consistent, and report the flip rate as a diagnostic. A high flip rate is not noise to be averaged away; it means the judge cannot tell these responses apart, and averaging manufactures a decision out of nothing.

**Verbosity bias.** Both of those models preferred the longer response more than 90% of the time. The control is to check whether the length distributions of your two systems differ, and if they do, either stratify the comparison by length bucket or say plainly that the comparison is confounded. A model that got longer and "won" on a judge may have won only by getting longer.

**Self-preference.** A judge favors output from its own model family — GPT-4 self-favoring by around 10 points of win rate, Claude-v1 by around 25. The control is to grade with a judge from a different family than the candidate, and when you are comparing two families to each other, grade with both judges and check the verdicts agree.

**Pointwise vs pairwise.** Pointwise means grading one output against a criterion; pairwise means picking a winner between two. Pairwise is easier for both models and humans, because relative judgment is more reliable than absolute, and it is the right shape for "did this prompt change help overall". But it gives you no absolute level, it cannot tell you *which* failure mode moved, it has no fixed reference point across time, and it is where position and verbosity bias live. Pointwise binary per failure mode gives a rate you can put a CI on, gate CI on, and track across releases. Use pointwise binary for regression gates, pairwise for exploratory "is B better than A".

## Part 4 — Validating the judge like a classifier

Here is the differentiator. A judge that flags hallucinations is a binary classifier. Hold out a set of examples labeled by a domain expert and measure it the way you would measure any classifier — and report **TPR and TNR separately**, never raw agreement.

Take 200 held-out human-labeled traces where the true failure rate is 10%, so 20 real failures and 180 clean. Suppose the judge has $\text{TPR} = 0.50$ and $\text{TNR} = 0.95$. Then it catches 10 of 20 failures, misses 10, and false-flags $0.05 \times 180 = 9$ clean traces. Raw agreement is

$$
\frac{10 + 171}{200} = 0.905
$$

90.5% agreement, and the judge misses half of all hallucinations. Its precision is $10/19 \approx 0.53$, so a coin flip on flagged items. Anyone reporting "our judge agrees with humans 90% of the time" has reported nothing. This is the same prevalence trap that c1.10 covers for accuracy under imbalance; it is more dangerous here because the number sounds like a validation.

Once you *have* TPR and TNR, you can do something useful with them. On unlabeled production traffic you observe a flag rate $q$. The judge flags a trace either by correctly catching a true failure or by false-flagging a clean one, so with true prevalence $p$:

$$
q = \text{TPR} \cdot p + (1 - \text{TNR})(1 - p)
$$

Solve for the quantity you actually care about:

$$
p = \frac{q - (1 - \text{TNR})}{\text{TPR} - (1 - \text{TNR})}
$$

With our numbers, $q = 0.05 + 0.45p$. Observe a 14% flag rate and the true hallucination rate is $(0.14 - 0.05)/0.45 = 0.20$ — double the raw reading. Two caveats to state out loud: the correction assumes TPR and TNR are stable on the new traffic, which fails when the traffic mix shifts, so you re-measure the judge on a fresh human-labeled sample periodically. And the denominator is $\text{TPR} - (1-\text{TNR})$, so a weak judge makes the correction explode — near-zero denominator, huge variance in $p$. A judge whose TPR and TNR sum to barely more than 1 carries almost no information.

The practical target reported by practitioners is above 90% agreement with a domain expert, reached after about three rounds of prompt refinement against disagreement cases. That is the loop: run the judge, pull the cases where it disagrees with the expert, read them, fix the criterion wording, re-run.

## Part 5 — Human evaluation and agreement

Humans are the ground truth that everything above is anchored to, which means the human process needs the same scrutiny.

A **rubric** is the criterion written so two people apply it identically. That means binary where possible, one dimension at a time, the decision procedure spelled out ("mark unsupported if any factual claim does not appear in the provided context, ignoring style"), and worked examples on both sides of the boundary. If two trained annotators disagree, the rubric is broken, not the annotators.

You measure that with **inter-annotator agreement**, and raw agreement is not enough because two annotators who both say "pass" 90% of the time will agree 82% of the time by chance alone. **Cohen's kappa** corrects for exactly that:

$$
\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}
$$

where $p_o$ is observed agreement and $p_e$ is the agreement expected if both raters labeled independently at their own observed rates. The numerator is agreement above chance; the denominator is the agreement available above chance, so $\kappa = 1$ is perfect and $\kappa = 0$ is chance-level.

Two raters, 100 items. Both pass 80, both fail 5, A passes where B fails 10, A fails where B passes 5.

$$
p_o = \frac{80 + 5}{100} = 0.85
$$

Rater A passes 90 times, B passes 85 times, so

$$
p_e = (0.90)(0.85) + (0.10)(0.15) = 0.765 + 0.015 = 0.78
$$

$$
\kappa = \frac{0.85 - 0.78}{1 - 0.78} = \frac{0.07}{0.22} \approx 0.32
$$

85% agreement, kappa 0.32 — fair at best. The panel is agreeing mostly because almost everything passes. This is the prevalence effect, and it is why kappa collapses on rare failure modes: when 95% of items are clean, chance agreement is already high and there is little room above it. The practical response is to **oversample suspected failures into the annotation set** so the labeled set is closer to balanced, then correct back when estimating the production rate.

For more than two raters the same idea generalizes to Fleiss' kappa, and Krippendorff's alpha extends it to mixed data types and missing labels. Know what each corrects for and stop there; the derivations are not asked.

## Part 6 — Benchmarks and task-specific metrics

Public benchmarks measure the capability of a base model, and they answer "which model should I start from", not "does my system work". They also degrade over time: test items leak into pretraining corpora (**contamination**), and a benchmark whose answers a model has memorized reports capability it does not have. That is the entire useful content of the topic for an engineering interview. Be aware that prep repos are saturated with benchmark suites, judge frameworks and contamination detection — that material is genuinely interesting and almost never asked. Do not spend your prep budget there.

What you actually ship is task-specific and mostly binary:

- **Structured extraction** — per-field exact match after normalization, reported per field. One aggregate hides that the date field is broken.
- **Code generation** — run the test suite. The metric is "tests pass", and it is deterministic ground truth.
- **Text-to-SQL** — execution match: run both queries, compare result sets. Never string-compare the SQL.
- **RAG answers** — groundedness as the fraction of factual claims supported by a retrieved span, with citations as the mechanism that makes it checkable.
- **Agents** — an assertion on the final environment state, not on the transcript. Did the file get written, did the booking exist.
- **Anything classification-shaped** — the machinery in c1.10 applies unchanged, including choosing the threshold from a cost matrix.

The pattern across all six: find the place where the task has a deterministic checkable consequence, and measure that instead of measuring the prose.

## Tradeoff matrix

| Method | Cost per item | Deterministic | Needs reference | Best for | Main failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Exact match | ~0 | Yes | Yes | Extraction, tool args, narrow answers | Useless when answers vary in form |
| Token F1 | ~0 | Yes | Yes | Short-answer QA | Punishes correct paraphrase |
| BLEU / ROUGE | ~0 | Yes | Yes | Legacy MT and summarization reporting | Zero score on correct rewording |
| BERTScore | Low | Yes | Yes | Paraphrase-tolerant comparison | Blind to negation; still needs one right answer |
| Deterministic assertion (tests, execution match, state check) | Low | Yes | No | Code, SQL, agents | Only exists where the task has a checkable consequence |
| LLM judge, pointwise binary | Medium | No | No | Named failure modes, CI gates | Must be validated; biased if unchecked |
| LLM judge, pairwise | Medium | No | No | "Is B better than A" overall | Position and verbosity bias; no absolute level |
| Human panel | High | No | No | Ground truth, judge calibration | Slow; needs rubric and kappa above chance |

## Production failure modes

1. **The judge drifts when the underlying model version changes.** You pin the candidate model and forget the judge is also a hosted model that gets updated. Your measured failure rate moves and no code changed. Pin the judge version, and keep a fixed gold set that you re-run on every judge change.
2. **The judge is graded once and never again.** TPR and TNR were measured on last quarter's traffic. Traffic shifted, the judge silently got worse, and every corrected rate downstream is wrong. Re-label a fresh sample on a schedule.
3. **Reward hacking against the judge.** Prompt iteration optimizes for whatever the judge rewards. If the judge likes long hedged answers with citations, you get long hedged answers with citations regardless of correctness. Keep a held-out set the prompt was never tuned against, and watch for the gap between judge score and human score widening.
4. **The eval set stops matching traffic.** It was built from launch-week queries; a year later the product has new surfaces and the eval set covers none of them. Refresh continuously from real traffic, stratified by the slices you care about.
5. **Aggregate score hides a slice regression.** Overall pass rate rises one point while non-English queries drop eight. Always gate per slice, never on the aggregate alone.
6. **Single-run comparison of a stochastic system.** Version B "wins" by two points, which is inside the noise of running B twice. Sample repeatedly per item, compare paired, report an interval.
7. **Annotator quality decays.** A subset of annotators start clicking through. Agreement drops, labels get noisier, and the judge you calibrate against them inherits the noise. Seed gold-standard items into the queue and track per-annotator agreement against them.

## Interview gotchas

- **"How would you evaluate the quality of our LLM's outputs?"** Do not list metrics. Ask what decision the number supports, then: read ~100 real traces, cluster the first failure in each into a counted taxonomy, build one binary judge per observed failure mode, validate each judge against human labels reporting TPR and TNR, and gate releases per slice. A metric list with no decision attached is the most common weak answer in this domain.
- **"You say your LLM judge agrees with humans 90% of the time. Is that good?"** No, and it may be meaningless. If the failure rate is 10%, a judge that never flags anything scores 90%. Report TPR and TNR separately, plus the class balance of the validation set.
- **"How do you stop a model-based grader from favoring its own model family?"** Name it as self-preference bias, quote the magnitude (around 10 points for GPT-4, around 25 for Claude-v1 in the published measurement), and use a judge from a different family; when comparing families, grade with both and require agreement.
- **"Why not just use BLEU?"** One sentence: it measures surface n-gram overlap, so a correct paraphrase can score zero and a fluent copy of the reference scores high. Then pivot to task-grounded checks.
- **"Two annotators agree 85% of the time. Is your rubric good?"** Compute kappa. In the example above 85% agreement is $\kappa = 0.32$, because almost everything passes. Prevalence inflates raw agreement.
- **"Should the judge give a 1–5 score?"** Prefer binary per named failure mode. A binary rate has a confidence interval and can gate a release; a 1–5 mean moves for reasons nobody can articulate, and annotators cannot reproduce the 3/4 boundary.
- **"What's the difference between a guardrail and an eval?"** A guardrail is synchronous, deterministic, inline and blocking — schema validation, PII filter, refusal policy. An eval is asynchronous measurement feeding an improvement loop. Naming the distinction is a cheap seniority signal and it comes up in reliability-design prompts.
- **"Do public benchmarks tell you your system works?"** No. They rank base models, they saturate, and contaminated test items inflate them. Your eval set comes from your traffic.
