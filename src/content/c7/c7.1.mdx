## TL;DR

Across a corpus of 519 reported quant interview questions from Jane Street, Citadel, Two Sigma, Optiver and SIG, 67% touch probability and 21% ask for an expected value. The striking thing is how few distinct techniques you need. Four tools do most of the work: **first-step analysis** (write one equation per state, solve the linear system), **linearity of expectation via indicator variables** (sum a pile of yes/no counters, ignore their dependence), **Bayes with a base rate** (posterior odds equal prior odds times a likelihood ratio), and **difference equations** for absorption probability and hitting time (gambler's ruin, and everything that is secretly gambler's ruin).

Almost every famous problem you have heard of is one of these four wearing a costume. Expected flips to see HHT is first-step analysis. Coupon collector is indicators. The 99%-accurate test for a rare disease is a base rate. The ant on a cube is gambler's ruin with the state space collapsed by symmetry. This lesson derives each tool and then drills it on the actual reported questions, with the arithmetic done out loud.

## What you'll be able to do after this lesson

- Set up and solve conditional-expectation state equations for a pattern-waiting problem, and explain why HHT takes 8 flips but HTH takes 10.
- Use indicator variables to compute an expected count without ever checking independence, and say exactly why that is legal.
- Convert a base-rate problem into natural frequencies in your head and get the posterior right in 20 seconds.
- Solve the biased-coin-from-a-jar problem and its follow-up ("what is the chance the next flip is heads").
- Derive both gambler's ruin results from a difference equation, and re-derive them in one line each with optional stopping.
- Recognize a random walk on a graph as gambler's ruin after a symmetry collapse.

## Part 1 — First-step analysis: conditional-expectation state equations

The tool rests on one identity, the law of total expectation. For any partition of the first move into outcomes $A_i$,

$$
E[X] = \sum_i P(A_i)\, E[X \mid A_i]
$$

The trick is to choose states so that after one step you are always back in a state you already named. Then you get a small linear system and you solve it.

### Expected flips until HHT

This one is Glassdoor-tagged to Jane Street more or less verbatim. Flip a fair coin until the sequence HHT appears. How many flips on average?

Name the states by **how much of the target pattern the current suffix has matched**. State $0$ is no progress, state $H$ means the last flip was H, state $HH$ means the last two were HH. Let $E_0$, $E_H$, $E_{HH}$ be the expected number of remaining flips from each.

From state $HH$: flip T (probability $1/2$) and you are done; flip H and your suffix is still HH.

$$
E_{HH} = 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac12 E_{HH} \;\Rightarrow\; E_{HH} = 2
$$

From state $H$: flip H and you advance to $HH$; flip T and your suffix is "HT", which contains no prefix of HHT, so you fall all the way back to state $0$.

$$
E_H = 1 + \tfrac12 E_{HH} + \tfrac12 E_0 = 2 + \tfrac12 E_0
$$

From state $0$: flip H and you advance, flip T and you stay.

$$
E_0 = 1 + \tfrac12 E_H + \tfrac12 E_0
$$

Substitute: $E_0 = 1 + \tfrac12(2 + \tfrac12 E_0) + \tfrac12 E_0 = 2 + \tfrac34 E_0$, so $\tfrac14 E_0 = 2$ and

$$
E_0 = 8
$$

### Why HTH is 10 and not 8

Run the same machine on HTH. The only line that changes is the fallback. From state $HT$, a heads finishes you; a tails gives suffix "TT" and you drop to state $0$. But from state $H$, a heads keeps you in state $H$ rather than sending you back, because that new H is itself a fresh first character.

$$
E_{HT} = 1 + \tfrac12 E_0, \qquad E_H = 1 + \tfrac12 E_H + \tfrac12 E_{HT}, \qquad E_0 = 1 + \tfrac12 E_H + \tfrac12 E_0
$$

The third gives $E_0 = 2 + E_H$. Substituting into the first, $E_{HT} = 2 + \tfrac12 E_H$. Putting that into the second, $\tfrac12 E_H = 1 + \tfrac12(2 + \tfrac12 E_H)$, so $E_H = 8$ and $E_0 = 10$.

Both patterns have probability $1/8$ per window, so a naive answer says both take 8. The difference is **self-overlap**. HTH overlaps itself: a failed attempt ending in H is already a partial success, so successes cluster, and clustered successes mean longer average gaps. HHT has no such overlap. If you want the shortcut, Conway's leading-number rule says the expected wait is $\sum_k 2^k$ over every $k$ where the last $k$ characters equal the first $k$. HHT: only $k=3$, giving 8. HTH: $k=3$ and $k=1$, giving $8+2=10$. HHH: $k=1,2,3$, giving $2+4+8=14$.

Generalizing the last one, waiting for $n$ heads in a row gives $E_n = 2^{n+1}-2$ from the chain $E_k = 1 + \tfrac12 E_{k+1} + \tfrac12 E_0$.

## Part 2 — Linearity of expectation via indicators

An **indicator variable** $I_A$ is 1 when event $A$ happens and 0 otherwise, so $E[I_A] = P(A)$. The technique: write the quantity you want as a sum of indicators, then add up probabilities.

Linearity holds with no independence assumption at all. The proof is one line and worth internalizing, because the interviewer will push on it:

$$
E[X+Y] = \sum_\omega P(\omega)\big(X(\omega)+Y(\omega)\big) = \sum_\omega P(\omega)X(\omega) + \sum_\omega P(\omega)Y(\omega) = E[X]+E[Y]
$$

Nothing about the joint distribution was used. Dependence changes $\text{Var}(X+Y)$, which picks up a covariance term, and it changes $E[XY]$. It never touches $E[X+Y]$. That single fact is what makes "expected number of X" problems easy even when the X's are tangled together.

### Coupon collector

Roll a fair die until you have seen all six faces. Expected rolls? Split the wait into stages. While you hold $k$ distinct faces, each roll is new with probability $(6-k)/6$, so the wait for the next new face is geometric with mean $6/(6-k)$.

$$
E = \sum_{k=0}^{5} \frac{6}{6-k} = 6\left(1+\tfrac12+\tfrac13+\tfrac14+\tfrac15+\tfrac16\right) = 6 \cdot \frac{49}{20} = 14.7
$$

In general $n H_n \approx n(\ln n + 0.577)$. For $n = 6$ that estimate gives $6(1.792+0.577) = 14.2$, close enough to say out loud under time pressure.

### The version where dependence would have killed you

Roll a die 10 times. Expected number of **distinct** faces seen? Let $I_j$ indicate that face $j$ appeared at least once. These indicators are strongly dependent, since seeing lots of 3s makes the other faces less likely to appear. Linearity does not care.

$$
E = \sum_{j=1}^{6} P(\text{face } j \text{ appears}) = 6\left(1 - (5/6)^{10}\right) = 6(1 - 0.1615) = 5.03
$$

Same move for the number of fixed points in a random shuffle of $n$ cards: $I_j$ indicates card $j$ landing in slot $j$, $P(I_j=1)=1/n$, so the expectation is $n \cdot 1/n = 1$ regardless of $n$. And for the number of adjacent HH pairs in $n$ flips: $n-1$ overlapping, dependent indicators, each with probability $1/4$, giving $(n-1)/4$.

## Part 3 — Bayes with base rates

Write Bayes in **odds form**, because it removes the arithmetic that people fumble:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{P(H \mid E)}{P(\bar H \mid E)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{P(H)}{P(\bar H)}}_{\text{prior odds}} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid \bar H)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}}
$$

### The rare disease

Prevalence 1 in 10,000. The test is 99% accurate in both directions. You test positive. Probability you are sick?

Use natural frequencies on a population of 1,000,000. Sick: 100 people, of whom 99 test positive. Healthy: 999,900 people, of whom 1% or 9,999 test positive. So

$$
P(\text{sick} \mid +) = \frac{99}{99 + 9999} = \frac{99}{10098} \approx 0.0098
$$

About 1%. The false positives from the enormous healthy group swamp the true positives. In odds form: prior odds $1:9999$, likelihood ratio $99:1$, posterior odds $99:9999$. Same answer, less scratch paper.

### The biased coin from a jar

Reported at Millennium and, in variants, nearly everywhere. A jar holds 1000 coins: 999 fair and 1 double-headed. You pick one at random and flip it 10 times, getting 10 heads. What is the probability you hold the double-headed coin?

Prior odds are $1:999$. The likelihood ratio is $1 \big/ (1/2)^{10} = 1024$. Posterior odds are $1024 : 999$, so

$$
P(\text{double-headed} \mid 10\text{H}) = \frac{1024}{1024+999} = \frac{1024}{2023} \approx 0.506
$$

Just over half. The general form after $n$ heads is $2^n/(2^n+999)$, which crosses 50% at $n=10$, exactly where $2^n$ passes 999.

The follow-up that catches people: **what is the probability the next flip is heads?** Average over the posterior, do not commit to one hypothesis.

$$
P(\text{next} = H) = 0.506 \cdot 1 + 0.494 \cdot \tfrac12 = 0.753
$$

One more variant to be ready for. If the reported evidence is "9 heads in 10 flips" rather than 10 in a row, the double-headed coin is **impossible**, since it cannot produce a tail. The likelihood ratio is $0$ and the posterior is 0. Interviewers use that to check whether you are reciting a pattern or actually computing a likelihood.

## Part 4 — Difference equations for gambler's ruin

You have $i$ dollars, your opponent has $N-i$. Each round you win a dollar with probability $p$ and lose one with probability $q=1-p$. Play until someone is broke.

### Absorption probability

Let $P_i$ be the probability you reach $N$ starting from $i$. Condition on the first round:

$$
P_i = p\,P_{i+1} + q\,P_{i-1}, \qquad P_0 = 0,\; P_N = 1
$$

Rewrite it in terms of the gaps $d_i = P_i - P_{i-1}$. Since $p+q=1$, the recurrence becomes $p(P_{i+1}-P_i) = q(P_i - P_{i-1})$, that is $d_{i+1} = (q/p)\,d_i$. The gaps are geometric.

For a **fair** game, $q/p = 1$, so all gaps are equal and $P_i$ is linear:

$$
P_i = \frac{i}{N}
$$

For a **biased** game, summing the geometric gaps and normalizing so $P_N=1$:

$$
P_i = \frac{1-(q/p)^i}{1-(q/p)^N}
$$

Worked numbers. You bring 20 dollars to a table where your opponent has 80, betting one dollar a hand. Fair game: $P = 20/100 = 20\%$. Now make it $p=0.49$, a 1% house edge. Then $q/p = 0.51/0.49 = 1.0408$, and $(1.0408)^{20} = 2.22$ while $(1.0408)^{100} = 54.5$, so

$$
P = \frac{1-2.22}{1-54.5} = \frac{-1.22}{-53.5} \approx 0.023
$$

A 1% edge against you turns a 20% chance into a 2.3% chance. That gap is the whole business model of a casino, and it is the reason position sizing matters more than edge when your bankroll is finite.

### Expected duration

Let $T_i$ be the expected number of rounds until absorption, with $T_0 = T_N = 0$. First-step analysis again:

$$
T_i = 1 + p\,T_{i+1} + q\,T_{i-1}
$$

Take $p=q=1/2$ and rearrange: $\tfrac12(T_{i+1} - 2T_i + T_{i-1}) = -1$, so the second difference of $T$ is $-2$. A sequence with constant second difference $-2$ is $-i^2$ plus any linear term, so $T_i = -i^2 + a + bi$. The boundary $T_0=0$ kills $a$, and $T_N=0$ forces $bN = N^2$, so $b=N$:

$$
T_i = i(N-i)
$$

With 20 dollars against 80 that is $20 \times 80 = 1600$ rounds on average. Note how large that is relative to the stakes, and note that the same 20% win probability is compatible with an enormous expected playing time.

### The martingale shortcut

A **martingale** is a process whose expected next value equals its current value. In a fair game your fortune $S_n$ is one. The optional stopping theorem says that under mild conditions $E[S_\tau] = S_0$ at the stopping time $\tau$. Since the walk stops at 0 or $N$,

$$
i = E[S_\tau] = N \cdot P_i + 0 \cdot (1-P_i) \;\Rightarrow\; P_i = \frac{i}{N}
$$

For the duration, use the second martingale $S_n^2 - n$, whose expectation is also constant. At $\tau$:

$$
E[S_\tau^2] - E[\tau] = i^2 \;\Rightarrow\; E[\tau] = N^2 \cdot \frac{i}{N} - i^2 = i(N-i)
$$

Two lines instead of two derivations. Say the difference-equation version first so the interviewer sees you can do it from scratch, then offer the martingale as the fast check.

### Ant on a cube is the same problem

An ant starts at one vertex of a cube and walks to a uniformly random neighbor each step. Expected steps to reach the opposite vertex? Eight vertices looks like eight states, but by symmetry only the graph distance $d \in \lbrace 0,1,2,3 \rbrace$ from the start matters. From distance 1 you go back to 0 with probability $1/3$ and out to 2 with probability $2/3$; from distance 2 you go back to 1 with probability $2/3$ and finish with probability $1/3$.

$$
T_0 = 1 + T_1, \qquad T_1 = 1 + \tfrac13 T_0 + \tfrac23 T_2, \qquad T_2 = 1 + \tfrac23 T_1
$$

Substituting, $T_1 = 1 + \tfrac13(1+T_1) + \tfrac23(1 + \tfrac23 T_1) = 2 + \tfrac79 T_1$, so $\tfrac29 T_1 = 2$, giving $T_1 = 9$ and $T_0 = 10$. It is a biased gambler's ruin on a 4-state line. Collapsing by symmetry before writing equations is what makes these tractable in 90 seconds.

## Tradeoff matrix

| Tool | Use it when | Cost | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| First-step / state equations | "Expected time until pattern or event", small memory | Solving a linear system by hand | State space is large or not finite |
| Indicators + linearity | "Expected number of X" | Must find the right indicator decomposition | You need a variance, a probability, or a max |
| Bayes with base rate | Evidence updates a hypothesis with a known prior | Easy to invert the conditional | Prior is genuinely unknown, or evidence is not independent given the hypothesis |
| Difference equations | Walks with absorbing boundaries | Boundary conditions, algebra | Steps are not identically distributed across states |
| Martingale + optional stopping | Fair or drift-corrected walks, fast answers | Must name the right martingale | Unbounded stopping times without integrability |

## Production failure modes

1. **Wrong state definition.** People index HHT states by "number of heads so far" instead of "length of matched suffix". That state is not Markov, the equations are inconsistent, and you get a wrong number with a confident derivation attached.
2. **Forgetting the partial-match fallback.** After a failure you rarely return to state 0. Getting the HTH fallback wrong is the single most common source of the answer 8 instead of 10.
3. **Assuming independence to justify linearity.** Candidates who believe linearity needs independence refuse to use indicators exactly when indicators are most valuable, then attempt an inclusion-exclusion sum and run out of time.
4. **Inverting the conditional.** Reporting $P(+ \mid \text{sick}) = 0.99$ as though it were $P(\text{sick} \mid +)$. This is the prosecutor's fallacy, and it shows up in real ML systems as fraud or abuse alerts that are 99% accurate and 99% wrong.
5. **Ignoring the base rate in a deployed classifier.** A model validated on a balanced set and shipped into a 1-in-10,000 population has a precision collapse that no threshold tuning was asked to anticipate.
6. **Confusing absorption probability with expected duration.** Two different equations with two different boundary conditions. $P$ has no $+1$ term; $T$ does, and $T$ is 0 at both ends while $P$ is 0 at one and 1 at the other.
7. **Applying optional stopping without checking conditions.** In an unfair game with an infinite bankroll the naive martingale argument produces nonsense, which is exactly the doubling-strategy trap.

## Interview gotchas

- **"Expected number of flips to see HHT?"** 8. Set up $E_0$, $E_H$, $E_{HH}$ and solve. Volunteer that HTH is 10 before they ask, and explain the overlap. That contrast is the actual thing being tested.
- **"Does linearity of expectation require independence?"** No. It follows from the definition of expectation as a sum over outcomes. Independence is needed for $E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]$ and for variances to add, not for expectations.
- **"A 99% accurate test for a 1-in-10,000 disease comes back positive. Are you sick?"** Almost certainly not, about 1%. Say the natural-frequency version out loud: 100 sick people in a million versus 9,999 false positives.
- **"999 fair coins and 1 double-headed. You flip 10 heads. Which coin do you have?"** Posterior 1024/2023, about 50.6%. If they change it to 9 heads out of 10, the answer is 0.
- **"What is the chance the next flip is heads?"** Average over the posterior: $0.506 + 0.494 \times 0.5 = 0.753$. Do not answer 1 or 0.5.
- **"You have 20 dollars, opponent has 80, fair coin, one-dollar bets. Win probability and expected duration?"** 20% and 1600 rounds. If they add a small edge against you, the win probability collapses; be ready to write $(1-(q/p)^i)/(1-(q/p)^N)$.
- **"Ant on a cube, steps to the opposite corner?"** 10. Collapse to distance states first and say why symmetry lets you.
- **"Can you get gambler's ruin without solving the recurrence?"** Yes. Optional stopping on $S_n$ gives $i/N$; on $S_n^2-n$ it gives $i(N-i)$. Offer this as a check, not as your only derivation.
