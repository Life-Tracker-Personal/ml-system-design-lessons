## TL;DR

Five distributions carry almost all of quantitative interviewing: binomial, Poisson, geometric, exponential, normal. They are not five unrelated facts. They are four views of one story (independent trials at a constant rate) plus the limit that everything collapses to. If you can derive the mean and variance of each in ten seconds and say what physical process generates it, you have the prerequisite for every harder question in this chapter.

On top of that base sit three things interviewers actually test. Memorylessness, which explains the inspection paradox and why you wait the full mean for the bus. Order statistics of uniforms, which the CDF-of-max trick solves in one line. And decision-making under a known distribution: backward induction for optimal stopping, Kelly for bet sizing, and fast mental estimates of mean and standard deviation for market-making questions where you get ninety seconds.

## What you'll be able to do after this lesson

- State mean, variance and generating story for binomial, Poisson, geometric, exponential and normal without looking anything up.
- Prove that geometric and exponential are the only memoryless distributions, and use memorylessness to skip algebra.
- Derive the Poisson limit of the binomial and say when the approximation is safe.
- Derive the inspection paradox properly, including why the answer is the full mean and not half of it.
- Get any order statistic of uniforms from the CDF-of-max trick and recognise the Beta distribution when it appears.
- Solve the red/black card stopping game by backward induction and get $2/3$ for the 2-2 deck.
- Derive Kelly's $f^\star = 2p - 1$ from maximising $E[\log W]$, and explain concretely why full Kelly is dangerous.
- Quote a market on a dice or coin sum in under a minute with a defensible width.

## Part 1 — Five distributions, one story

Start with a single biased coin: probability $p$ of success, trials independent.

**Binomial.** Count successes in $n$ fixed trials. $P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$. Write $X = \sum_{i=1}^n I_i$ with $I_i$ the indicator of success on trial $i$. Linearity gives $E[X] = np$ immediately, and independence lets variance add: $\mathrm{Var}(I_i) = p(1-p)$, so $\mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p)$. The indicator decomposition is the whole trick and it generalises to problems where the trials are *not* independent (the mean still works, the variance does not).

**Geometric.** Count trials until the first success. $P(X = k) = (1-p)^{k-1}p$. Do not sum the series. Condition on the first trial:

$$E[X] = 1 + (1-p)E[X] \implies E[X] = \frac{1}{p}$$

The same one-step conditioning gives $E[X^2] = 1 + (1-p)\,E[(X+1)^2]$, which unwinds to $\mathrm{Var}(X) = (1-p)/p^2$.

**Poisson.** Count events in a fixed window when they arrive at a constant rate $\lambda$ with no clustering. $P(X=k) = e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$, and $E[X] = \mathrm{Var}(X) = \lambda$. Mean equals variance is the diagnostic: real count data with variance well above the mean is *overdispersed* and is not Poisson.

**Exponential.** The continuous waiting time between those events. $f(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}$, $P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}$, $E[T] = 1/\lambda$, $\mathrm{Var}(T) = 1/\lambda^2$. Standard deviation equals the mean, which is why exponential waiting times feel so erratic.

**Normal.** What sums turn into. If $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, then $\sum X_i$ is approximately $\mathcal{N}(n\mu, n\sigma^2)$. Keep 68 / 95 / 99.7 percent within one, two and three standard deviations loaded in memory; every fast estimation question uses it.

## Part 2 — Memorylessness, and why only two distributions have it

A distribution is memoryless if having waited already tells you nothing:

$$P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = P(T > t)$$

Let $G(t) = P(T > t)$. The condition says $G(s+t) = G(s)G(t)$ for all $s, t \ge 0$. That is the Cauchy functional equation in multiplicative form. For a right-continuous $G$ with $G(0)=1$, the only solutions are $G(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$, which is exactly the exponential. Sketch: $G(1) = G(1/n)^n$ forces $G(1/n) = G(1)^{1/n}$, so $G(q) = G(1)^q$ on the rationals, and continuity extends it to the reals. Write $G(1) = e^{-\lambda}$ and you are done.

Run the identical argument on the integers and you get $G(n) = G(1)^n$, which is the geometric distribution. So geometric is the unique memoryless discrete distribution and exponential the unique memoryless continuous one. They are the same statement at two resolutions.

Two consequences worth memorising. First, the minimum of independent exponentials with rates $\lambda_i$ is exponential with rate $\sum \lambda_i$, since $P(\min > t) = \prod e^{-\lambda_i t}$. Second, the probability that machine $i$ fires first is $\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j$, and that fact is independent of when the first event happens. Competing-risk questions collapse to those two lines.

## Part 3 — Poisson: the limit, the process and the inspection paradox

### Binomial to Poisson

Chop a window into $n$ tiny slots, each with success probability $p = \lambda/n$. Then

$$P(X=k) = \binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^k\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n-k} = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k}\cdot\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n-k}$$

As $n \to \infty$ the first factor goes to 1, the last goes to $e^{-\lambda}$, and you land on $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$. The practical rule: the approximation is good when $n$ is large and $np$ is moderate, say $n \ge 100$ and $np \le 10$. Note the variance check. Binomial variance is $np(1-p) \to np$ when $p$ is tiny, matching Poisson's $\mathrm{Var} = \lambda$.

A worked example. 400 servers, each fails independently on a given day with probability $0.005$. Then $\lambda = 2$, and $P(\text{no failures}) = e^{-2} \approx 0.135$, $P(\text{at least 3}) = 1 - e^{-2}(1 + 2 + 2) \approx 1 - 0.677 = 0.323$.

### The inspection paradox

Buses arrive as a Poisson process with rate $\lambda = 1/10$ per minute, so gaps between buses are exponential with mean 10 minutes. You show up at a random time. How long do you wait?

Memorylessness answers it in one line: the time until the next arrival has the same exponential distribution regardless of how long ago the last bus left, so you wait 10 minutes on average, not 5.

The reason people expect 5 is worth spelling out, because the fix generalises to non-exponential gaps. You do not land in a uniformly chosen gap. You land in a gap chosen in proportion to its length, since long gaps cover more of the timeline. That is *length-biased sampling*: if gap lengths have density $f$, the gap you land in has density $\ell f(\ell)/E[L]$, so

$$E[\text{gap you land in}] = \frac{E[L^2]}{E[L]} = \frac{E[L]^2 + \mathrm{Var}(L)}{E[L]} = E[L] + \frac{\mathrm{Var}(L)}{E[L]}$$

Given the gap, your arrival is uniform inside it, so you wait half of it. For exponential gaps, $E[L^2] = 2/\lambda^2$, so the sampled gap averages $2/\lambda = 20$ minutes and you wait half of that, which is 10. The two halves cancel exactly. Deterministic 10-minute buses have $\mathrm{Var}(L)=0$ and you really do wait 5. The excess wait is entirely driven by the variance of the gaps.

## Part 4 — Order statistics of uniforms

Let $U_1, \dots, U_n$ be independent Uniform$(0,1)$. The maximum is easy because "max $\le x$" means "all of them $\le x$":

$$P(U_{(n)} \le x) = x^n \implies f(x) = n x^{n-1} \implies E[U_{(n)}] = \int_0^1 n x^n\,dx = \frac{n}{n+1}$$

For $n=2$ that is $E[\max] = 2/3$, and by symmetry $E[\min] = 1/3$. Sanity check: they sum to 1, as they must, since $\max + \min = U_1 + U_2$.

The general $k$-th order statistic follows from a counting argument. To have $U_{(k)}$ in $[x, x+dx]$, pick which point lands there ($n$ ways), which $k-1$ of the rest fall below ($\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ ways), and the rest fall above:

$$f_{U_{(k)}}(x) = \frac{n!}{(k-1)!\,(n-k)!}\,x^{k-1}(1-x)^{n-k}$$

That is exactly a Beta$(k,\, n-k+1)$ density, and its mean is $k/(n+1)$. There is a picture that makes the answer obvious without any integration: $n$ uniform points cut $[0,1]$ into $n+1$ gaps, those gaps are exchangeable, so each has mean $1/(n+1)$, and the $k$-th point sits after $k$ of them.

Two classics that fall out. The expected number of Uniform$(0,1)$ draws until the running sum exceeds 1 is $e$, because $P(\text{sum of } n \text{ draws} \le 1) = 1/n!$ (that is the volume of a simplex) and $E[N] = \sum_{n\ge 0} P(N > n) = \sum 1/n! = e$. And for the broken-stick problem, two uniform cuts on a stick form a triangle with probability $1/4$, since a triangle fails exactly when some piece exceeds $1/2$ and the three failure events are disjoint with probability $1/4$ each.

## Part 5 — Optimal stopping by backward induction

The staple: a shuffled deck of 2 red and 2 black cards, drawn one at a time. You win \$1 per red drawn and lose \$1 per black. You may stop whenever you like and keep what you have. What is the EV under optimal play?

Define $V(r,b)$ as the expected *additional* gain with $r$ red and $b$ black still in the deck, playing optimally. Stopping is worth 0, so

$$V(r,b) = \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{r}{r+b}\big(1 + V(r-1,b)\big) + \frac{b}{r+b}\big(-1 + V(r,b-1)\big)\right)$$

Boundaries: $V(0,b) = 0$ (nothing left but losses, so stop) and $V(r,0) = r$ (take all the reds). Now grind upward.

- $V(1,1) = \max(0,\ \tfrac12(1+0) + \tfrac12(-1+1)) = \tfrac12$
- $V(2,1) = \max(0,\ \tfrac23(1+\tfrac12) + \tfrac13(-1+2)) = 1 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac43$
- $V(1,2) = \max(0,\ \tfrac13(1+0) + \tfrac23(-1+\tfrac12)) = \max(0, \tfrac13 - \tfrac13) = 0$
- $V(2,2) = \max(0,\ \tfrac12(1 + 0) + \tfrac12(-1 + \tfrac43)) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac23$

The answer is $2/3$. Two things to say out loud in the room. The value is strictly positive even though the full deck has zero mean, because the option to stop is worth something and options are never worth less than nothing. And the optimal policy is not "stop when ahead": at $(1,2)$ you are indifferent, and at $(2,1)$ you continue despite being behind, because the remaining deck is red-rich.

## Part 6 — Sizing and quoting

### Kelly

You have edge $p > 1/2$ on an even-money bet and you can repeat it. Betting a fixed fraction $f$ of wealth, after $n$ bets wealth is a product, so take logs and maximise the growth rate per bet:

$$g(f) = p\log(1+f) + (1-p)\log(1-f)$$

$$g'(f) = \frac{p}{1+f} - \frac{1-p}{1-f} = 0 \implies p(1-f) = (1-p)(1+f) \implies f^\star = 2p - 1$$

So with $p = 0.6$ you bet 20 percent of your bankroll. Maximising $E[\log W]$ rather than $E[W]$ is the point worth defending: $E[W]$ is maximised by betting everything, which drives you to ruin with probability 1, while log wealth is the thing that actually compounds.

Why full Kelly is dangerous in practice, in three concrete ways. Growth is flat at the top and cliff-like past it, so overbetting costs far more than underbetting; in the Gaussian approximation $g(f) \approx f\mu - f^2\sigma^2/2$, and $g(2f^\star) = 0$ exactly. Full Kelly also has brutal drawdowns: the probability of your bankroll ever falling to a fraction $a$ of its starting value is $a$, so a 50 percent drawdown at some point is a coin flip. And $p$ is estimated, not known, so overestimating your edge by 20 percent silently moves you past optimal. Half Kelly gets you $3/4$ of the maximum growth rate with half the volatility, which is why practitioners use it.

### Fast market-making

The format is: "make a market on the sum of three dice," ninety seconds, and the interviewer trades against your quote. The mechanics are just mean and variance adding over independent draws.

One die: $E = 3.5$, $\mathrm{Var} = \frac{1}{6}\sum (k-3.5)^2 = 35/12 \approx 2.92$. Three dice: $E = 10.5$, $\mathrm{Var} = 8.75$, $\sigma \approx 2.96$. A market of $9.5$ at $11.5$ is defensible: centred on the true mean, about two-thirds of a standard deviation wide.

For coins, the number of heads in 100 flips has mean 50 and $\sigma = \sqrt{100 \cdot 0.25} = 5$, so quote 48 at 52. Width is a real decision, not a formality. Quote too tight and an informed counterparty picks you off; quote too wide and you never trade, which in an interview reads as no appetite for risk. Say your reasoning aloud: centre on EV, scale width to $\sigma$, then widen further if the quantity is one the interviewer might already know.

## Tradeoff matrix

| Distribution | Generating story | Mean | Variance | Memoryless | Interview tell |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Binomial$(n,p)$ | successes in $n$ fixed trials | $np$ | $np(1-p)$ | no | "how many out of $n$" |
| Geometric$(p)$ | trials until first success | $1/p$ | $(1-p)/p^2$ | yes | "how long until" |
| Poisson$(\lambda)$ | events in a window at constant rate | $\lambda$ | $\lambda$ | n/a | rare events, large $n$, small $p$ |
| Exponential$(\lambda)$ | gap between Poisson events | $1/\lambda$ | $1/\lambda^2$ | yes | continuous waiting time |
| Normal | sums of many independents | $\mu$ | $\sigma^2$ | no | anything you must estimate fast |
| Uniform order stat $U_{(k)}$ | $k$-th of $n$ uniforms | $k/(n+1)$ | $\frac{k(n-k+1)}{(n+1)^2(n+2)}$ | no | max, min, ranks, Beta |

## Production failure modes

1. **Assuming Poisson arrivals for bursty traffic.** Web requests cluster (retries, cron jobs, cache expiry storms), so the variance far exceeds the mean. Capacity planned on a Poisson tail underestimates the real p99 badly. Check the variance-to-mean ratio before trusting the model.
2. **Reporting the mean of a length-biased sample.** Sessions sampled by "who is online right now" oversample long sessions exactly like the bus paradox. The correct estimate needs the $E[L^2]/E[L]$ correction, or a sampling scheme keyed to session starts instead.
3. **Exponential survival models on non-constant hazard.** Exponential time-to-event assumes a constant hazard rate. Customer churn and hardware failure both have hazard that varies with age, and forcing exponential produces confident, wrong retention forecasts.
4. **Normal approximation on rare events.** A conversion test at $p = 0.001$ with $n = 5000$ has $np = 5$, so the binomial is skewed and the normal confidence interval can dip below zero. Use the Poisson or an exact interval.
5. **Full-Kelly sizing on an estimated edge.** Any allocation system that solves for the growth-optimal fraction from historical win rates is overfitting the edge, and the resulting drawdowns look like a bug in the strategy rather than in the sizing.
6. **Independence assumed when computing variance.** Means always add. Variances only add when the terms are uncorrelated. Correlated failures across "independent" replicas in the same rack are the classic version of this.

## Interview gotchas

- **"Buses come every 10 minutes on average. How long do you wait?"** Ten minutes, if arrivals are Poisson. You wait half of the gap you land in, but you land in a length-biased gap that averages 20 minutes. Only deterministic schedules give you 5.
- **"Which distributions are memoryless?"** Geometric and exponential, and they are the only ones. The proof is $G(s+t)=G(s)G(t)$ forcing $G(t)=e^{-\lambda t}$.
- **"When can I use Poisson instead of binomial?"** Large $n$, small $p$, moderate $np$. Rough guide $n \ge 100$, $np \le 10$. The tell that it is failing is $p$ not being small, since then $np(1-p)$ is visibly less than $np$.
- **"$E[\max(X,Y)]$ for two uniforms on $[0,1]$?"** $2/3$. Say $P(\max \le x) = x^2$ and differentiate, do not integrate a joint density.
- **"Two red, two black, stop when you like. EV?"** $2/3$ by backward induction. Show the recursion and the $\max(0, \cdot)$, and note the optimal policy is not "stop when ahead."
- **"Why maximise $E[\log W]$ instead of $E[W]$?"** Because wealth compounds multiplicatively. Maximising $E[W]$ tells you to bet everything, which has growth rate $-\infty$ almost surely.
- **"Make a market on the sum of three dice."** $10.5$ centre, $\sigma \approx 2.96$, quote 9.5 at 11.5. State mean and variance out loud so the interviewer sees the method.
- **"Expected draws from $U(0,1)$ until the sum exceeds 1?"** $e$. Use $P(N > n) = 1/n!$ and sum the tail.
