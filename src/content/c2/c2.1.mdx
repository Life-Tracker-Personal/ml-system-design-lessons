## TL;DR

A neural network is an alternating stack of two things: affine maps (a matrix multiply plus a bias) and elementwise nonlinearities. That is the whole computation. Backpropagation is not a separate algorithm bolted on top; it is the chain rule applied to that stack, evaluated right to left so that each intermediate result is computed once and reused. Reverse-mode automatic differentiation ("autodiff") is the general version of the same trick, and it costs roughly one extra forward pass to get gradients for every parameter at once.

The organizing idea for this whole chapter: **the gradient reaching layer $\ell$ is a product of the Jacobians of every layer above it, so training health is a question about what that product does to magnitude.** Every factor in the product either shrinks the signal, preserves it, or grows it. Sigmoid contributes a factor of at most $0.25$, which is why deep sigmoid networks stop learning. ReLU contributes exactly $1$ or exactly $0$. Initialization sets the starting scale of the weight factors, normalization resets it each layer, and residual connections add an identity term so the product can never collapse to zero. Learn the product, and the rest of the chapter is corollaries.

## What you'll be able to do after this lesson

- Write the forward pass of an MLP with correct shapes, and explain in one sentence why two consecutive linear layers are pointless.
- Derive backpropagation for a 2-layer ReLU network by hand, with real numbers, and verify one entry against a finite difference.
- Write the vectorized NumPy backward pass for a 2-layer net with softmax and cross-entropy, getting every shape right on the first try.
- Explain what a framework builds during the forward pass, what `.backward()` traverses, and why an in-place operation can break it.
- Quantify vanishing and exploding gradients as a product of per-layer factors, not as a slogan.
- Say precisely why ReLU displaced sigmoid and tanh, what a dead ReLU is, and how you would detect one.
- Give a concrete construction showing why depth buys expressiveness that width cannot cheaply match.

## Part 1 — What a network actually computes

**Goal: strip the mystique and get to the two operations.** A feedforward network with one hidden layer, written for a single input row $x \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d}$:

$$
z_1 = x W_1 + b_1, \qquad a_1 = \phi(z_1), \qquad z_2 = a_1 W_2 + b_2
$$

with $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times h}$, $b_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times h}$, $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times k}$, $b_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times k}$. Batches stack rows: $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, and the bias broadcasts. Keep this row-major convention in your head for the rest of the lesson, because half of all interview shape errors come from silently switching between $Wx$ and $xW$.

The nonlinearity $\phi$ is the only thing standing between this and linear regression. Drop it and $z_2 = x W_1 W_2 + \text{const}$, and $W_1 W_2$ is a single $d \times k$ matrix with rank at most $\min(d, h, k)$. So a stack of linear layers is one linear layer with a rank cap and more parameters to optimize. That is the answer to "why shouldn't we have two consecutive linear layers?" — you have bought a bottleneck, not a model. (The one time it is deliberate: low-rank adapters like LoRA use exactly this factorization *because* they want the rank cap.)

> **Stop and think.** If nonlinearity is what buys expressiveness, why not use a nonlinearity that is as far from linear as possible, like $\sin$? Hint: think about what the derivative of your choice does to the gradient product in Part 4, not about what the forward function can represent.

## Part 2 — Backprop by hand, with numbers

**Goal: do one full backward pass with real arithmetic so the shapes and the chain rule stop being abstract.** Take $d = 2$, $h = 2$, $k = 1$, ReLU hidden activation, linear output, squared-error loss on one example.

$$
x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}, \quad
W_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -0.6 \\ -0.3 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
b_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
W_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1.0 \\ -2.0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b_2 = 0.05
$$

Target $y = 1$, loss $L = \tfrac12(\hat{y} - y)^2$.

**Forward.** Column $j$ of $W_1$ holds the incoming weights of hidden unit $j$.

$$
z_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1(0.5) + 2(-0.3) + 0.2 & \; 1(-0.6) + 2(0.2) + 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 & -0.2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
a_1 = \mathrm{ReLU}(z_1) = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\hat{y} = z_2 = 0.1(1.0) + 0(-2.0) + 0.05 = 0.15
$$

$$
L = \tfrac12(0.15 - 1)^2 = \tfrac12(0.7225) = 0.36125
$$

**Backward.** Define $\delta_2 = \partial L / \partial z_2$ and $\delta_1 = \partial L / \partial z_1$. Work strictly right to left; each step is one application of the chain rule.

1. $\delta_2 = \hat{y} - y = -0.85$. (Shape $1 \times 1$.)
2. $\partial L / \partial W_2 = a_1^{\top} \delta_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}(-0.85) = \begin{bmatrix} -0.085 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Shape $h \times k$, matching $W_2$. This is the matmul rule you must know cold: **for $z = aW$, the weight gradient is $a^{\top} \delta$ and the input gradient is $\delta W^{\top}$.**
3. $\partial L / \partial b_2 = \delta_2 = -0.85$.
4. $\partial L / \partial a_1 = \delta_2 W_2^{\top} = -0.85\begin{bmatrix} 1.0 & -2.0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.85 & 1.70 \end{bmatrix}$.
5. ReLU's local derivative is a 0/1 mask, so $\delta_1 = \partial L/\partial a_1 \odot \mathbb{1}[z_1 > 0] = \begin{bmatrix} -0.85 & 1.70\end{bmatrix} \odot \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.85 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
6. $\partial L / \partial W_1 = x^{\top} \delta_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -0.85 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.85 & 0 \\ -1.70 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, and $\partial L / \partial b_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -0.85 & 0\end{bmatrix}$.

Three things to notice, all of them interview material. First, **unit 2 receives exactly zero gradient** on this example: its pre-activation was negative, the mask zeroed it, and no update reaches its incoming weights. That is the dead-ReLU mechanism in miniature. Second, $\partial L/\partial W_1$ is proportional to $x$, so a feature on a scale of $10^3$ produces a gradient $10^3$ times larger than a feature on a scale of $1$ — that is the mechanical reason input normalization matters. Third, every quantity you needed on the way back ($x$, the mask, $a_1$) was produced on the way forward. Backprop's memory cost is exactly that: the stored activations.

**Verify it.** Bump $W_1[0,0]$ from $0.5$ to $0.501$. Then $z_1 = [0.101, -0.2]$, $\hat{y} = 0.151$, and $L = \tfrac12(0.849)^2 = 0.3604005$. The finite difference is $(0.3604005 - 0.36125)/0.001 = -0.8495$, against the analytic $-0.85$. That agreement, to the expected first-order accuracy, is the gradient check you should run on any backward pass you hand-write in an interview.

## Part 3 — The vectorized version, with softmax and cross-entropy

**Goal: the artifact an ML-coding round actually asks for.** Swap the scalar output for $k$ classes with softmax and cross-entropy. The reason interviewers like this pairing is the punchline: the two functions' Jacobians collapse and the gradient at the logits is just $p - y$, with $y$ one-hot. No sigmoid derivative, no saturation term.

```python
def forward_backward(X, Y, W1, b1, W2, b2):
    # X: (N, d)   Y: (N, k) one-hot
    Z1 = X @ W1 + b1                 # (N, h)
    A1 = np.maximum(Z1, 0)           # (N, h)
    Z2 = A1 @ W2 + b2                # (N, k)

    Z2 = Z2 - Z2.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)      # log-sum-exp stability
    P  = np.exp(Z2); P /= P.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    loss = -np.sum(Y * np.log(P + 1e-12)) / len(X)

    dZ2 = (P - Y) / len(X)           # (N, k)  <- the whole trick
    dW2 = A1.T @ dZ2                 # (h, k)
    db2 = dZ2.sum(axis=0)            # (k,)
    dA1 = dZ2 @ W2.T                 # (N, h)
    dZ1 = dA1 * (Z1 > 0)             # (N, h)  ReLU mask
    dW1 = X.T @ dZ1                  # (d, h)
    db1 = dZ1.sum(axis=0)            # (h,)
    return loss, dW1, db1, dW2, db2
```

Two shape rules cover every line: a weight gradient is `inputs.T @ delta`, and a bias gradient is `delta.sum(axis=0)` because the bias was broadcast across the batch, so its gradient is the sum over the batch. Divide by $N$ exactly once (here, folded into `dZ2`) or your effective learning rate silently scales with batch size. Subtracting the row max before `exp` is not cosmetic: logits of $+1000$ overflow to `inf` and the loss becomes `NaN`.

## Part 4 — The computational graph and what `.backward()` does

**Goal: answer "how does autograd work" at mechanism level, which the 2026 loop reports flag as rarely understood.** During the forward pass the framework does not just compute numbers. Each operation on a tensor that requires grad appends a node to a directed acyclic graph, and that node stores whatever inputs its local derivative will need. Multiplication stores both operands. ReLU stores the sign mask. A matmul stores both matrices. The graph is built dynamically, per forward pass, and thrown away after the backward.

`.backward()` starts from a scalar with an implicit seed gradient of $1$, walks the graph in reverse topological order, and at each node applies a **vector-Jacobian product**: it takes the incoming gradient $\bar{v}$ and returns $\bar{v} J$, never materializing $J$. That is why reverse mode is cheap. For a function $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, one reverse sweep gives all $n$ partial derivatives at roughly the cost of one forward pass; forward-mode AD would need $n$ sweeps. Gradients accumulate into `.grad` by addition, which is why you call `zero_grad()` and also why a tensor used twice correctly gets both contributions summed.

Three consequences worth having ready:

- `detach()` returns a tensor that shares storage but has no graph edge, so gradient flow stops there. This is how you implement a stop-gradient (target networks, straight-through estimators, freezing an encoder).
- `torch.no_grad()` skips graph construction entirely. It saves the activation memory, which is why inference and evaluation loops run under it.
- In-place ops (`x += 1`, `relu_()`) overwrite a buffer some node saved for its backward. If that node still needs the pre-op value, you get *"one of the variables needed for gradient computation has been modified by an inplace operation."* The graph is fine; the saved tensor is not.

Activation memory, not parameter memory, is usually what makes a deep model not fit. Gradient checkpointing is the direct trade: drop the saved activations for a block and recompute them during the backward pass, paying about 30% more compute for a large memory cut.

## Part 5 — Vanishing and exploding gradients as arithmetic

**Goal: replace the slogan with a number.** Chain the per-layer rule from Part 2 across $L$ layers. The gradient at layer $\ell$ is

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_\ell} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_L} \prod_{i=\ell+1}^{L} \bigl( D_i W_i^{\top} \bigr)
$$

where $D_i$ is the diagonal matrix of activation derivatives at layer $i$. A product of $L - \ell$ matrices. If each factor typically scales magnitude by $\gamma$, the gradient scales by $\gamma^{L-\ell}$, and exponentials are unforgiving in both directions.

**Vanishing.** The sigmoid $\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$ has derivative $\sigma(1-\sigma)$, maximized at $z = 0$ where it equals exactly $0.25$. So $D_i$ contributes at most $0.25$ per layer even in the best case. Ten sigmoid layers: $0.25^{10} \approx 9.5 \times 10^{-7}$. The first layer's gradient is a millionth of the last layer's, and with a learning rate tuned for the top of the network the bottom simply does not move. Away from $z = 0$ it is far worse: at $|z| = 5$, $\sigma' \approx 0.0066$, so a single saturated unit contributes a factor of $1/150$. Tanh is better (derivative $1 - \tanh^2$, max $1$ at zero) but saturates the same way at the tails.

**Exploding.** Nothing caps the $W_i^{\top}$ factors. If weights are initialized so each layer multiplies the gradient norm by $1.5$, then across 30 layers you get $1.5^{30} \approx 1.9 \times 10^{5}$. One step at a normal learning rate lands the weights somewhere absurd, the next forward pass overflows, and the loss prints `NaN`. You detect this by logging per-layer gradient norms, not by staring at the loss — the norm spikes before the loss does.

**Why RNNs are the pathological case.** A recurrent net applies *the same* weight matrix at every timestep, so the product becomes $W^{T}$, a matrix power. Its behavior is governed by the spectral radius: less than 1 and it decays to zero geometrically, greater than 1 and it blows up. There is no averaging-out across differently-initialized layers to save you. LSTMs fix this structurally by giving the cell state an additive path with a gate near 1, not by choosing a nicer activation. You will not be asked the gate equations; you will be asked why the problem is worse in RNNs, and "repeated multiplication by the same Jacobian" is the answer.

**Why ReLU won.** $\mathrm{ReLU}'(z)$ is exactly $1$ for $z > 0$. Not $0.25$, not $0.9$ — exactly $1$. The activation stops contributing any shrink factor at all, and the entire burden of keeping the product near 1 moves to the weight matrices, where initialization (He/Kaiming's variance $2/\text{fan-in}$, the factor of 2 compensating for ReLU killing half the distribution) and normalization layers can control it. ReLU is also trivially cheap, and it produces exact zeros, which makes activations sparse.

The cost is the other branch: $\mathrm{ReLU}'(z) = 0$ for $z \le 0$. A unit whose pre-activation is negative for *every* input in the data has permanently zero gradient and can never recover. That is a **dead ReLU**. You detect it by logging the fraction of zero activations per layer during training; a layer sitting at 95% zeros and rising is dying. Usual cause is too high a learning rate driving a large negative bias, and the usual fixes are a lower learning rate, a smaller bias init, or a leaky variant. Modern transformers use GELU or SwiGLU, which are smooth and have small nonzero gradient just below zero, so the dead branch is soft rather than absolute. That is the practical reason, more than any story about probabilistic gating.

## Part 6 — Why depth helps

**Goal: give the concrete construction instead of the theorem everyone recites.** The universal approximation theorem says one hidden layer of sufficient width can approximate any continuous function. It is true, it is on every prep list, and it predicts nothing useful, because it says nothing about how many units you need or whether gradient descent will find them.

The useful statement is about efficiency. A ReLU network is a piecewise-linear function, and the relevant currency is how many linear pieces it can create per parameter. One hidden layer of $n$ ReLU units on a 1-D input gives at most $n + 1$ pieces: each unit contributes one kink. Pieces grow **linearly** in width.

Now compose. Two ReLU units can implement the tent map $f(x) = 2\min(x, 1-x)$ on $[0,1]$, which folds the interval onto itself. Composing $L$ tents gives a sawtooth with $2^L$ linear pieces using about $2L$ units. Pieces grow **exponentially** in depth. To match an $L$-layer sawtooth, a shallow network needs on the order of $2^L$ units. Telgarsky (2016) made this a separation theorem; Montúfar et al. (2014) counted regions in the general case.

That is depth's real argument: composition reuses features, and each layer multiplies rather than adds. The catch is exactly Part 5 — the same composition that multiplies expressiveness also multiplies gradient magnitudes, which is why deep networks were hard to train until initialization, normalization, and residual connections arrived to control the product.

## Tradeoff matrix

| Activation | Derivative range | Saturates? | Gradient cost per layer | Where it lives now |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sigmoid | $(0, 0.25]$ | Both tails | $\le 0.25$ always | Output layer for binary probability only |
| Tanh | $(0, 1]$ | Both tails | $\le 1$, but tails kill it | Small RNNs, gates |
| ReLU | $\lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace$ | Negative half is dead | Exactly 1 or 0 | CNNs, most MLPs |
| Leaky ReLU | $\lbrace \alpha, 1 \rbrace$ | No | 1 or $\alpha$ | Dead-unit insurance |
| GELU / SwiGLU | Smooth, small negative | No hard zero | Near 1 in the active region | Transformer feedforward blocks |

## Production failure modes

1. **Loss goes to `NaN` at step 400.** Almost always exploding gradients or a numerically unstable loss. Log the global grad norm every step; you will see it spike one or two steps before the `NaN`. Fixes: gradient clipping to keep the run alive, then actually fix the conditioning (lower LR, warmup, check for a division by a near-zero, check for `log(0)`).
2. **A softmax over raw logits with no max-subtraction.** Works fine in testing on small logits, overflows to `inf` in production when a confident model produces logits near 1000. Always subtract the row max.
3. **Silent dead layer.** A ReLU layer drifts to 100% zero activations after a learning-rate change. The loss keeps decreasing because the rest of the network compensates, so nothing alerts. Only per-layer activation-sparsity logging catches this.
4. **Forgetting `zero_grad()`.** Gradients accumulate by design, so a missing zero-out means step $t$ trains on the sum of all gradients up to $t$. The loss looks like it is exploding for no reason. The same accumulation is a *feature* when you are deliberately doing gradient accumulation across micro-batches.
5. **Evaluation loop without `no_grad()`.** The graph is built and activations retained for the whole validation set, and you OOM at eval time on a model that trains fine.
6. **In-place op inside a residual block.** `x += self.f(x)` throws the modified-by-an-inplace-operation error, while `x = x + self.f(x)` does not. Same math, different graph bookkeeping.

## Interview gotchas

- **"Write backprop for a 2-layer network in vectorized NumPy."** They are grading shapes and stability, not cleverness. Say the two rules out loud as you write: weight gradient is `inputs.T @ delta`, bias gradient is `delta.sum(axis=0)`. Subtract the max inside softmax. Divide by the batch size exactly once. Finish by saying you would gradient-check one entry against a finite difference.
- **"Why did ReLU replace sigmoid?"** The cheap answer is "it's faster." The graded answer is the product of Jacobians: sigmoid's derivative is capped at 0.25, so ten layers multiply the gradient by at most $10^{-6}$; ReLU's derivative on the active branch is exactly 1, so the activation contributes no shrink factor.
- **"How do you know gradients are exploding?"** Per-layer gradient-norm logging showing a spike, `NaN` loss, or weights that jump orders of magnitude. Then the follow-up you should pre-empt: clipping keeps the run alive but treats the symptom; the cure is initialization, normalization, residual connections, warmup, or a lower LR.
- **"What's a dead neuron?"** A ReLU unit whose pre-activation is negative on all inputs, so its gradient is identically zero and it never recovers. Detect via activation sparsity per layer. Prevent with a lower LR, sane bias init, or a leaky/GELU variant.
- **"Why not two linear layers in a row?"** $W_1 W_2$ is one matrix of rank at most $\min$ of the dimensions. You get a bottleneck and more parameters, no extra expressiveness.
- **"How does autograd work?"** The forward pass builds a DAG whose nodes store the inputs their local derivative needs; `.backward()` walks it in reverse topological order applying vector-Jacobian products and accumulating into `.grad`. `detach()` cuts an edge, `no_grad()` skips recording, in-place ops corrupt saved tensors.
- **"Why is depth better than width?"** Skip the universal approximation theorem. Give the sawtooth: $2L$ ReLU units in depth make $2^L$ linear pieces; a single hidden layer needs on the order of $2^L$ units for the same function. Depth multiplies, width adds.
- **"Why are RNNs especially susceptible?"** They multiply by the *same* Jacobian at every timestep, so the gradient behaves like a matrix power governed by the spectral radius. Decay or blow-up is geometric with sequence length, and no per-layer variation averages it out.
