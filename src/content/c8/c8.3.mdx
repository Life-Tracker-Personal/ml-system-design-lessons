## TL;DR

A Sharpe ratio is not a number, it is an estimate with a standard error, and that standard error is enormous relative to the Sharpes anyone actually runs. For independent returns the standard error of the Sharpe is roughly $1/\sqrt{n}$ in per-period units, where $n$ is the number of return observations, and that collapses to one clean fact worth memorising: **the t-statistic of a track record is $t = SR_{\text{ann}} \times \sqrt{T_{\text{years}}}$**. A Sharpe of 1 needs about four years of daily data before it is even nominally significant, and about eight before you would reliably detect it. That is why a backtest is evidence about a search procedure, not evidence about a strategy.

Four consequences follow. A very large claimed Sharpe is refuted in days rather than years, because the required sample size scales as $1/SR^2$ in the true Sharpe $SR$. Positive autocorrelation makes the naive $\sqrt{252}$ annualization overstate the Sharpe, sometimes by more than half. Testing 100 signals and finding 5 at $p < 0.05$ is exactly what pure noise produces, which is what Bonferroni, Benjamini-Hochberg and the Deflated Sharpe Ratio exist to correct. And in high dimensions the covariance matrix you feed the optimizer is not merely noisy, it is singular, which means the optimizer can find portfolios the data claims have zero risk.

## What you'll be able to do after this lesson

- Derive the standard error of the Sharpe ratio by the delta method and convert it into $t = SR\sqrt{T}$.
- Compute how many years of daily data separate a Sharpe of 1 from a Sharpe of 0, at 5 percent and at 80 percent power.
- Answer the Sharpe-8 question cold: how many losing days it takes to reject the claim, and why the answer is days rather than years.
- Apply the Lo (2002) autocorrelation correction and say which direction the naive $\sqrt{q}$ scaling errs, for $q$ sampling periods per year.
- Decompose in-sample-to-live Sharpe decay into overfitting, multiple testing, alpha decay, regime change and costs, with the cost arithmetic done explicitly.
- Show why a universe rebuilt from today's index membership biases mean, volatility and drawdown all in the same direction, and say what to ask about a backtest you did not run.
- Run Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg on a real p-value list, and explain what the Deflated Sharpe Ratio adds on top of the Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio.
- Explain exactly which observations leak under k-fold CV on a forward-return label, and size the purge and the embargo.
- Diagnose a singular or negative-eigenvalue covariance matrix and name the fixes.

## Part 1 — The Sharpe ratio is an estimator

Start with what you are actually computing. Over $n$ periods you observe returns $r_1, \dots, r_n$ and form

$$\widehat{SR} = \frac{\hat\mu}{\hat\sigma}, \qquad \hat\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum r_t, \qquad \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum (r_t - \hat\mu)^2$$

A hat throughout means a quantity computed from your $n$ observations rather than the unknown truth. Both the numerator and the denominator are random. The population quantity $SR = \mu/\sigma$, built from the true per-period mean $\mu$ and true per-period standard deviation $\sigma$, is fixed; your estimate is not, and the question is how far apart they can be.

Assume for a moment that returns are independent and identically distributed normal draws. Then $\hat\mu$ and $\hat\sigma$ are independent, $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu) = \sigma^2/n$, and $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\sigma^2) = 2\sigma^4/n$. That gets you the variance of $\hat\sigma^2$, but the Sharpe divides by $\hat\sigma$, not $\hat\sigma^2$, so you need to push a variance through a nonlinear function. The trick is to linearise: near the truth, a smooth function $f$ moves by roughly $f'$ times however much its argument moved, so $\mathrm{Var}(f(\hat\theta)) \approx f'(\theta)^2\,\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)$. That first-order propagation is called the delta method. Applied to $\hat\sigma = \sqrt{\hat\sigma^2}$, whose derivative at $\sigma^2$ is $1/(2\sigma)$, it gives $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\sigma) \approx \left(\frac{1}{2\sigma}\right)^2 \cdot \frac{2\sigma^4}{n} = \frac{\sigma^2}{2n}$.

The Sharpe is a function of two estimates, so linearise again, now in two variables: the variance of $g(\hat\mu, \hat\sigma)$ is the sum of each partial derivative squared times the variance of the corresponding estimate. For $g(\mu, \sigma) = \mu/\sigma$ the partials are $1/\sigma$ and $-\mu/\sigma^2$:

$$\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{SR}) \approx \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \frac{\mu^2}{\sigma^4}\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}{2n} = \frac{1}{n}\left(1 + \frac{SR^2}{2}\right)$$

$$\boxed{\;\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{SR}) \approx \sqrt{\frac{1 + SR^2/2}{n}}\;}$$

Two things to notice. The leading term is $1/\sqrt{n}$ and does not depend on the strategy at all. The $SR^2/2$ correction is negligible at daily frequency, where most of this work happens: an annualized Sharpe of 1 is a daily Sharpe of $1/\sqrt{252} = 0.063$, which makes the bracket $1.002$, a 0.1 percent effect. So in daily units, **the standard error of the Sharpe is $1/\sqrt{n}$ and nothing else matters**.

### The one formula to memorise

Convert to annualized units. If the sampling period is daily, the daily Sharpe $\widehat{SR}_{\text{day}}$ relates to the annualized Sharpe $SR_{\text{ann}}$ by $\widehat{SR}_{\text{day}} = SR_{\text{ann}}/\sqrt{252}$, and $n = 252\,T$ for a track record of $T$ years. The t-statistic, the estimate divided by its own standard error, is

$$t = \frac{\widehat{SR}_{\text{day}}}{\mathrm{SE}} \approx \widehat{SR}_{\text{day}}\sqrt{n} = \frac{SR_{\text{ann}}}{\sqrt{252}}\sqrt{252\,T} = SR_{\text{ann}}\sqrt{T}$$

The sampling frequency cancels: the $\sqrt{252}$ that shrinks the per-period Sharpe is the same $\sqrt{252}$ that inflates the observation count. Sampling more often does not buy you significance, only more calendar time does. Every question about whether a backtest is real starts here, because $SR_{\text{ann}}$ and the number of years are the only two inputs it needs.

### Working the arithmetic

How long to distinguish a Sharpe of 1 from a Sharpe of 0? At the two-sided 5 percent level you need $t \ge 1.96$, so $\sqrt{T} \ge 1.96$ and $T \ge 3.84$ years. That is only the coin-flip threshold, because at exactly $T = 3.84$ your *expected* t-statistic sits on the critical value, and the realised one lands above it half the time. To detect the strategy 80 percent of the time, the expected t has to clear the critical value by the point of the standard normal that leaves 20 percent in the upper tail, $z_{0.20} = 0.84$. So $t \ge 2.80$ and $T \ge 7.84$ years.

Because $T$ scales as $1/SR^2$ the picture degrades fast: a Sharpe of $0.5$ needs $(2.80/0.5)^2 = 31$ years, a Sharpe of $2$ needs $2.0$ years. Almost every strategy anyone actually runs sits in the region where a decade of honest data cannot prove it works.

> **Stop and think.** Your backtest reports Sharpe 1.5 over 3 years of daily data. Is it significant? Compute $t$ before reading on, then ask yourself what else you would want to know before believing it. The number takes one line; what you do with it does not.

$t = 1.5 \times \sqrt{3} = 1.5 \times 1.732 = 2.60$, one-sided $p \approx 0.005$. That looks strong. Part 5 is the reason it usually is not.

## Part 2 — The Sharpe-8 question

A strategy claims a Sharpe of 8, and after $n$ days it has lost money. What must $n$ be before you reject the claim?

The reflex is "you need years of track record", carried over from Part 1. It points the wrong way here. Part 1 asked how long until you can prove a small edge exists; this asks how long until you can disprove an enormous one, and those two sample sizes move in opposite directions. **An extreme claim is refuted fast.** An annualized Sharpe of 8 means a daily Sharpe of

$$SR_{\text{day}} = \frac{8}{\sqrt{252}} = \frac{8}{15.875} = 0.504$$

Write $S_n = r_1 + \dots + r_n$ for the cumulative return over the first $n$ days. Under the claim, with per-day mean $\mu$ and per-day standard deviation $\sigma$, $S_n$ has mean $n\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{n}$, so the probability of being down after $n$ days is

$$P(S_n \le 0) = \Phi\!\left(-\frac{n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\right) = \Phi\!\left(-SR_{\text{day}}\sqrt{n}\right) = \Phi(-0.504\sqrt{n})$$

Reject one-sided at 5 percent when $0.504\sqrt{n} \ge 1.645$, so $\sqrt{n} \ge 3.265$ and $n \ge 10.7$. **Eleven trading days.** Check it: at $n = 11$, $0.504 \times 3.317 = 1.672$, giving $p = 0.047$. Just over two weeks of flat-to-down PnL kills the claim.

Now the same computation for an ordinary claim. Sharpe 1 means $SR_{\text{day}} = 0.063$, so $0.063\sqrt{n} \ge 1.645$ gives $n \ge 682$ days, about 2.7 years of losing money before you can reject at 5 percent. Writing $n^\star$ for the smallest $n$ at which the claim falls, the general result is worth carrying:

$$n^\star = \left(\frac{1.645}{SR_{\text{ann}}/\sqrt{252}}\right)^2 = \frac{682}{SR_{\text{ann}}^2}\ \text{trading days}$$

Sharpe 8 gives 11 days, Sharpe 2 gives 170 days, Sharpe 1 gives 682 days. The contrast is what matters. A big claimed Sharpe is a big claim in the statistical sense, since it predicts almost no losing fortnights, and it hands you the sample size to kill it with. A modest claimed Sharpe is nearly unfalsifiable on any timescale a fund will wait.

## Part 3 — Annualization when returns are autocorrelated

The $\sqrt{252}$ in Part 1 assumed independence. Where does it come from, and what happens when it fails?

The annual return is the sum of $q$ period returns, where $q$ is the number of sampling periods in a year: 252 for daily data, 12 for monthly. Its mean is $q\mu$. Its variance is

$$\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{q} r_t\right) = q\sigma^2 + 2\sigma^2\sum_{k=1}^{q-1}(q-k)\rho_k$$

where $\rho_k$ is the lag-$k$ autocorrelation of the return series, for lags $k = 1, \dots, q-1$, and the $(q-k)$ weight counts how many pairs of observations inside the window sit exactly $k$ apart. If all $\rho_k = 0$ the sum is $q\sigma^2$, the standard deviation is $\sigma\sqrt{q}$, and the Sharpe scales by $\sqrt{q}$. That is the only case where $\sqrt{252}$ is correct.

In general the mean still scales by $q$ but the standard deviation no longer scales by $\sqrt{q}$, so the annualized Sharpe is the per-period Sharpe multiplied by $q$ over the true $q$-period standard deviation. That ratio is Lo's (2002) scaling factor:

$$\eta(q) = \frac{q}{\sqrt{q + 2\sum_{k=1}^{q-1}(q-k)\rho_k}}$$

With positive autocorrelation the sum under the square root exceeds $q$, so $\eta(q) < \sqrt{q}$ and **the naive $\sqrt{q}$ scaling overstates the annualized Sharpe**.

Work Lo's own illustration with monthly data, $q = 12$, and returns following an AR(1) process, meaning each month's return regresses on the previous one with coefficient $\rho = 0.5$, so that $\rho_k = 0.5^k$. The weighted sum is $11(0.5) + 10(0.25) + 9(0.125) + 8(0.0625) + \dots = 5.5 + 2.5 + 1.125 + 0.5 + 0.219 + 0.094 + \dots \approx 10.0$. That sum enters the variance doubled, adding 20 to the 12 already there. So the denominator is $\sqrt{12 + 20} = \sqrt{32} = 5.657$ and $\eta = 12/5.657 = 2.12$, against a naive $\sqrt{12} = 3.464$. The naive number is $3.464/2.12 = 1.63$ times too big, a 63 percent overstatement. A milder $\rho = 0.2$ gives a weighted sum of $\approx 2.69$, so a denominator of $\sqrt{12 + 5.375} = \sqrt{17.375} = 4.17$, $\eta = 2.88$, and a 20 percent overstatement.

Positive autocorrelation in a reported return stream is usually a symptom, not a feature. Illiquid positions marked to stale prices smooth the series, and monthly marks on private or thinly traded assets do the same. A fund reporting a monthly Sharpe of 2 on a book with lag-1 autocorrelation $\rho_1 = 0.4$ is inflating it by tens of percent through the annualization alone, before anyone argues about alpha. Checking the lag-1 autocorrelation of a claimed track record is a thirty-second sanity test, and almost nobody handed a track record runs it.

## Part 4 — Why the in-sample Sharpe does not survive

Backtest Sharpe rarely survives contact with live capital. Five distinct mechanisms drive the decay, and separating them matters because each has a different fix.

**1. Overfitting.** You tuned parameters to the sample. The reported Sharpe is the maximum over a grid, and the maximum of noisy estimates is biased upward even when every configuration is worthless.

**2. Multiple testing.** You tested many candidate signals, not one. It is the largest single term in most decays, and Part 5 takes it apart.

**3. Alpha decay.** The edge was real and is being competed away. Published anomalies decay measurably as capital arrives, so monitor rolling signal quality and expect a half-life rather than assuming the edge is stationary.

**4. Regime change.** The relationship held under one volatility, rate or liquidity environment and not another. After a single break, regime change and overfitting produce the same picture: good in sample, bad after a date. One break is one observation, and it does not let you distinguish them.

**5. Transaction costs.** Usually simulated optimistically or omitted. Sharpe is scale-free and cost is not, so you cannot subtract a cost from a Sharpe directly.

Take a gross backtest Sharpe of 3 with 100 percent daily turnover and 5 basis points round-trip cost. Cost drag is $252 \times 1.0 \times 0.0005 = 12.6$ percent per year. To subtract it you need the return level, which means you need the volatility. At 10 percent annualized volatility, gross return is $3 \times 0.10 = 30$ percent, net is $17.4$ percent, and net Sharpe is $1.74$. At 5 percent volatility, gross return is 15 percent, net is 2.4 percent, and net Sharpe collapses to $0.48$. Same gross Sharpe, same costs, opposite verdicts. Capacity finishes the job: market impact grows roughly as the square root of participation rate, so the cost term rises with assets under management until net Sharpe hits zero.

Three numbers make the triage concrete: backtest 1.8, paper 1.2, live 0.4, with 0.6 to account for at each step. The first gap is a research-process failure (overfitting, look-ahead, unrealistic fills). The second is an execution failure (implementation shortfall, market impact, adverse selection, queue position). Different people diagnose them with different data, and conflating them is the mistake.

HRT publishes a three-word rubric for defending a modelling choice: **simplicity** (prefer the linear model when it ties), **reproducibility** (stable across seeds, hyperparameters, implementations), **generality** (works across problems, not one benchmark). Jump's named rejection reason is failure to articulate methodology choices, which puts the weight on stating the choice and the alternative you rejected rather than on adding another technique.

### The bias that is already in the sample

The five mechanisms above act on the search or on the world. You tried too many things, the edge got competed away, the environment moved, the fills were fantasy. There is a separate class of error that acts on the data itself, before any search runs, and it is worth separating because every correction in this lesson assumes it away.

State it in general form first. You care about some population of units and an outcome $y$ measured on them. Your table is not the population. Some rule decided which units got a row. Write $Z = 1$ for a unit that appears in your sample and $Z = 0$ for one that does not. If $Z$ carries no information about $y$, then the sample mean of $y$ estimates the population mean $E[y]$ and everything downstream is honest. If $Z$ and $y$ are dependent, the sample mean estimates the conditional mean $E[y \mid Z = 1]$ instead, and the difference

$$\text{bias} = E[y \mid Z = 1] - E[y]$$

does not shrink with $n$. More rows make the estimate more precisely wrong. This is the whole idea: **if the rule that decides which rows exist correlates with the outcome, every statistic computed on that sample is biased.** Purging, embargoes, walk-forward splits, Bonferroni, the Deflated Sharpe Ratio — all of them partition, reweight or discount rows that are already in front of you. None of them can restore a row that was never written down. The damage happened upstream of the split.

#### The concrete case: a universe built from today's index

You want to backtest a US equity strategy over 2010 to 2025. The obvious move is to pull the current members of a large-cap index, download 15 years of daily prices for each name, and run the strategy on that panel. Every name in that panel is a company that still exists, is still listed, and is still large enough to be in the index on the day you pulled the file.

Absent from it: firms that went bankrupt, firms delisted for failing a listing standard, firms acquired at a distressed price, and firms that shrank out of the index. The membership rule that put a row in your table is "was in the index on the day I ran the query", and whether a firm satisfies that rule is a function of the returns you are trying to measure. Three consequences, all pointing the same way:

- Measured mean return is too high, because the worst realizations were deleted.
- Measured volatility is too low, because the deleted realizations are the large-magnitude ones.
- Measured drawdown is too shallow, because delistings cluster in exactly the periods a drawdown is measuring.

The name for this is **survivorship bias**.

#### Working the numbers

These figures are assumptions chosen to make the arithmetic legible, not measured quantities. The mechanism is what transfers; the magnitude depends entirely on the universe and the period.

1. The benchmark universe holds 500 names. In an average year a fraction $d_B$ of them leaves for a bad reason. Assume $E[d_B] = 0.03$ and, because delistings cluster in bad years rather than arriving at a steady rate, a standard deviation of $0.02$ around that.
2. The strategy holds 50 names selected on cheapness. Cheapness and distress travel together, so assume the strategy's exit rate is twice the base rate, $d_S = 2 d_B$.
3. A name that leaves realizes $-60$ percent over its final year before the position goes to zero.
4. Names that survive the year return 10 percent in the benchmark and 13 percent in the strategy. These are the only numbers a survivor-only panel can report, because those are the only names in it.
5. Among surviving names, the strategy's excess return over the benchmark has an annualized standard deviation of 3 percent, independent of the delisting channel.
6. The backtest covers 15 years.

**What the survivor-only panel reports.** Strategy 13 percent, benchmark 10 percent, edge 3.00 percentage points per year, with 3 percent tracking error. The information ratio — the same quantity as a Sharpe ratio, computed on excess return over a benchmark rather than over cash — is $3.00/3.00 = 1.00$. Its t-statistic is $1.00 \times \sqrt{15} = 1.00 \times 3.873 = 3.87$. That is a strong-looking result on the face of it.

**What the full panel reports.** Restore the departed names. The strategy's realized return is a weighted average over the names that survived and the names that did not:

$$(1 - 0.06)(13) + (0.06)(-60) = 12.22 - 3.60 = 8.62 \text{ percent}$$

and the benchmark's is

$$(1 - 0.03)(10) + (0.03)(-60) = 9.70 - 1.80 = 7.90 \text{ percent}$$

The true edge is $8.62 - 7.90 = 0.72$ percentage points, against the 3.00 the survivor panel reported. Of the apparent edge, $(3.00 - 0.72)/3.00 = 0.76$, so 76 percent of it was the deletion rule and not the strategy.

Now the volatility, which is the half people forget. Write the strategy's annual return as a function of the exit rate: with 13 percent on survivors and $-60$ on leavers, it is $13(1 - d_S) - 60\,d_S = 13 - 73\,d_S$, and substituting $d_S = 2 d_B$ gives $13 - 146\,d_B$. The benchmark is $10(1 - d_B) - 60\,d_B = 10 - 70\,d_B$. Subtract:

$$\text{excess return} = 3 - 76\,d_B$$

Check it at the average exit rate: $3 - 76(0.03) = 3 - 2.28 = 0.72$, matching the figure above. The point of writing it this way is the coefficient. Every one-point move in $d_B$ swings the excess return by 76 basis points, so the standard deviation of $d_B$ feeds straight through: $76 \times 0.02 = 1.52$ percentage points of annual volatility that the survivor panel records as zero. Adding it to the 3 percent from surviving names, and treating the two channels as independent,

$$\text{true tracking error} = \sqrt{3^2 + 1.52^2} = \sqrt{9 + 2.31} = \sqrt{11.31} = 3.36 \text{ percent}$$

So the true information ratio is $0.72/3.36 = 0.214$, and its t-statistic over the same 15 years is $0.214 \times \sqrt{15} = 0.214 \times 3.873 = 0.83$. **The t-statistic fell from 3.87 to 0.83, and the only thing that changed was which rows existed.**

The drawdown moves too. Take a year in which the exit rate reaches $d_B = 0.09$, three standard deviations above its mean, which is what a credit crisis looks like in this model. Excess return that year is $3 - 76(0.09) = 3 - 6.84 = -3.84$ percentage points. The survivor panel records $+3.00$ for that year, as it does for every year, because the names that produced the loss are not in it. A backtest built this way does not merely overstate the mean. It deletes the worst year from the sample and then reports a smooth equity curve as evidence of robustness.

One more thing is worth saying plainly, because it separates this from everything else in the lesson. Nothing above involved a search. A single strategy, specified in advance, tested exactly once, with perfectly purged cross-validation and an honest trial count of $N = 1$, still reports 3.00 on this panel. Overfitting corrections price the search. They have nothing to say about the sample.

#### Rebuilding the universe

The fix is to define the universe as of each date rather than once. On each rebalance date $t$, the candidate set must be the securities a person standing at $t$ could actually have traded and would actually have seen in the index. That needs an index membership history with an effective-from and an effective-to date for each security, so the query can ask which rows were live at $t$.

The trap is the vendor field named "current constituents", or a members table with no date column at all. It joins cleanly, it returns 500 tidy rows, and it is correct on exactly one date. A table with no date column cannot fail noisily. It fails silently and always in the same direction, which is the worst combination a data source can have.

This is the same discipline a feature store enforces under the name **point-in-time correctness**, covered in [c1.1](/c/c1/c1.1): when you join a feature to a label at time $t$, you take the value the feature had at $t$, not its current value. What differs is the object it applies to. Point-in-time on columns asks what a number was on a given date. Point-in-time on rows asks which entities existed on that date. A pipeline can get the first perfectly right and the second completely wrong, because the row set is usually fixed once at the top of the query and never revisited, while the feature joins get all the review attention.

Two details decide whether a rebuild is real rather than cosmetic:

- **The delisting return.** Restoring a name to the panel and then ending its price series on its last trading day, with no return booked, deletes the loss a second time. The final period needs a number: liquidation proceeds, the acquisition price, or a documented delisting return for that exchange and reason. A universe rebuilt correctly with missing terminal returns keeps most of the bias.
- **The identifier.** Tickers are recycled. Joining prices to membership on ticker splices two unrelated companies into one series, and the splice tends to land exactly where the first company died. Join on a permanent security identifier.

#### Two relatives worth naming

**Backfill bias**, also called instant history bias. A database adds a fund or a security, and on the day it is added the vendor imports its entire prior history. Which records get imported is not random. A manager submits to a database when the numbers are worth showing, so the pre-inclusion portion of every series is a track record that exists in the data *because* it was good. The conditioning rule is the decision to submit, and that decision saw the returns. The defence is mechanical: drop every observation dated before the record's entry date into the database, which requires the vendor to store that date and requires you to use it.

**Self-selection in reported returns.** Reporting is voluntary and continues at the reporter's discretion. A fund having a bad year can stop reporting and keep trading, so the cross-section of reported returns in a bad year is missing the worst of that year. This is the same conditioning applied to which *periods* of a surviving unit appear, rather than to which units appear, and it defeats the naive repair for survivorship: adding back funds that later closed does not help when their final quarters were never filed in the first place.

#### The same bias outside finance

Generalise the row rule one step and it stops being about markets. Labels often exist only for units that some earlier system chose to act on. A credit model trained on repayment outcomes sees repayment only for applicants the previous policy approved. A fraud model trained on chargebacks sees chargebacks only for transactions that were let through. A ranking model trained on clicks sees clicks only for items that were shown, and shown near the top.

The labelled sample is the output of a policy, not a draw from the world. The old system's rule is now baked into the training distribution: you fit $E[y \mid \text{acted on}]$ and then deploy the model to answer a question about $E[y]$. It is the same $Z$ with the approval decision playing the role of index membership. The general name is **selection bias**; the logged-data version appears in [c6.2](/c/c6/c6.2) as position bias, and the winner's-curse version appears in [c1.1](/c/c1/c1.1) as the optimism of a selected minimum. The reason to notice they are one family is that the diagnostic is identical across all of them. Ask what rule decided which rows exist, then ask whether that rule could have known the outcome.

#### Interrogating someone else's backtest

The practical situation is not defining the term. It is being handed a results deck, no code, and a claimed Sharpe, and needing to find out in ten minutes whether the universe was rebuilt point-in-time. Ask for artefacts that are cheap for an honest pipeline to produce and awkward for a survivor panel to fake.

1. **How many names are in the universe on each date, and can I see that count as a time series?** A membership count that is flat across 15 years is a snapshot replayed backwards. A real index history breathes as names enter and leave. This costs one query and catches most cases.
2. **What is the list of securities that entered and exited, with dates and exit reasons?** Ask for the reason field by name: merger, acquisition, bankruptcy, listing-standard failure, index reconstitution. A pipeline that never stored a reason never handled the delisting return either.
3. **What return was booked in the final period for exiting names, and where did that number come from?** "The series just ends" is a failing answer, because it reintroduces the bias inside an otherwise correct rebuild.
4. **What is the as-of date of the membership table, and does the query filter on it?** If the answer describes a table with no effective-date columns, the rest of the diligence is moot.
5. **What identifier joins prices to membership?** A permanent security identifier is right. Ticker is not.
6. **What fraction of total PnL came from names that no longer exist today?** A 15-year backtest that never once held a company that later died is a reconstructed universe. This number also sizes the exposure directly, since it is the share of the result that lives on names a survivor panel would have deleted.
7. **Rerun it on a panel with delisted names restored, and show both equity curves.** The gap between them is the answer, and every other question is a proxy for it.

The same interrogation applied to fundamental data asks a different question with the same shape: are the accounting values as-reported or as-restated, and does the join use the announcement date or the fiscal period end. Restated financials in a backtest are point-in-time failure on the columns rather than on the rows, and a pipeline that got the row set right can still have that one wrong.

## Part 5 — Multiple testing is the main problem

You test 100 signals and 5 come back at $p < 0.05$. What do you conclude?

Nothing. Suppose the global null holds: every one of the 100 signals is worthless. Each test still fires with probability 0.05 by construction, so the count of false positives $X$ is Binomial$(100, 0.05)$, with mean exactly 5 and standard deviation $\sqrt{100 \times 0.05 \times 0.95} = 2.18$. Observing 5 is the modal outcome of pure noise, and $P(X \ge 5) \approx 0.56$. You have observed exactly what pure noise produces, and a result that pure noise produces half the time is not evidence against pure noise.

### Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg

Write $m$ for the number of tests in the family and $\alpha$ for the false-positive rate you are willing to live with. If you run each test at level $\alpha/m$, the chance that at least one of them fires under the global null is at most $m \times (\alpha/m) = \alpha$, since the probability of a union never exceeds the sum of the parts. That one line is the whole of **Bonferroni**: it controls the family-wise error rate, the probability of even one false positive anywhere. With $m = 100$ and $\alpha = 0.05$ the per-test threshold is $0.0005$. Because the union bound never assumed anything about how the tests relate, Bonferroni holds under any dependence structure, and that generality is exactly why it is conservative. The conservatism bites here, because trading signals are heavily correlated and the effective number of independent tests is far below 100.

Controlling the chance of any error at all is a strict demand. A research pipeline can usually live with a known fraction of junk in a batch. Let $R$ be the number of hypotheses you reject and $V$ the number of those rejections that are false; the quantity to bound is then $E[V/R]$, the expected fraction of your rejections that are wrong, called the false discovery rate. **Benjamini-Hochberg** bounds it with a sorted comparison. Under the global null the $k$-th smallest of $m$ p-values sits near $k/m$, so a p-value far below that line is hard to explain as noise. Write $p_{(k)}$ for the $k$-th smallest p-value, find the largest $k$ with $p_{(k)} \le k\alpha/m$, and reject the first $k$.

Worked example, $m = 100$, $\alpha = 0.10$, sorted p-values $0.0001, 0.0008, 0.004, 0.03, \dots$. The thresholds $k\alpha/m$ are $0.001, 0.002, 0.003, 0.004$. Then $0.0001 \le 0.001$ passes, $0.0008 \le 0.002$ passes, $0.004 > 0.003$ fails, $0.03 > 0.004$ fails. The largest passing $k$ is 2, so reject the top two, where Bonferroni at $\alpha = 0.05$ kept only the first. FDR is the right frame for a research pipeline, where you want a batch of candidates with a bounded junk rate rather than certainty about any one.

### The trap follow-up

Return to Sharpe 1.5 over 3 years, $t = 2.60$. Now add one fact: it was the best of 200 configurations tried. Bonferroni needs $p \le 0.05/200 = 0.00025$, which on a standard normal is a cutoff of $z \ge 3.48$. Setting $SR\sqrt{3} \ge 3.48$ means you needed a Sharpe of $2.01$. Your 1.5 does not clear it, and the shortfall is not marginal.

### Probabilistic and Deflated Sharpe

The **Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio** (PSR) asks: what is the probability that the true Sharpe exceeds a chosen benchmark $SR^\star$, given the observed Sharpe $\widehat{SR}$, the number of periods $n$ in the track record, and the shape of the return distribution? In per-period units,

$$\mathrm{PSR}(SR^\star) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{(\widehat{SR} - SR^\star)\sqrt{n-1}}{\sqrt{1 - \gamma_3\widehat{SR} + \frac{\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\widehat{SR}^2}}\right)$$

with $\gamma_3$ the skewness and $\gamma_4$ the kurtosis (not excess) of the per-period returns, and $\Phi$ the standard normal cumulative distribution function. The denominator is the corrected standard error: negative skew makes $-\gamma_3\widehat{SR}$ positive and inflates it, and fat tails do the same through $\gamma_4$. Set $\gamma_3 = 0$ and $\gamma_4 = 3$, the values for a normal, and it collapses back to $\sqrt{1 + \widehat{SR}^2/2}$, which is Part 1.

The **Deflated Sharpe Ratio** (DSR) is the PSR evaluated against a benchmark that is not zero but the *expected maximum* Sharpe across $N$ independent trials, where $N$ is the number of strategy configurations you searched over. That expectation has a shape worth anticipating: the largest of $N$ normal draws sits near the quantile that cuts off the top $1/N$ of the distribution, $\Phi^{-1}(1 - 1/N)$, and the limiting distribution of a maximum, the Gumbel, refines the answer by blending that quantile with a slightly higher one. Writing $\max_N \widehat{SR}$ for the largest Sharpe among the $N$ trials, the approximation is

$$E[\max_N \widehat{SR}] \approx \sigma_{\widehat{SR}}\left[(1-\gamma)\,\Phi^{-1}\!\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{N}\right) + \gamma\,\Phi^{-1}\!\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{Ne}\right)\right]$$

Here the bare $\gamma \approx 0.5772$ is the Euler-Mascheroni constant that appears in the Gumbel limit, unrelated to the moment symbols $\gamma_3$ and $\gamma_4$ above, and $\sigma_{\widehat{SR}}$ is the standard deviation of the Sharpes across your $N$ trials, which is what sets the scale of the noise you were selecting from. So DSR is PSR with a multiple-testing benchmark in place of zero, and the two are one construction described in two steps.

Run the numbers on the Sharpe-1.5 backtest: $N = 200$ trials whose Sharpes had cross-sectional standard deviation $0.5$. Then $\Phi^{-1}(0.995) = 2.576$, and since $Ne = 200 \times 2.718 = 543.7$, the second quantile is $\Phi^{-1}(1 - 1/543.7) = 2.905$. Weighting the two by $1 - \gamma$ and $\gamma$ gives $E[\max] \approx 0.5[0.4228(2.576) + 0.5772(2.905)] = 1.38$. **The expected best-of-200 Sharpe under the null is 1.38 annualized.** Your 1.5 is barely above the noise ceiling.

Deflate it. In daily units $\widehat{SR} = 1.5/15.875 = 0.0945$ and $SR^\star = 1.38/15.875 = 0.0871$, with $n = 756$ trading days in three years. The numerator is the gap $(0.0945 - 0.0871)$ times $\sqrt{755} = 27.48$, which is $0.203$, and with normal moments the denominator is $1.002$. So

$$\mathrm{DSR} = \Phi(0.203) = 0.58$$

against the undeflated $\mathrm{PSR}(0) = \Phi(0.0945 \times 27.48 / 1.002) = \Phi(2.59) = 0.995$, which benchmarks against zero instead. The same track record goes from 99.5 percent confidence to 58 percent purely by accounting for the search. Nothing about the returns changed; only the benchmark did.

One honest caveat on the moment terms. At daily frequency $\widehat{SR}$ is tiny, so the skew and kurtosis corrections barely move the answer even with $\gamma_4 = 15$. They bite at low frequency where $\widehat{SR}$ per period is large. At monthly frequency the same annualized 1.5 gives $\widehat{SR} = 0.433$, and with $\gamma_3 = -1.5$, $\gamma_4 = 8$ the denominator becomes $\sqrt{1 + 0.650 + 0.328} = 1.41$ against $1.05$ under normality, a 34 percent inflation of the standard error. Short-volatility strategies reporting monthly are exactly where this correction earns its keep.

## Part 6 — Why k-fold cross-validation is invalid here

Standard k-fold cross-validation shuffles observations into $k$ folds, trains on $k-1$ of them and tests on the one held out. On financial time series this breaks in two independent ways, and fixing one leaves the other untouched.

**Ordering.** Shuffling trains the model on the future to predict the past. Any relationship that exists only because you already know how the period ended is available in training and is not available live. That alone disqualifies shuffled k-fold no matter how the folds are cut.

**Label overlap.** This is the mechanical leak, and the discriminating question is how much you must drop. Suppose the label is a 5-day forward return, so the label at time $t$ is a function of prices over $[t, t+5]$. If $t$ sits in a training fold and $t+3$ sits in the test fold, the two labels share three days of realized return. They are not independent observations, and the model can score well on the test fold by memorising overlapping outcomes.

The fix is **purging**: remove from training every observation whose label interval overlaps a test label interval. Write $h$ for the label horizon, the number of days of future price the label spans, so $h = 5$ in this example. For a contiguous test block that means dropping the $h$ observations immediately before the block, whose labels reach into it, and the $h$ immediately after the block end, whose labels overlap those of the last test points. **Purge width equals the label horizon**, so a 5-day label costs 5 observations on each side.

Purging is not enough, because features are serially correlated too. A 20-day moving average computed just after the test block still carries information from inside it. So you add an **embargo**: extra observations dropped after the test block, sized by the memory of your features, commonly 1 percent of the sample. On 1000 daily observations with 5 folds, a 5-day label and a 10-day embargo, you lose roughly $5 \times (5 + 5 + 10) = 100$ observations. That is the price of an honest estimate.

**Walk-forward** is the other standard: train on everything up to a date $T_1$, test on the window from $T_1$ to a later date $T_2$, roll both dates forward, never look back. It is the closest analogue of live deployment, because it never trains on anything that happened after the test window. The costs are real. Early folds train on little data, so the model you evaluate is not the model you would deploy, and you get far fewer test observations than k-fold, so the out-of-sample Sharpe is itself noisy in exactly the way Part 1 described. Expanding windows are more stable, rolling windows adapt faster to regime change, and choosing between them is a judgement to defend rather than a setting.

The name "purged cross-validation" is the easy half. The number that follows from it, the purge width, is the part that has to be derived from the label horizon each time.

## Part 7 — Covariance in high dimensions

Every portfolio construction step takes a covariance matrix as input. Estimate the sample covariance of 500 stocks from 60 months of data, and something breaks before you get to the optimizer.

Count parameters first. A $500 \times 500$ covariance matrix has $500 \times 501/2 = 125\,250$ free parameters. You have $500 \times 60 = 30\,000$ numbers. You are estimating four times more parameters than you have data points, and no amount of cleverness fixes that arithmetic.

The consequence is structural, not statistical. Write $N$ for the number of assets and $T$ for the number of time periods observed, so $N = 500$ and $T = 60$ here; this $T$ counts observations, not the years of Part 1. The sample covariance $\hat\Sigma = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_t (r_t - \bar r)(r_t - \bar r)^\top$, with $r_t$ the vector of $N$ returns at time $t$ and $\bar r$ their sample mean, is a sum of $T$ rank-one matrices, minus one degree of freedom for the mean, so

$$\mathrm{rank}(\hat\Sigma) \le \min(N,\, T-1) = 59$$

A $500 \times 500$ matrix of rank 59 has at least 441 exactly zero eigenvalues. It is singular, so $\hat\Sigma^{-1}$ does not exist and mean-variance optimization has no solution. Worse than having no solution: every weight vector $w$ in the null space satisfies $w^\top \hat\Sigma w = 0$, a portfolio the data asserts has literally zero risk. Hand that to an optimizer and it levers the position without bound. This is Michaud's "error maximizer": the optimizer does not find the best portfolio, it finds the largest estimation error.

Even when $T > N$ the matrix is ill-conditioned, and there is a precise account of how badly. Take returns that are pure noise, so the true covariance is the identity $I$ and every true eigenvalue is 1. The sample eigenvalues do not cluster at 1; they spread into a band whose width is governed by the aspect ratio $q = N/T$, assets over observations. (This $q$ is the aspect ratio, not the annualization horizon of Part 3.) The Marchenko-Pastur law gives the band exactly: sample eigenvalues fill $[(1-\sqrt{q})^2, (1+\sqrt{q})^2]$. The law is stated for the $1/T$ normalization rather than the $1/(T-1)$ used for $\hat\Sigma$ above; the two differ by a factor $T/(T-1)$, which vanishes in the limit the law describes and is well inside its accuracy at any size where you would invoke it. At $N = 500$, $T = 60$, $q = 8.33$ and the upper edge is $(1 + 2.887)^2 = 15.1$, so noise alone produces eigenvalues fifteen times the true value. With five years of daily data, $T = 1260$, $q = 0.397$ and the edge is $2.66$: everything below 2.66 is still indistinguishable from noise.

### The negative eigenvalue

A risk system reports a negative eigenvalue on a correlation matrix. Start with what that proves. A genuine sample covariance is positive semi-definite by construction, since $w^\top\hat\Sigma w = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_t (w^\top(r_t - \bar r))^2 \ge 0$ for every $w$, being a positive multiple of a sum of squares. A negative eigenvalue is a $w$ with $w^\top\hat\Sigma w < 0$, so the matrix in front of you was not built that way. The usual causes:

- Pairwise-complete estimation, where each pair uses a different window because of missing data or different listing histories.
- Ad-hoc edits: overriding a correlation by hand, blending in a view, flooring or capping entries.
- Mixing estimators, such as a shrunk correlation matrix combined with unshrunk volatilities.
- Floating-point noise on eigenvalues that are truly zero. Diagnose by magnitude: around $10^{-16}$ times the trace is numerical, not structural.

Three fixes, all named. Project to the nearest positive semi-definite matrix by clipping negative eigenvalues to zero and reconstructing (Higham 1988). Clip below the Marchenko-Pastur edge and replace the noise bulk with its average, preserving the trace. Or pull the whole matrix partway toward something you trust more, which is shrinkage: $\hat\Sigma_{\text{shrunk}} = \delta F + (1-\delta)\hat\Sigma$, where $F$ is a structured target such as a constant-correlation matrix, a scaled identity, or a factor model, and $\delta \in [0,1]$ sets how far you move. Picking $\delta$ by hand is arbitrary, so **Ledoit-Wolf** solves for it analytically, choosing the $\delta$ that minimizes the expected squared elementwise distance between the shrunk matrix and the true covariance, the Frobenius loss. Shrinkage also guarantees invertibility, because a positive definite target added to a positive semi-definite matrix is positive definite. The analytic form of $\delta$ is not something to reproduce from memory; the mechanism and the name are.

## Tradeoff matrix

| Correction | What it fixes | Assumption | Cost of using it | When it is the wrong tool |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $t = SR\sqrt{T}$ | naive belief in a Sharpe | iid returns | none, it is arithmetic | autocorrelated or fat-tailed returns |
| Lo $\eta(q)$ | annualization under autocorrelation | stationary $\rho_k$ | need to estimate $\rho_k$ | short samples where $\hat\rho$ is noise |
| Bonferroni | family-wise error over $m$ tests | any dependence | very low power when tests correlate | exploratory research pipelines |
| Benjamini-Hochberg | false discovery rate | independence or positive dependence | some false positives by design | when even one false positive is fatal |
| PSR | length, skew, kurtosis | known higher moments | needs $\ge 100$ obs for stable moments | tiny samples |
| Deflated Sharpe | number of trials on top of PSR | you can count and characterise trials | requires honest $N$ and trial dispersion | when $N$ is unknowable |
| Point-in-time universe | survivorship in the row set | dated membership plus delisting returns exist | vendor data cost, slower joins, more plumbing | a single instrument whose row set cannot change |
| Purged CV + embargo | label overlap and feature memory | you know the label horizon | 10 percent or more of the sample | when ordering itself is the problem |
| Walk-forward | ordering and regime realism | future is like the recent past | few test points, noisy estimate | very short histories |
| Shrinkage / eigenvalue clipping | singular or ill-conditioned $\hat\Sigma$ | target is roughly right | bias toward the target | when $T \gg N$ and $\hat\Sigma$ is fine |

## Production failure modes

1. **Reporting the maximum of a grid search as the strategy's Sharpe.** Every parameter sweep, feature set and universe filter is a trial. Teams that log 3 configurations and try 300 cannot compute a DSR, because the trial count is the input they destroyed.
2. **Annualizing a smoothed return series with $\sqrt{12}$.** Illiquid marks create positive autocorrelation. The reported Sharpe is inflated by tens of percent before anyone argues about alpha. Check $\hat\rho_1$ on any track record you are handed.
3. **Subtracting costs from a Sharpe instead of from a return.** Sharpe is scale-free, cost is not. You must convert to return space using the volatility, and low-volatility strategies die from costs that high-volatility strategies shrug off.
4. **Shuffled k-fold in a research library's default settings.** `sklearn`'s `cross_val_score` shuffles by default in some splitters and never knows your label horizon. The out-of-sample number it prints on a forward-return label is not out of sample.
5. **Re-using the same holdout.** A holdout used fifty times is an in-sample set with extra steps. The multiple-testing correction applies to holdout evaluations too.
6. **Feeding a singular covariance to an optimizer with a pseudo-inverse.** The pseudo-inverse silently ignores the null space rather than erroring, so the position sizes look finite and are still built on directions with zero estimated risk.
7. **Building correlations pairwise across assets with different histories.** It produces a non-PSD matrix, which surfaces later as a negative portfolio variance and a risk report nobody trusts.

## Interview gotchas

- **"A strategy claims Sharpe 8 and has lost money over $n$ days. How large must $n$ be to reject the claim?"** Eleven trading days. Daily Sharpe is $8/\sqrt{252} = 0.504$, $P(\text{down after } n) = \Phi(-0.504\sqrt{n})$, and you reject one-sided at 5 percent once $0.504\sqrt{n} \ge 1.645$. Say explicitly that the answer is days *because* the claim is extreme, and contrast it with Sharpe 1 needing 682 days.
- **"Your backtest has Sharpe 1.5 over 3 years of daily data. Significant?"** $t = 1.5\sqrt{3} = 2.60$. Then volunteer the follow-up yourself: how many configurations were tried. If it was best-of-200, you needed Sharpe 2.01 under Bonferroni.
- **"You test 100 signals and 5 come back at $p < 0.05$. What do you conclude?"** Nothing. Expected false positives under the global null is exactly 5. Then name Bonferroni for FWER, Benjamini-Hochberg for FDR, and DSR for the Sharpe-specific version.
- **"What does the Deflated Sharpe Ratio deflate by?"** By the expected maximum Sharpe over $N$ trials, estimated from the dispersion of your trial Sharpes via an extreme-value approximation. It is the PSR with a non-zero benchmark, and the PSR is where the skew and kurtosis corrections live.
- **"Does positive autocorrelation make the annualized Sharpe too high or too low?"** Too high. The $q$-period variance is $q\sigma^2 + 2\sigma^2\sum(q-k)\rho_k$, so the true denominator exceeds $\sigma\sqrt{q}$ and $\eta(q) < \sqrt{q}$. The monthly case with $\rho = 0.5$ works out to a 63 percent overstatement, and Lo reports errors as large as 65 percent across his examples.
- **"Why can't you use k-fold CV here?"** Two reasons, and give both: shuffling trains on the future, and forward-return labels overlap across the fold boundary. Then size it: purge equal to the label horizon on each side, plus a separate embargo for serially correlated features.
- **"Sample covariance of 500 stocks from 60 months. What's the problem?"** Rank at most 59, so it is singular and has 441 zero eigenvalues, meaning portfolios the data claims are riskless. Add the Marchenko-Pastur edge at $(1+\sqrt{8.33})^2 = 15.1$ to show noise eigenvalues dwarf real ones.
- **"You get a negative eigenvalue. What do you do?"** First say it proves the matrix was not computed as a single sample covariance, because those are PSD by construction. Then check the magnitude for floating-point noise, then name the fixes: nearest-PSD projection, eigenvalue clipping at the MP edge, or Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage.
- **"How would you check whether a backtest's universe was survivorship-free?"** Lead with the cheapest test: plot the universe count by date and see whether it is flat. Then ask for the entry and exit log with exit reasons, and for the return booked in the final period of exiting names, since a rebuilt universe with missing terminal returns keeps most of the bias. Say explicitly that no amount of purged cross-validation repairs this, because the rows were deleted before any split ran.
- **"The model worked 2010 to 2019 and broke in 2020. Regime change or overfit?"** You cannot tell from one break, and saying so is the correct answer. Then describe what would distinguish them: rolling-window parameter stability, a Chow test at the break, CUSUM, and whether the degradation was gradual or discontinuous.
