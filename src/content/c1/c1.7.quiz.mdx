**Q1.** Prove that Lloyd's algorithm monotonically decreases the within-cluster sum of squares and terminates. Why does "terminates" not mean "global optimum"? And why is the centroid update the *mean* (not the median)?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

$J(C,\mu)=\sum_j\sum_{x_i\in C_j}\lVert x_i-\mu_j\rVert^2$ depends on assignments $C$ and centroids $\mu$. Lloyd alternates two coordinate-descent steps:

- **Assign** (fix $\mu$, minimize over $C$): each point goes to its nearest centroid, which minimizes its term pointwise ⇒ $J$ cannot increase.
- **Update** (fix $C$, minimize over each $\mu_j$): $\partial_{\mu_j}\sum_{x\in C_j}\lVert x-\mu_j\rVert^2=-2\sum_{x\in C_j}(x-\mu_j)=0\Rightarrow \mu_j=\overline{C_j}$, the mean ⇒ $J$ cannot increase.

So $J$ is non-increasing and bounded below by 0. There are finitely many possible assignments ($\le k^n$), and the algorithm never repeats one (each step strictly decreases $J$ or stops) ⇒ it **terminates** in finitely many iterations.

**Local, not global.** Both steps minimize over one coordinate block, so the fixed point is a coordinate-descent local minimum of a non-convex objective; different initializations reach different fixed points (hence k-means++ and multiple `n_init`). Classic counter-example: 4 points at the corners of a $2\times1$ rectangle, $k=2$ — left/right split is optimal, top/bottom is a suboptimal local min.

**Mean, not median.** The update minimizes *squared* distance, whose minimizer is the mean. Under $L_1$ (sum of absolute distances, k-medians) the optimal center would be the coordinate-wise median — the same "loss ⇒ optimal point statistic" point as c1.2 (squared→mean, absolute→median).

**Rubric:**
- Both steps weakly decrease $J$; show the update's $\partial/\partial\mu_j=0\Rightarrow$ mean
- Bounded below + finite partitions ⇒ terminates
- Coordinate descent on non-convex $J$ ⇒ local min; init-dependent (counter-example)
- Squared loss ⇒ mean; $L_1$ ⇒ median (c1.2 tie)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q2.** Derive the GMM M-step updates for $\pi_j,\mu_j,\Sigma_j$ as the maximizer of the expected complete-data log-likelihood (ELBO) with responsibilities held fixed.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

With responsibilities $\gamma_{ij}$ fixed (from the E-step), the M-step maximizes $Q=\sum_i\sum_j \gamma_{ij}\bigl[\log\pi_j+\log\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)\bigr]$.

**$\mu_j$:** $\partial_{\mu_j}Q=\sum_i\gamma_{ij}\Sigma_j^{-1}(x_i-\mu_j)=0\Rightarrow \mu_j=\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{ij}x_i}{\sum_i\gamma_{ij}}=\frac{1}{N_j}\sum_i\gamma_{ij}x_i$, with $N_j=\sum_i\gamma_{ij}$.

**$\Sigma_j$:** differentiating the Gaussian log-density w.r.t. $\Sigma_j$ (or $\Sigma_j^{-1}$) and setting to 0 gives the responsibility-weighted covariance $\Sigma_j=\frac{1}{N_j}\sum_i\gamma_{ij}(x_i-\mu_j)(x_i-\mu_j)^\top$.

**$\pi_j$:** maximize $\sum_j N_j\log\pi_j$ s.t. $\sum_j\pi_j=1$. Lagrangian $\sum_j N_j\log\pi_j+\lambda(1-\sum_j\pi_j)$; $\partial_{\pi_j}=N_j/\pi_j-\lambda=0\Rightarrow\pi_j=N_j/\lambda$; summing and using $\sum\pi_j=1$, $\sum_j\pi_j=1\Rightarrow\lambda=\sum_jN_j=n$, so $\pi_j=N_j/n$.

These are exactly the weighted versions of the single-Gaussian MLE — soft counts $N_j$ replace hard counts.

**Rubric:**
- $Q$ = responsibility-weighted complete-data log-likelihood
- $\mu_j$: weighted mean (from $\partial/\partial\mu_j$)
- $\Sigma_j$: weighted covariance
- $\pi_j=N_j/n$ via a Lagrange multiplier for the simplex constraint

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q3.** Show that k-means is the $\varepsilon\to0$ hard-EM limit of a GMM with $\Sigma_j=\varepsilon I$ and equal weights. Which k-means assumption does each of these encode: spherical clusters, shared variance, equal weight?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

With $\Sigma_j=\varepsilon I$ and $\pi_j=1/k$, the E-step responsibility is

$$\gamma_{ij}=\frac{\exp(-\lVert x_i-\mu_j\rVert^2/2\varepsilon)}{\sum_\ell\exp(-\lVert x_i-\mu_\ell\rVert^2/2\varepsilon)}$$

— a softmax over $-\lVert x_i-\mu_j\rVert^2$ with **temperature $\varepsilon$**. As $\varepsilon\to0^+$, the softmax sharpens to a **hard argmin**: $\gamma_{ij}\to\mathbb{1}[j=\arg\min_\ell\lVert x_i-\mu_\ell\rVert^2]$. So:
- the **E-step becomes the assign step** (each point to its nearest centroid);
- the **M-step** $\mu_j=\sum_i\gamma_{ij}x_i/\sum_i\gamma_{ij}$ becomes the **centroid mean**;
- and $-2\varepsilon\log p(x)\to\sum_i\lVert x_i-\mu_{c_i}\rVert^2=$ WCSS (up to constants). So minimizing WCSS = hard-EM in this limit.

**Assumption mapping:** $\Sigma_j=\varepsilon I$ (isotropic) ⇒ **spherical clusters**; the *shared* $\varepsilon$ across components ⇒ **equal spread/variance**; equal $\pi_j$ together with the squared-distance assignment ⇒ bias toward **equal-size clusters**. A full-covariance, free-weight GMM relaxes all three.

**Rubric:**
- Responsibility = softmax with temperature $\varepsilon$; $\varepsilon\to0$ → hard argmin
- E-step → assign; M-step → mean; scaled neg-log-likelihood → WCSS
- Spherical ⇐ $\varepsilon I$; equal variance ⇐ shared $\varepsilon$; equal size ⇐ equal $\pi_j$ + squared distance

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q4.** Prove that the optimal k-means objective $J^\star(k)$ is non-increasing in $k$, with $J^\star(n)=0$. What does this imply about choosing $k$ by minimizing inertia, and how do silhouette / gap / BIC escape the problem?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Monotonicity.** Any optimal $k$-clustering can be reproduced by a $(k{+}1)$-clustering that keeps those $k$ clusters and splits one (or leaves one empty), which can only lower (or keep) the WCSS. So $J^\star(k{+}1)\le J^\star(k)$. At $k=n$, each point is its own centroid ⇒ $J^\star(n)=0$.

**Consequence.** Minimizing inertia over $k$ trivially returns $k=n$ — you **cannot** select $k$ by minimizing $J$. (Exact analogue of $R^2$ being non-decreasing in the number of features, c1.4 — fit always improves with more capacity, so you need a complexity-aware criterion.)

**Escapes:** the **elbow** looks for the kink where marginal WCSS reduction drops (heuristic); the **silhouette** maximizes a separation-vs-cohesion score $s(i)$ that *can* decrease as $k$ grows; the **gap statistic** compares $\log W_k$ to its expectation under a uniform null reference and picks the smallest $k$ beating the null — the only one with a principled baseline; and for a **GMM**, **BIC/AIC** penalize the parameter count, giving a true penalized-likelihood selection.

**Rubric:**
- Nesting/refinement argument ⇒ $J^\star$ non-increasing; $J^\star(n)=0$
- Therefore inertia-minimization ⇒ $k=n$ (can't select $k$ that way); $R^2$ analogy
- Silhouette (peak), gap (null reference), BIC/AIC (penalized likelihood) as valid selectors

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q5.** Prove that EM never decreases the marginal log-likelihood. Then explain what EM actually converges to for a GMM, given that the GMM likelihood is unbounded.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The ELBO.** For any distribution $q_i(z)$ over the latent $z$,

$$\log p(x_i)=\log\sum_z q_i(z)\frac{p(x_i,z)}{q_i(z)}\ \ge\ \sum_z q_i(z)\log\frac{p(x_i,z)}{q_i(z)}=:\text{ELBO}(q_i,\theta)$$

by **Jensen's inequality** ($\log$ concave). The gap is exactly $\log p(x_i)-\text{ELBO}=\text{KL}(q_i\,\|\,p(z\mid x_i,\theta))\ge0$.

**E-step** sets $q_i=p(z\mid x_i,\theta^{\text{old}})$ (the responsibilities) ⇒ KL $=0$ ⇒ the ELBO *touches* $\log p$ at $\theta^{\text{old}}$. **M-step** maximizes $\sum_i\text{ELBO}$ over $\theta$ ⇒ $\sum_i\log p(x_i)\big|_{\theta^{\text{new}}}\ge\text{ELBO}(\theta^{\text{new}})\ge\text{ELBO}(\theta^{\text{old}})=\sum_i\log p(x_i)\big|_{\theta^{\text{old}}}$. So the log-likelihood is **non-decreasing every iteration**.

**What it converges to.** A **local maximum or saddle** — the GMM likelihood is non-concave (multimodal, with $k!$ label-permutation symmetric modes). Worse, it is **unbounded**: a component can latch onto a single point, send its variance $\to0$, and drive the likelihood $\to+\infty$ — a degenerate "global max" that's useless. So EM finds a *useful local* optimum, not the global MLE; in practice you floor the covariance (`reg_covar`), use multiple restarts, or a Bayesian/MAP prior.

**Rubric:**
- ELBO via Jensen; gap = $\text{KL}(q\,\|\,p(z\mid x))$
- E-step zeroes KL (ELBO touches $\log p$); M-step raises ELBO ⇒ monotone non-decreasing likelihood
- Converges to local max/saddle, not global (non-concave)
- Likelihood unbounded (component collapse $\sigma\to0$); `reg_covar`/restarts fix

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q6.** Construct a 2-D dataset where k-means with the *correct* $k$ returns the wrong clustering for purely geometric reasons (not bad initialization). Name the assumption that breaks and a method that succeeds.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Construction (two concentric rings).** Inner ring of radius 1, outer ring of radius 5, both centered at the origin, $k=2$. The natural clusters are inner vs. outer. But k-means partitions space by **straight perpendicular bisectors** between centroids (a Voronoi tessellation) — it can only produce **convex** regions. No placement of two centroids carves "inner ring vs outer ring"; the optimal-WCSS solution slices the plane into two half-planes, cutting *both* rings in half. So even with perfect initialization, k-means returns left-half vs right-half, not inner vs outer.

(Equivalent constructions: two Gaussians with a 10:1 variance/size ratio — k-means steals points from the large/spread cluster to balance WCSS; or two crescent "moons.")

**Assumption that breaks.** Spherical, equal-variance, convex clusters — i.e. the isotropic-shared-covariance GMM limit (from the lesson's k-means↔GMM derivation). Concentric rings are non-convex and not linearly separable by bisectors.

**Methods that succeed.** **DBSCAN** (density-connectivity follows each ring), or **spectral clustering** (clusters in the eigenspace of a neighborhood affinity graph, which "unfolds" the rings). A full-covariance GMM still fails on rings (they're not Gaussian), so this specifically needs a density/graph method.

**Rubric:**
- Concrete non-convex construction (concentric rings / moons / unequal-variance Gaussians)
- Voronoi/straight-bisector argument ⇒ only convex partitions ⇒ fails even with good init
- Names the spherical/convex assumption (k-means↔GMM limit)
- DBSCAN / spectral succeeds (and why GMM also fails on rings)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q7.** Given points and current centroids, (a) perform one k-means++ seeding step — compute each point's $D(x)^2$ and the sampling probabilities; (b) explain why $D^2$ weighting (not uniform) gives the $O(\log k)$ guarantee.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**(a) The $D^2$ step.** After choosing some centers, for every point $x$ compute $D(x)=\min_{\text{chosen }\mu}\lVert x-\mu\rVert$ (distance to the *nearest* already-chosen center). The next center is sampled with probability

$$P(x)=\frac{D(x)^2}{\sum_{x'}D(x')^2}$$

So for a worked set: compute each $D(x)$, square it, and normalize by the sum of squares to get the probabilities. (E.g. with chosen center at the origin and points at distances 1, 2, 4: $D^2=1,4,16$, sum $=21$, probabilities $\tfrac{1}{21},\tfrac{4}{21},\tfrac{16}{21}$ — the far point is $16\times$ likelier to be picked than the near one.)

**(b) Why $D^2$.** Uniform seeding can place two centers in the same dense blob, leaving a far cluster uncovered (a bad local min Lloyd can't escape). $D^2$ weighting makes points *far from all current centers* exponentially more likely to be chosen, so seeds spread out to cover distinct clusters. Arthur & Vassilvitskii prove this yields $\mathbb{E}[J_{\text{seed}}]\le 8(\ln k+2)\,J_{\text{OPT}}$ — $O(\log k)$-competitive *before any Lloyd iteration* — and the $\log k$ factor is tight.

**Rubric:**
- $D(x)=$ min distance to a chosen center; $P(x)\propto D(x)^2$; compute + normalize
- Far points much likelier (the squaring) → seeds spread across clusters
- $D^2$ avoids the uniform-seeding "two centers in one blob" failure
- State the $8(\ln k+2)$ / $O(\log k)$ guarantee

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q8.** Construct a dataset with two clusters of different density where **no single $\varepsilon$** lets DBSCAN recover both. Explain the limitation and how HDBSCAN resolves it.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Construction.** A **dense** cluster (points ~0.1 apart) and a **sparse** cluster (points ~2 apart), well separated. DBSCAN uses one global $(\varepsilon,\text{minPts})$:
- A **small $\varepsilon$** (say 0.3) finds the dense cluster but **shatters the sparse one into noise** (its points have no neighbors within 0.3).
- A **large $\varepsilon$** (say 3) captures the sparse cluster but **merges the dense cluster with anything nearby** (and may bridge the two via stray points).

No single $\varepsilon$ satisfies both densities simultaneously — that's DBSCAN's core limitation: it assumes roughly uniform density.

**HDBSCAN's fix.** HDBSCAN doesn't fix one $\varepsilon$; it builds a hierarchy over *all* density thresholds (via a mutual-reachability minimum spanning tree), then extracts the clusters that are **most stable** across a range of $\varepsilon$. Each cluster is effectively allowed its own density level, so the dense and sparse clusters are recovered together — and it still labels genuine low-density points as noise. Hence "prefer HDBSCAN over hand-tuning DBSCAN's `eps`" when densities vary.

**Rubric:**
- Two-density construction; show small-$\varepsilon$ shatters sparse, large-$\varepsilon$ merges dense
- Root cause: single global $\varepsilon$ assumes uniform density
- HDBSCAN varies $\varepsilon$ / builds a density hierarchy and extracts most-stable clusters
- Each cluster gets its own effective density; noise still labeled

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q9.** Prove that the t-SNE/UMAP objective is invariant under rotation, reflection, and translation of the output embedding. Conclude rigorously why the axes and inter-cluster distances on the plot are not interpretable.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Invariance.** Both objectives depend on the embedding *only through pairwise distances* $\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert$: t-SNE's low-dim affinity $q_{ij}=\frac{(1+\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k\neq l}(1+\lVert y_k-y_l\rVert^2)^{-1}}$ and UMAP's $q_{ij}=(1+a\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert^{2b})^{-1}$ are functions of $\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert$ alone. Any **rigid motion** $y_i\mapsto Ry_i+t$ with $R$ orthogonal ($R^\top R=I$) preserves every pairwise distance: $\lVert (Ry_i+t)-(Ry_j+t)\rVert=\lVert R(y_i-y_j)\rVert=\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert$. So every $q_{ij}$ is unchanged ⇒ the KL (t-SNE) and BCE (UMAP) objectives are unchanged. The optimum is therefore an entire orbit of rotated/reflected/translated embeddings.

**Conclusions.**
- **Axes are meaningless:** since any rotation/reflection is an equally-optimal solution, the horizontal and vertical directions carry no units or interpretation (unlike PCA axes, which are specific eigenvectors).
- **Inter-cluster distances are not metric:** the affinities depend on distance through a *heavy-tailed* (t-SNE Cauchy / UMAP) kernel and a *per-point adaptive bandwidth*, so a given high-dim affinity does not map to a fixed display distance; the optimization is also only sensitive to *local* neighbor structure, leaving large gaps under-determined. Hence printed gaps between clusters are artifacts.
- (Cluster *sizes* are likewise meaningless because the per-point bandwidth normalizes local density away.)

**Rubric:**
- Objective depends only on pairwise $\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert$
- Orthogonal transform + translation preserves all pairwise distances ⇒ objective invariant
- ⇒ axes carry no meaning (whole orbit is optimal)
- ⇒ inter-cluster distances/sizes not metric (heavy tail + adaptive bandwidth + local-only sensitivity); contrast PCA

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q10.** Explain why running t-SNE on isotropic Gaussian *noise* at low perplexity produces apparent clusters. Give the mechanism and the protocol that prevents the misread.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The phenomenon** (Wattenberg et al., *Distill* 2016): t-SNE on data with *no* cluster structure (a single isotropic Gaussian blob) shows distinct "clumps" at small perplexity — pure artifacts.

**Mechanism.** Perplexity sets each point's neighbor bandwidth ≈ its effective number of neighbors. At **low perplexity** (say 2–5), each point's affinity distribution concentrates on a handful of nearest neighbors, so t-SNE only tries to preserve tiny local neighborhoods. The non-convex KL objective then finds a low-cost configuration that packs those small neighborhoods into separated blobs — there's no global constraint forcing the blobs together, and random initialization seeds where they form. The result *looks* clustered, but the "clusters" are just locally-coherent groups the optimizer happened to separate, not structure in the data.

**Protocol to avoid the misread.**
1. **Sweep perplexity** (e.g. 5, 30, 50, 100) — real clusters persist across the range; artifacts shift/dissolve.
2. **Run enough iterations** (under-converged runs show spurious structure) and use **PCA init** + a fixed seed for reproducibility.
3. **Verify any cluster/gap against the original-space data** (e.g. distances, a metric clustering, or known labels) — never report a t-SNE clump as a finding on its own.
4. Treat t-SNE/UMAP as **hypothesis-generating**, not confirmatory.

**Rubric:**
- Noise → fake clusters at low perplexity (the Distill result)
- Mechanism: low perplexity → tiny neighborhoods → non-convex KL packs them into separated blobs, no global constraint
- Protocol: sweep perplexity, more iterations + PCA-init + seed, verify against original data
- Conclude: visualizer is hypothesis-generating only

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q11.** A teammate upgrades scikit-learn 1.3→1.4 and their `KMeans(init='k-means++')` results get noisier with no code change. Diagnose the exact cause and the one-line fix. Also: why does `AgglomerativeClustering(affinity='cosine')` now raise?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**KMeans diagnosis.** `n_init` changed default to **`'auto'`** (deprecation started 1.2, default in 1.4). With `init='k-means++'`, `'auto'` resolves to **`n_init=1`** — a *single* seeding + Lloyd run — whereas the old default was `n_init=10` (ten restarts, keep the best by inertia). Fewer restarts ⇒ more sensitivity to the random seed ⇒ noisier, occasionally-worse clusterings. (With `init='random'` or a callable, `'auto'` resolves to `10`, so only the k-means++ path is affected — a subtle asymmetry.)

**Fix:** set it explicitly — `KMeans(n_clusters=k, init='k-means++', n_init=10)`.

**AgglomerativeClustering.** The parameter `affinity` was **removed in 1.4** (deprecated earlier) and renamed **`metric`**. So `affinity='cosine'` raises; use `metric='cosine'` (and note `linkage='ward'` *only* supports `metric='euclidean'`, so cosine requires a non-Ward linkage like `average`/`complete`).

**Rubric:**
- `n_init='auto'` (default since 1.4) ⇒ `n_init=1` with k-means++ (was 10) → noisier
- The `init='random'`→10 vs `'k-means++'`→1 asymmetry
- Fix: set `n_init=10` explicitly
- `affinity` removed/renamed to `metric` in 1.4 (and Ward needs Euclidean)

</details>

**Q12.** Given two clusterings as a contingency table, compute the Rand index and Adjusted Rand Index, and explain why two clusterings that "look 80% similar" can have ARI near 0. When would you prefer AMI over NMI?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Rand index.** Over all $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs of points, RI $=\frac{a+b}{\binom n2}$ where $a$ = pairs in the *same* cluster in both clusterings, $b$ = pairs in *different* clusters in both. It's the fraction of pairs the two clusterings *agree* on (together-together or apart-apart).

**Adjusted Rand Index.** RI is inflated by chance (random clusterings score well above 0, especially with many clusters). ARI corrects for it:

$$\text{ARI}=\frac{\text{RI}-\mathbb{E}[\text{RI}]}{\max(\text{RI})-\mathbb{E}[\text{RI}]}$$

where $\mathbb{E}[\text{RI}]$ is computed from the contingency-table margins under a fixed-margins null. ARI $=1$ for identical clusterings, $\approx0$ for random, and can go negative. (Compute it from the pair counts $\sum_{ij}\binom{n_{ij}}{2}$ and the margin sums — the standard Hubert–Arabie formula.)

**Why "80% similar" can give ARI ≈ 0.** Most pairs of points are in *different* clusters in both clusterings (the "apart-apart" $b$ term dominates when there are many clusters), so raw RI is high almost automatically — that agreement is mostly *expected by chance*. ARI subtracts that expectation, so a clustering only marginally better than random lands near 0 despite a high RI. Always report the chance-corrected ARI, not RI.

**AMI vs NMI.** NMI (normalized mutual information) is also chance-inflated — it tends to **favor clusterings with more clusters**. **AMI** (adjusted mutual information) subtracts the expected MI under the same null. Prefer **AMI** when the two clusterings have **different numbers of clusters or very unequal cluster sizes**; NMI is acceptable only when cluster counts are comparable.

**Rubric:**
- RI $=(a+b)/\binom n2$ (pair agreement); compute from the table
- ARI = chance-corrected RI (Hubert–Arabie); 1 identical, 0 random, can be negative
- High RI from dominant apart-apart pairs is mostly chance ⇒ ARI ≈ 0 despite "80% similar"
- AMI over NMI when cluster counts/sizes differ (NMI favors more clusters)

</details>

(Two regularity failures: a parameter on the boundary and loss of identifiability under the null.) What do statisticians use instead — and why does this make "just do a hypothesis test for $k$" a trap in a quant loop?
17. **Density as an anomaly score.** Use a fitted GMM's $\log p(x)$ to flag anomalies (the fraud-detection shape). How do you set the threshold, why does a calibrated density beat distance-to-nearest-centroid, and in what dimensionality regime does the whole approach collapse?
18. **Mixtures make fat tails.** Show that a two-component scale mixture of normals (same mean, different variances) has excess kurtosis — a Jensen argument on $\sigma^4$ vs. $(\sigma^2)^2$ suffices. Connect: why do quant researchers reach for regime mixtures before exotic distributions when modeling returns?
19. **GMM vs. HMM for regimes.** A GMM on daily returns treats each day's regime as an independent draw; markets have *persistent* regimes. What does an HMM add, and how does Baum–Welch's E-step differ from the GMM E-step (forward–backward smoothed posteriors vs. pointwise responsibilities)?

## Rapid-fire: embeddings, spectral methods & production clustering

1. **Derive the t-SNE gradient.** Starting from $\text{KL}(P\,\|\,Q)$ with $q_{ij}\propto(1+\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert^2)^{-1}$, derive $\frac{\partial C}{\partial y_i}=4\sum_j(p_{ij}-q_{ij})(1+\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert^2)^{-1}(y_i-y_j)$ and split it into attraction and repulsion. Where does the repulsion come from, given the objective never mentions one? What does early exaggeration (multiplying $p_{ij}$ by ~12 for the first few hundred iterations) do to that balance, and why does it decide the final cluster layout?
2. **The crowding problem, quantitatively.** In $d$ dimensions the volume of a shell at radius $r$ scales like $r^{d-1}$ — compare how many roughly-equidistant "moderate distance" neighbors a point can have in 10-D vs. 2-D. Derive why no 2-D layout can honor all moderate high-dim distances, why symmetric SNE's Gaussian low-dim kernel therefore crushes clusters together, and exactly how the Student-t (Cauchy) tail fixes it.
3. **The out-of-sample problem.** sklearn's `TSNE` has `fit_transform` but no `transform` — explain *why* from the objective (each embedded point is a free parameter; there is no learned map), and what changes in umap-learn's `UMAP.transform` or a parametric t-SNE that makes new-point projection possible.
4. **UMAP's speed is a different objective.** The standard story: UMAP is t-SNE but faster (negative sampling) and better at global structure (a "real" repulsive loss). Challenge both halves: what *effective* objective does negative-sampled SGD actually optimize — it is not UMAP's written fuzzy cross-entropy — and what did matched-initialization experiments (PCA/spectral init for both methods) reveal about the "better global structure" claim?
5. **PCA first, then t-SNE.** Why is reducing to ~50 principal components before t-SNE the standard recipe (noise, distance concentration, cost) — and construct the counter-case: a dataset where the discriminative direction has *small* variance, so PCA-first erases the very structure you wanted to see.
6. **Distance concentration, derived.** For i.i.d. high-dimensional features, derive why the coefficient of variation of pairwise distances shrinks like $1/\sqrt d$. Then trace the damage method by method — the k-means assignment step, DBSCAN's single global $\varepsilon$, the silhouette, nearest-neighbor queries at $d=1000$ — and explain why *learned* embeddings often escape (low intrinsic dimension).
7. **Trust, quantified.** You visualize 10M query embeddings and see topical islands. What *numbers* do you compute before believing them — neighborhood-preservation scores like trustworthiness (it's in `sklearn.manifold`), $k$-NN recall between the spaces, stability across seeds and hyperparameters?
8. **t-SNE coordinates as features.** A teammate feeds 2-D t-SNE coordinates into a downstream classifier. Construct the full case against: no out-of-sample map for serving, non-metric distances, information destroyed by the compression — and the subtle leakage if the embedding was fit on train and test together.
9. **Spectral clustering from first principles.** Define the graph Laplacian $L=D-W$ and prove $f^\top Lf=\frac12\sum_{ij}w_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2$. Show why the multiplicity of eigenvalue $0$ equals the number of connected components, what the leading eigenvectors encode (connectivity, not position — the kernel-PCA connection), why the pipeline *ends with k-means* in eigenvector space, and how the eigengap suggests the number of clusters.
10. **Chaining and linkage pathologies.** Two compact clusters joined by a thin bridge of a dozen points: predict what single, complete, average, and Ward linkage each return, and why. Then: in what precise sense is Ward "agglomerative k-means," why does its objective (minimum WCSS increase) require Euclidean geometry, and which linkages can produce dendrogram *inversions* — a merge lower than an earlier one — breaking the "cut the tree at height $h$" reading?
11. **Is DBSCAN really $k$-free?** "DBSCAN doesn't need $k$" is its standard selling point. Argue that it merely relocates the choice: $(\varepsilon,\text{minPts})$ fixes a density scale, and the cluster count is an *output* of that choice. Then the implementation gotcha: why is DBSCAN's assignment of *border* points dependent on visit order — i.e. not even deterministic?
12. **DBSCAN in high dimensions.** The k-distance elbow plot is the standard way to pick `eps` — walk through it, explain why it's the elbow method wearing a different hat, then explain why concentration flattens the elbow in high dimensions until no `eps` separates "dense" from "sparse."
13. **Stability is not correctness.** Cluster-validation-by-stability says: subsample, re-cluster, and check cross-run agreement (ARI). Explain the appeal, then the known pathology (von Luxburg): why an *underfit* $k$ — even $k=1$ — can be maximally stable. Give a concrete symmetric dataset where stability selection prefers the wrong $k$, and say what normalization or null reference repairs the method.
14. **Cosine vs. Euclidean.** For unit-normalized vectors, derive $\lVert u-v\rVert^2=2-2\cos(u,v)$ and conclude the two rankings coincide. What must change inside k-means to make it "spherical k-means," and when does normalizing away the norm discard real signal (frequency, confidence)?
15. **k-means is the ANN workhorse.** In a Faiss-style IVF index, k-means centroids partition the corpus and `nprobe` controls how many cells you scan — derive the recall/latency trade. Then product quantization: 1536-d float32 is 6144 bytes; 64 sub-vectors, each quantized to one of 256 k-means centroids, is 64 bytes. Reconstruct that 96× figure and name what you lost.
16. **Deduplicate the pretraining corpus.** Exact and near-hash dedup miss paraphrases. Explain the embed-cluster-prune recipe (the SemDeDup shape: k-means on embeddings, drop near-duplicates within clusters), and the threshold trade-off between redundancy and diversity in the surviving data.
17. **Embedding drift in production.** Your retrieval embeddings are fixed but user queries evolve. Design the monitor: why can the population mean embedding stay flat while cluster structure shifts underneath, and what per-cluster statistics (occupancy, centroid displacement, assignment entropy) catch it?
18. **Cold-start via clusters.** A recsys serves brand-new users from user-cluster prototypes. Defend the design, then attack it: what replaces "nearest prototype" as history accrues, and how does serving from clusters create a feedback loop that freezes the clusters in place?
19. **Cluster the correlation matrix.** Hierarchical risk parity (López de Prado 2016) clusters assets with the distance $d_{ij}=\sqrt{(1-\rho_{ij})/2}$. Verify this is a genuine metric, and explain what clustering buys over inverting a noisy sample covariance (conditioning, error amplification) in portfolio construction.
20. **PCA factors or clusters?** The top eigenvector of an equity correlation matrix is "the market"; sector structure shows up in later components *and* in clusters. Ding & He (2004) showed the continuous relaxation of the k-means indicator problem is solved by the top principal components — reconcile that with "PCA and clustering answer different questions," and say when a quant should prefer which.
