## TL;DR

Every regression loss function is **secretly a statement about what the noise in your data looks like**. MSE is MLE under Gaussian noise; MAE is MLE under Laplace noise; Huber is what you reach for when noise is mostly Gaussian but has a heavy tail of outliers. Choosing a loss is choosing a noise model — and the choice changes outlier sensitivity, optimization difficulty, and the very meaning of "best fit."

## What you'll be able to do after this lesson

- Derive that minimizing MSE is equivalent to maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE) under additive Gaussian noise.
- Derive the same equivalence between MAE and Laplace noise.
- Compute the gradient (or subgradient) of each loss, and predict how each will behave on a dataset with 1% gross outliers.
- Define Huber loss, prove that its gradient is continuous at the threshold $\delta$, and pick $\delta$ for a given dataset.
- Recognize quantile (pinball) loss, log-cosh, and ε-insensitive loss, and say *which noise model each corresponds to*.
- Diagnose why "my MAE loss in XGBoost isn't training" and propose a fix.
- Defend a loss choice in an interview by referring to the data's noise structure rather than to muscle memory.

## Why losses come before models

In Chapter 1 of every ML course (and most production code), the first piece of code anyone writes is `model.fit(X, y)`. The choice of *what `.fit` is actually optimizing* — the loss function — usually defaults to whatever the library picked. For a senior interview, that default is the wrong answer twice:

1. **The loss is the spec.** It's the formal statement of what "good predictions" means. Two models with the same architecture trained with different losses are different functions of the data.
2. **The loss encodes assumptions about the noise.** When the noise model is wrong, the "optimal" model is the wrong model — and the failure modes (sensitivity to outliers, biased predictions, training instability) follow directly from the mismatch.

So we treat losses as first-class, *before* picking model families.

> **Vocabulary primer.** A short grounding of words this lesson uses constantly. The "rusty staff engineer" assumption from c1.1 still applies.
>
> - **Loss function** $\mathcal{L}(y, \hat{y})$ — a scalar measure of how wrong a single prediction is. The *training loss* is the average (or sum) of $\mathcal{L}$ over the training set.
> - **Residual** $r_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$ — the signed error on example $i$.
> - **MLE** — Maximum Likelihood Estimation. The classical procedure for fitting model parameters: pick parameters that make the observed data most probable under the model.
> - **Gradient** — the partial derivative of the loss with respect to the model's parameters. Almost every modern optimizer follows the negative gradient downhill.
> - **Subgradient** — a generalization of the gradient that's defined for non-smooth functions (functions with kinks, like $|x|$). At a kink, the subgradient is a *set* of slopes rather than a single value.
> - **Outlier** — an example whose label is far from what the model would predict given its features. Often (not always) due to measurement error or labeling mistake.

## The big idea: every regression loss is a noise model

This single observation organizes the rest of the lesson.

Suppose you assume that the true relationship between features $x$ and label $y$ is

$$y = f^\star(x) + \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim p_\text{noise}$$

where $f^\star$ is the (unknown) true function and $\epsilon$ is a random noise term drawn from some distribution $p_\text{noise}$. If you fit a model $\hat{f}_\theta(x)$ with parameters $\theta$ by maximizing the likelihood of the observed data:

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta \prod_{i=1}^n p_\text{noise}(y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i))$$

then taking the negative log of the product turns it into a sum, and the *form* of the loss you end up minimizing depends entirely on $p_\text{noise}$:

$$-\log \prod_i p_\text{noise}(r_i) = -\sum_i \log p_\text{noise}(r_i)$$

So if $p_\text{noise}$ is Gaussian, $\log p_\text{noise}(r) = -r^2 / (2\sigma^2) + \text{const}$, and you're minimizing $\sum r_i^2$ — that's MSE. If $p_\text{noise}$ is Laplace, $\log p_\text{noise}(r) = -|r|/b + \text{const}$, and you're minimizing $\sum |r_i|$ — that's MAE. **The choice of loss is the choice of noise distribution.** Every regression loss in this lesson is a special case of this template.

> **Stop and think.** If the noise distribution is something else entirely — say, Student-$t$ with $\nu$ degrees of freedom — what loss would MLE prescribe? *Hint:* $-\log p_\nu(r) = \frac{\nu + 1}{2}\log(1 + r^2/\nu) + \text{const}$. This is "robust regression" and we'll meet a numerical cousin (log-cosh) below.

## Mean squared error (MSE)

### Definition

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}(y, \hat{y}) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

You'll also see this written as **squared error** (the per-example version $(y - \hat{y})^2$), **L2 loss**, or **quadratic loss**. The constant $1/n$ doesn't change the location of the minimum; some sources omit it or use $1/(2n)$ to make the gradient cleaner.

### Derivation as MLE under Gaussian noise

Assume $y_i = \hat{f}_\theta(x_i) + \epsilon_i$ with $\epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ i.i.d. Then the likelihood of one example is

$$p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i))^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

The log-likelihood of the dataset:

$$\log L(\theta) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i))^2$$

Maximizing $\log L(\theta)$ over $\theta$ is equivalent to minimizing $\sum_i (y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i))^2$ — exactly the MSE objective. The variance $\sigma^2$ doesn't affect the minimizing $\theta$; it only rescales the loss.

**Conclusion: minimizing MSE = MLE under Gaussian noise of any fixed variance.**

### Gradient

For a single example, $\mathcal{L} = (y - \hat{y})^2$ where $\hat{y} = \hat{f}_\theta(x)$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{y}} = -2(y - \hat{y}) = -2 r$$

(Here $r = y - \hat{y}$ is the residual.) So **the gradient signal scales linearly with the residual.** Bigger errors push harder.

### Outlier sensitivity, quantified

The linear-in-residual gradient is why MSE is fragile against outliers. Suppose you have 999 examples with residual roughly 1 and one example with residual 100 (an outlier). The gradient contribution from the bulk is $999 \times 2 = 1998$. The contribution from the single outlier is $2 \times 100 = 200$ — that single example's gradient is **10% as influential as the entire well-behaved dataset combined.**

Squared loss values it even more: $\sum r_i^2$ on the bulk is $999$; on the outlier alone is $10000$. The single outlier *dominates* the loss numerically.

This isn't a bug; it's the consequence of assuming Gaussian noise. Gaussians have light tails — they assign vanishingly small probability to large residuals — so a large residual is interpreted as "this *can't* be normal noise; my model must be way off." The optimizer dutifully bends the model to reduce that one residual.

### Why MSE has a closed-form solution for linear models

Linear regression stacks the per-example predictions $\hat{y}_i = x_i^\top\theta$ into $\hat{y} = X\theta$, where $X$ is the $n \times p$ design matrix (row $i$ is $x_i^\top$). Differentiate the objective w.r.t. the parameter *vector* $\theta$ in steps — this is where the per-example $-2r$ from above turns into matrices.

**Objective.**

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(y_i - x_i^\top\theta\bigr)^2 = \frac{1}{n}\,\|y - X\theta\|_2^2$$

**One example, by the chain rule.** We showed above that squared error has $\partial \mathcal{L}_i / \partial \hat{y}_i = -2 r_i$ with $r_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$. Since $\hat{y}_i = x_i^\top\theta$ is linear in $\theta$, $\partial \hat{y}_i / \partial \theta = x_i$, so by the chain rule

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_i}{\partial \theta} = \underbrace{(-2 r_i)}_{\partial \mathcal{L}_i / \partial \hat{y}_i} \cdot \underbrace{x_i}_{\partial \hat{y}_i / \partial \theta} = -2\,\bigl(y_i - x_i^\top\theta\bigr)\,x_i$$

**Average over the dataset.**

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n -2\,\bigl(y_i - x_i^\top\theta\bigr)\,x_i = -\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\,\bigl(y_i - x_i^\top\theta\bigr)$$

**Vectorize the sum.** Each feature vector $x_i$ is a column of $X^\top$, so a sum of the form $\sum_i x_i\,r_i$ is exactly the matrix–vector product $X^\top r$ with the residual vector $r = y - X\theta$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\,\bigl(y_i - x_i^\top\theta\bigr) = X^\top (y - X\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = -\frac{2}{n}\,X^\top(y - X\theta)$$

**Set the gradient to zero and solve.**

$$-\frac{2}{n}\,X^\top(y - X\theta) = 0 \implies X^\top X\,\theta = X^\top y \implies \theta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$$

The closed form exists because squaring makes the objective a quadratic in $\theta$, whose gradient is linear. **Linear gradient → linear system → closed form.** No iterative optimizer needed. (The last step needs $X^\top X$ to be invertible — i.e. $X$ has full column rank; perfect collinearity among features breaks it, which is exactly where ridge regression earns its keep.)

### When MSE is the right choice

- Noise is approximately Gaussian (often a defensible default when residuals are due to many small independent effects — central-limit-theorem reasoning).
- Outliers are rare and you want them to pull the model toward them (e.g., you suspect they're real signal, not bad labels).
- You need a closed-form fit (small linear models, online learning with rapid retraining).
- The downstream cost of error is genuinely quadratic. Optimal portfolio variance, Bayesian quadratic loss, etc.

## Mean absolute error (MAE)

### Definition

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MAE}}(y, \hat{y}) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n |y_i - \hat{y}_i|$$

Also called **L1 loss**.

### Derivation as MLE under Laplace noise

The Laplace distribution (also "double-exponential") has density

$$p_\text{Laplace}(r) = \frac{1}{2b} \exp\!\left(-\frac{|r|}{b}\right)$$

The log-likelihood of one example is then $-\log(2b) - |r|/b$. Summing over examples and dropping constants:

$$\log L(\theta) = -\text{const} - \frac{1}{b} \sum_{i=1}^n |y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i)|$$

Maximizing this is equivalent to minimizing $\sum |r_i|$ — the MAE objective.

**Conclusion: minimizing MAE = MLE under Laplace noise.**

Laplace has *heavier tails* than Gaussian — it assigns much more probability to large residuals. So when you assume Laplace noise, you're saying "occasional large errors are normal; don't bend the model to chase them."

### Subgradient

The function $|r|$ is differentiable everywhere except at $r = 0$. Its derivative is the **sign function**:

$$\frac{d|r|}{dr} = \text{sign}(r) = \begin{cases} +1 & r > 0 \\ -1 & r < 0 \\ \text{undefined} & r = 0 \end{cases}$$

At $r = 0$ specifically, $|r|$ has a kink (a corner), and the derivative isn't a single number. The **subgradient** at this kink is the *set* of all slopes of lines that lie below the function and touch it at zero: the interval $[-1, +1]$. Any value in $[-1, +1]$ is a valid subgradient at zero.

For the chain-rule gradient with respect to model parameters $\theta$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta} = -\text{sign}(r) \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{f}_\theta(x)}{\partial \theta}$$

**Key property: the gradient magnitude is constant** (either $+1$ or $-1$ times the model-Jacobian). The gradient doesn't grow with the residual — large residuals exert the same force as small ones. That's the source of MAE's outlier robustness.

### Outlier robustness, quantified

Back to the 999-vs-1 example. With MAE, the gradient contribution from the bulk (residuals $\approx 1$) is $999 \times 1 = 999$. The contribution from the outlier (residual = 100) is just $1 \times 1 = 1$. The outlier is now **0.1% of the gradient signal**, not 10%. 100× less influence than under MSE.

### Why MAE doesn't have a closed-form solution for linear models

The MAE gradient is $-\text{sign}(r) X$ for linear regression. Setting it to zero doesn't give a linear system — `sign` is non-linear. The MAE optimum corresponds to the **conditional median** of $y$ given $X$, and there's no closed-form expression for the conditional-median parameters of a linear model.

You instead use iterative methods: linear programming, IRLS (iteratively reweighted least squares), or subgradient descent. Tractable but slower than MSE's matrix inverse.

### Why MAE is the median, MSE is the mean

A useful intuition: if you have to predict a single number $\hat{y}$ for a distribution of true labels $Y$:

- The value that minimizes $E[(Y - \hat{y})^2]$ is $\hat{y} = E[Y]$ — the **mean**.
- The value that minimizes $E[|Y - \hat{y}|]$ is $\hat{y} = \text{median}(Y)$ — the **median**.

This is why MSE-trained regression models predict conditional means and MAE-trained regression models predict conditional medians. In a skewed distribution (income, claim sizes, latency), these can be very different — and the right choice depends on what you're trying to measure.

### When MAE is the right choice

- Noise is heavy-tailed (Laplace-ish) or includes occasional gross errors.
- You're predicting a quantity whose median is more meaningful than its mean (latency p50, median home price).
- Labels are noisy or contain mistakes — MAE won't over-react.
- Downstream cost is roughly linear in error.

## Huber loss

### Motivation

You have noise that's *mostly* Gaussian but has a fat tail of gross outliers — e.g. sensor readings that are usually accurate but occasionally garbled. MSE will be pulled around by the outliers; MAE will give up the efficiency of squared-loss optimization in the bulk. **Huber is the hybrid: quadratic in the middle, linear in the tails.**

Formally, with threshold $\delta > 0$:

$$\mathcal{L}_\delta(r) = \begin{cases} \tfrac{1}{2} r^2 & |r| \le \delta \\ \delta\bigl(|r| - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta\bigr) & |r| > \delta \end{cases}$$

### Properties

**Continuity at the threshold.** At $r = \delta$:

- Quadratic branch: $\tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2$.
- Linear branch: $\delta(\delta - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta) = \tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2$.

Equal. The function joins smoothly at $\pm\delta$.

**Gradient continuity at the threshold.** Differentiating each branch w.r.t. $r$:

- Quadratic: $\dfrac{d}{dr}(\tfrac{1}{2} r^2) = r$.
- Linear (for $r > \delta$): $\dfrac{d}{dr}(\delta r - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2) = \delta$.

At $r = \delta$, both branches give a derivative of $\delta$. The gradient is continuous — no kink, no jump, no subgradient pathology. Same on the negative side at $r = -\delta$. This is why Huber is *the* loss for gradient-based optimizers that don't want to deal with `sign(0)`.

**Behavior far from / near zero.**

- $|r| \ll \delta$: behaves like MSE/2. Quadratic. Sensitive to gradient signal scaling with residual size.
- $|r| \gg \delta$: behaves like $\delta \cdot |r|$. Linear. Outlier-robust.

### Choosing $\delta$

The standard rule of thumb: pick $\delta$ to be the $\approx$95th percentile of "honest" residuals — so that 95% of residuals get squared treatment and the heavy-tailed 5% gets linear treatment. In practice:

1. Fit the model with MSE for a quick first pass.
2. Look at the residual distribution. Estimate the noise scale $\hat{\sigma}$ from the median absolute deviation (MAD) or the standard deviation of the bulk.
3. Set $\delta \approx 1.345 \hat{\sigma}$ (the value that achieves 95% asymptotic efficiency under pure Gaussian noise, from Huber's original paper).
4. Refit with Huber and the chosen $\delta$.

If $\delta$ is too small, Huber approaches MAE — you lose the smooth-quadratic efficiency on the bulk. If $\delta$ is too large, Huber approaches MSE — outliers regain their grip. The sweet spot is data-dependent.

### When Huber is the right choice

- Noise is dominated by a Gaussian bulk but you have a known minority of outliers (sensor errors, fraud anomalies in regression labels).
- You want a smooth gradient everywhere (for gradient boosting or neural nets).
- You're willing to tune one extra hyperparameter ($\delta$).

## Beyond the basics

Three losses you should recognize even if you don't use them daily.

### Quantile (pinball) loss

For a quantile level $q \in (0, 1)$:

$$\rho_q(r) = \begin{cases} q \cdot r & r \ge 0 \\ (q - 1) \cdot r & r < 0 \end{cases}$$

This is the loss that, minimized, gives a regression model predicting the **$q$-th quantile** of $Y \mid X$ rather than the mean or median. Useful when:

- You need *prediction intervals* — fit one model at $q = 0.05$ and another at $q = 0.95$ to get a 90% interval per row.
- The distribution is skewed and you want a specific quantile (e.g., p99 latency).
- Asymmetric error costs — under-predicting demand may be 3× worse than over-predicting; quantile loss handles this naturally.

**Special case $q = 0.5$:** $\rho_{0.5}(r) = 0.5 |r|$ — half of MAE. So minimizing pinball at $q = 0.5$ is equivalent to minimizing MAE, and you recover the median predictor.

### log-cosh

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{logcosh}}(r) = \log(\cosh(r))$$

Recall $\cosh(r) = (e^r + e^{-r})/2$. Behavior:

- Near zero: Taylor expansion gives $\log(\cosh(r)) \approx r^2/2 - r^4/12 + \ldots$ — quadratic like MSE.
- Far from zero: $\log(\cosh(r)) \to |r| - \log 2$ — linear like MAE.

So log-cosh is a *smooth*, *everywhere-differentiable* hybrid of MSE and MAE. It has no kinks (unlike Huber's analytical join) and no threshold hyperparameter. Cost: slightly more compute per gradient step.

### ε-insensitive (Vapnik) loss

Used in support vector regression (SVR):

$$\mathcal{L}_\epsilon(r) = \max(0, |r| - \epsilon)$$

Predictions within $\epsilon$ of the true label contribute *zero* loss. Beyond that, the loss is linear in the residual. Encourages a model that "fits the tube" rather than the exact line. Used when you want a sparse-support, robust regression and don't care about residuals below a noise threshold.

## Tradeoff matrix

| Loss | Noise model | Predicts | Outlier sensitivity | Differentiable everywhere | Closed-form linear fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MSE | Gaussian | Conditional mean | High | Yes | Yes |
| MAE | Laplace | Conditional median | Low | No (kink at 0) | No |
| Huber | Gaussian + heavy tail | Mix (depends on $\delta$) | Low for $\|r\| > \delta$ | Yes | No |
| log-cosh | Smooth hybrid | Mix | Low | Yes | No |
| Pinball ($q$) | Asymmetric Laplace | Conditional $q$-quantile | Low | No (kink at 0) | No |
| ε-insensitive | "Don't care" tube | Tube center | Low | No (kinks at $\pm \epsilon$) | No (SVR has its own optimizer) |

## Worked decision walk-throughs

**Scenario A: predicting house sale prices.** Labels in the long upper tail (mansions, luxury sales) skew the distribution. **MSE** would chase those mansions and inflate predictions on ordinary homes. **MAE** predicts the median sale price — much closer to what you actually want for typical valuations. Most production models in this domain use MAE or quantile loss (for confidence intervals).

**Scenario B: forecasting daily ad revenue, $\sigma \approx \$10\text{k}$ per day under normal conditions; once a month there's a $\$200\text{k}$ promotional spike.** Promotional spikes are real, not measurement errors — you don't want to entirely ignore them. **MSE** lets them dominate and biases the model. **MAE** treats them like normal residuals — under-fits them. **Huber** with $\delta \approx \$15\text{k}$: squared treatment on normal days, linear treatment on promotional days. The model fits the bulk well and tolerates spikes without chasing them.

**Scenario C: tabular regression with XGBoost, you want MAE for outlier robustness.** XGBoost's tree-fitting uses a second-order (Newton) step, and the leaf-value formula $v^\star = -\sum g_i / (\sum h_i + \lambda)$ divides by the summed Hessian. MAE's Hessian is zero everywhere, so a *naive* MAE objective (gradient $\pm 1$, Hessian $0$) gives the optimizer nothing to size its steps with — the denominator collapses to the regularizer $\lambda$ and leaf values are junk. Two fixes: (1) **modern XGBoost ($\ge$ 1.7) ships native `reg:absoluteerror`**, which sidesteps the problem with *adaptive leaf values* — it grows the tree structure with a placeholder Hessian, then overwrites each leaf with the **median** of its residuals (exactly Friedman's classic LAD-TreeBoost). (2) **pseudo-Huber loss**, $\mathcal{L}(r) = \delta^2\bigl(\sqrt{1 + (r/\delta)^2} - 1\bigr)$ — a smooth Huber approximation with a strictly positive Hessian, shipped as `reg:pseudohubererror`.

**Scenario D: latency prediction, you care about p99 not p50.** Use **pinball loss at $q = 0.99$**. Reports the conditional 99th-percentile latency, not the median.

## Production failure modes

1. **MSE on rare-but-real spikes.** You're predicting daily revenue. The Black Friday outlier dominates training, your weekday predictions become inflated, weekday MAPE doubles.
2. **MAE chosen for "robustness," then a custom XGBoost objective won't learn.** A hand-rolled MAE objective returns Hessian $\equiv 0$, so the Newton leaf-value step has nothing to divide by. Fix: use the native `reg:absoluteerror` (adaptive leaf values, $\ge$ XGBoost 1.7) or pseudo-Huber.
3. **Huber with the wrong $\delta$.** $\delta$ chosen by intuition (e.g., $\delta = 1.0$) on a dataset where the residual scale is $\hat{\sigma} = 50$. Effectively pure MAE. The bulk of residuals never gets quadratic treatment, optimization is slower and less efficient than needed.
4. **Squared-error metric reported, MAE optimized.** Common when teams switch losses mid-project but keep reporting RMSE for legacy reasons. Reported metric stops corresponding to the model objective.
5. **Predicting medians, downstream system expects means.** A pricing pipeline trained with MAE produces median price predictions; an aggregator multiplies by volume to estimate revenue, which is a mean operation. Result: systematically biased revenue estimates because $\sum \text{median}_i \ne \text{median}\sum$.

## Interview gotchas

- "Which loss should I use for regression?" — interviewers want you to answer "what does the noise look like?" not "MSE because it's the default."
- "Why is MAE more robust than MSE?" — they want the *gradient* explanation (constant vs linear-in-residual) plus the *probabilistic* one (heavier-tailed noise model).
- "How would you give me a prediction interval, not a point prediction?" — quantile loss at $q = 0.05$ and $q = 0.95$. Bonus: discuss calibration of the interval.
- "Your custom XGBoost MAE objective isn't training — what's wrong?" — the Hessian is zero, so the Newton leaf-value step has nothing to divide by. Use native `reg:absoluteerror` (adaptive leaf values) or pseudo-Huber. Being able to explain *why* MAE has zero Hessian on the spot (because $\partial^2 |r| / \partial r^2 = 0$ everywhere except the kink) — and that modern XGBoost works around it by recomputing leaf values as residual medians — is the differentiator.
- "When would you actively choose MSE despite outliers being present?" — when the outliers are real signal (e.g., financial returns: a 10% day matters disproportionately and you *want* the model to fit it).
