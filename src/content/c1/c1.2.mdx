## TL;DR

Every regression loss function is **secretly a statement about what the noise in your data looks like**. Pick a loss and you have picked a probability distribution for your errors, whether you meant to or not. MSE is MLE under Gaussian noise; MAE is MLE under Laplace noise; Huber is what you reach for when noise is mostly Gaussian but has a heavy tail of outliers. Choosing a loss is choosing a noise model — and the choice changes outlier sensitivity, optimization difficulty, and the very meaning of "best fit."

A classification loss is the **same maximum-likelihood story as regression**, specialized to discrete labels. **Cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood of a Categorical/Bernoulli model**, and minimizing it is minimizing the KL divergence from your prediction to the label distribution. Everything else — focal loss, class-balanced weighting, label smoothing — is a *deliberate distortion* of that objective to fix imbalance or miscalibrated confidence. Every distortion has a price, and the price is almost always paid in **calibration**: whether the number your model calls "0.9" actually happens 90% of the time.

## What you'll be able to do after this lesson

- Derive that minimizing MSE is equivalent to maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE) under additive Gaussian noise.
- Derive the same equivalence between MAE and Laplace noise.
- Compute the gradient (or subgradient) of each loss, and predict how each will behave on a dataset with 1% gross outliers.
- Define Huber loss, prove that its gradient is continuous at the threshold $\delta$, and pick $\delta$ for a given dataset.
- Recognize quantile (pinball) loss, log-cosh, and ε-insensitive loss, and say *which noise model each corresponds to*.
- Diagnose why "my MAE loss in XGBoost isn't training" and propose a fix.
- Defend a loss choice in an interview by referring to the data's noise structure rather than to muscle memory.

- Derive that cross-entropy is MLE under a Categorical/Bernoulli model, and that $\text{CE} = H(p) + \text{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$.
- Derive the clean softmax–cross-entropy gradient $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial z = q - y$, and explain why that cancellation is *the* reason CE pairs with softmax.
- Give two independent arguments for why CE beats MSE on classification.
- Define focal loss and class-balanced loss, and compute *exactly* how hard focal down-weights an easy example.
- Reason about calibration like a statistician: proper scoring rules, ECE, the Brier decomposition, temperature scaling — and why modern nets are overconfident.
- Defend a loss choice for an imbalanced problem, and recognize when the loss is the *wrong* lever (decoupling, logit adjustment).

## Why losses come before models

Open any notebook. In Chapter 1 of every ML course (and most production code), the first piece of code anyone writes is `model.fit(X, y)`. Nobody stops to ask what `.fit` is minimizing. The choice of *what `.fit` is actually optimizing* — the loss function — usually defaults to whatever the library picked. That default is the wrong answer twice:

1. **The loss is the spec.** It's the formal statement of what "good predictions" means. Two models with the same architecture trained with different losses are different functions of the data.
2. **The loss encodes assumptions about the noise.** When the noise model is wrong, the "optimal" model is the wrong model — and the failure modes (sensitivity to outliers, biased predictions, training instability) follow directly from the mismatch.

Point 2 is the one that surprises people, and everything below elaborates it. Losses come first here, *before* model families.

Before any of that, a short glossary of the words the rest of the lesson leans on.

> **Vocabulary primer.** A short grounding of words this lesson uses constantly.
>
> - **Loss function** $\mathcal{L}(y, \hat{y})$ — a scalar measure of how wrong a single prediction is. The *training loss* is the average (or sum) of $\mathcal{L}$ over the training set.
> - **Residual** $r_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$ — the signed error on example $i$.
> - **MLE** — Maximum Likelihood Estimation. The classical procedure for fitting model parameters: pick parameters that make the observed data most probable under the model.
> - **Gradient** — the partial derivative of the loss with respect to the model's parameters. Almost every modern optimizer follows the negative gradient downhill.
> - **Subgradient** — a generalization of the gradient that's defined for non-smooth functions (functions with kinks, like $|x|$). At a kink, the subgradient is a *set* of slopes rather than a single value.
> - **Jacobian** — for a function with vector output (e.g. a model's prediction as a function of its parameters $\theta$), the matrix of all first partial derivatives: entry $(i,j)$ is $\partial(\text{output}_i)/\partial\theta_j$. When the output is a single scalar it collapses to one row — the gradient. The "model-Jacobian" $\partial\hat{y}/\partial\theta$ below is just *how the prediction moves as you nudge each parameter*.
> - **Hessian** — the matrix of *second* partial derivatives of a scalar function, $H_{jk} = \partial^2\mathcal{L}/(\partial\theta_j\,\partial\theta_k)$. It measures **curvature**: how fast the gradient itself changes. Second-order optimizers (Newton's method, and XGBoost's leaf-value step) divide the gradient by the Hessian to size each step — which is exactly why a loss whose Hessian is zero, like MAE, breaks a naive Newton update.
> - **Outlier** — an example whose label is far from what the model would predict given its features. Often (not always) due to measurement error or labeling mistake.

## The big idea: every regression loss is a noise model

This single observation organizes the rest of the lesson. Read it once now; every section below is a special case of it.

Suppose you assume that the true relationship between features $x$ and label $y$ is

$$y = f^\star(x) + \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim p_\text{noise}$$

Read that out loud: the label you observe is the true function's value plus a random kick. Here $f^\star$ is the (unknown) true function, $\epsilon$ is a random noise term, and $p_\text{noise}$ is the distribution that kick is drawn from. Write the training set as the $n$ pairs $(x_i, y_i)$ with the index $i$ running from 1 to $n$. If you fit a model $\hat{f}_\theta(x)$ with parameters $\theta$ by maximizing the likelihood of the observed data:

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta \prod_{i=1}^n p_\text{noise}(y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i))$$

In English: find the parameters that make the residuals you actually observed the most probable set of noise kicks. Each factor in the product is "how likely was a kick of exactly this size," and the product runs over every training row.

Products of $n$ small numbers are miserable to work with, so take the negative log. The log turns the product into a sum, and the *form* of the loss you end up minimizing depends entirely on $p_\text{noise}$. Write $r_i = y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i)$ for the residual on example $i$:

$$-\log \prod_i p_\text{noise}(r_i) = -\sum_i \log p_\text{noise}(r_i)$$

That right-hand side is a per-example penalty summed over the dataset. That is exactly the shape of a training loss. So the loss you write down is nothing more than $-\log$ of a noise density, and different densities give different penalties.

Now turn the crank twice. If $p_\text{noise}$ is Gaussian, $\log p_\text{noise}(r) = -r^2 / (2\sigma^2) + \text{const}$, and you're minimizing $\sum r_i^2$ — that's MSE. If $p_\text{noise}$ is Laplace, $\log p_\text{noise}(r) = -|r|/b + \text{const}$, and you're minimizing $\sum |r_i|$ — that's MAE. **The choice of loss is the choice of noise distribution.** Every regression loss in this lesson is a special case of this template.

> **Stop and think.** If the noise distribution is something else entirely — say, Student-$t$ with $\nu$ degrees of freedom — what loss would MLE prescribe? *Hint:* $-\log p_\nu(r) = \frac{\nu + 1}{2}\log(1 + r^2/\nu) + \text{const}$. This is "robust regression" and we'll meet a numerical cousin (log-cosh) below.

## Mean squared error (MSE)

### Definition

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}(y, \hat{y}) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

Square every error, average the squares. That's the whole formula.

You'll also see this written as **squared error** (the per-example version $(y - \hat{y})^2$), **L2 loss**, or **quadratic loss**. The constant $1/n$ doesn't change the location of the minimum; some sources omit it or use $1/(2n)$ to make the gradient cleaner.

### Derivation as MLE under Gaussian noise

Now run the template from the previous section with a specific density plugged in.

Assume $y_i = \hat{f}_\theta(x_i) + \epsilon_i$ with $\epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ i.i.d. Then the likelihood of one example is

$$p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i))^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

Everything in front of the exponential is a constant that doesn't involve $\theta$. Everything inside the exponential is the squared residual, scaled.

Take the log of each factor and add them up. The log-likelihood of the dataset:

$$\log L(\theta) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i))^2$$

Read the two terms. The first has no $\theta$ in it at all, so it's dead weight for optimization. The second is a negative constant times the sum of squared residuals.

Maximizing $\log L(\theta)$ over $\theta$ is equivalent to minimizing $\sum_i (y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i))^2$ — exactly the MSE objective. The variance $\sigma^2$ doesn't affect the minimizing $\theta$; it only rescales the loss.

**Conclusion: minimizing MSE = MLE under Gaussian noise of any fixed variance.**

### Gradient

The optimizer never sees the loss value. It sees the derivative. So compute it.

For a single example, $\mathcal{L} = (y - \hat{y})^2$ where $\hat{y} = \hat{f}_\theta(x)$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{y}} = -2(y - \hat{y}) = -2 r$$

(Here $r = y - \hat{y}$ is the residual.) In words: the push this example applies is proportional to how wrong it is. So **the gradient signal scales linearly with the residual.** Bigger errors push harder. Double the error and you double the shove.

That sounds reasonable, right up until one row is wrong by a factor of a hundred.

### Outlier sensitivity, quantified

Take 1000 rows. On 999 of them your model is doing fine, with residuals of roughly 1. On one row, a sensor glitched and the label came back with a residual of 100.

Add up the gradient. From the 999 well-behaved rows you get $999 \times 2 = 1998$. From the single glitched row you get $2 \times 100 = 200$. So one row out of a thousand supplies 200 units of pull against the other 999 rows' 1998 — that single example's gradient is **10% as influential as the entire well-behaved dataset combined.**

The linear-in-residual gradient is why MSE is fragile against outliers. Now do the same accounting on the loss value rather than the gradient, and it looks worse. Squared loss values it even more: $\sum r_i^2$ on the bulk is $999$; on the outlier alone is $10000$. The single outlier *dominates* the loss numerically. By the number MSE is reporting, your model's entire job is that one row.

This isn't a bug; it's the consequence of assuming Gaussian noise. Gaussians have light tails — they assign vanishingly small probability to large residuals — so a large residual is interpreted as "this *can't* be normal noise; my model must be way off." The optimizer dutifully bends the model to reduce that one residual. The loss is behaving exactly as specified. The specification was wrong.

### Why MSE has a closed-form solution for linear models

MSE buys you something real in exchange for that fragility, and this is it. For a linear model you never have to run an optimizer at all. Here is where that comes from.

Linear regression stacks the per-example predictions $\hat{y}_i = x_i^\top\theta$ into $\hat{y} = X\theta$ and the labels into a vector $y$, where $X$ is the $n \times p$ design matrix — one row per training example and one column per feature, so $p$ is the number of features and row $i$ is $x_i^\top$. Differentiate the objective w.r.t. the parameter *vector* $\theta$ in steps — this is where the per-example $-2r$ from above turns into matrices.

**Objective.**

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(y_i - x_i^\top\theta\bigr)^2 = \frac{1}{n}\,\|y - X\theta\|_2^2$$

Average squared residual, written first as a sum and then as a squared vector norm. Same object twice.

**One example, by the chain rule.** We showed above that squared error has $\partial \mathcal{L}_i / \partial \hat{y}_i = -2 r_i$ with $r_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$. Since $\hat{y}_i = x_i^\top\theta$ is linear in $\theta$, $\partial \hat{y}_i / \partial \theta = x_i$, so by the chain rule

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_i}{\partial \theta} = \underbrace{(-2 r_i)}_{\partial \mathcal{L}_i / \partial \hat{y}_i} \cdot \underbrace{x_i}_{\partial \hat{y}_i / \partial \theta} = -2\,\bigl(y_i - x_i^\top\theta\bigr)\,x_i$$

Read the two braces: how much the loss moves when the prediction moves, times how much the prediction moves when the parameters move. The second factor is $x_i$ because for a linear model the feature vector *is* the model-Jacobian.

**Average over the dataset.**

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n -2\,\bigl(y_i - x_i^\top\theta\bigr)\,x_i = -\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\,\bigl(y_i - x_i^\top\theta\bigr)$$

Nothing happened there except moving the constant out front and swapping the order of a scalar and a vector.

**Vectorize the sum.** Each feature vector $x_i$ is a column of $X^\top$, so a sum of the form $\sum_i x_i\,r_i$ is exactly the matrix–vector product $X^\top r$ with the residual vector $r = y - X\theta$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\,\bigl(y_i - x_i^\top\theta\bigr) = X^\top (y - X\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = -\frac{2}{n}\,X^\top(y - X\theta)$$

That step is worth pausing on: "sum over examples of (feature vector times its residual)" and "matrix times residual vector" are the same arithmetic, written two ways.

**Set the gradient to zero and solve.**

$$-\frac{2}{n}\,X^\top(y - X\theta) = 0 \implies X^\top X\,\theta = X^\top y \implies \theta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$$

The closed form exists because squaring makes the objective a quadratic in $\theta$, whose gradient is linear. **Linear gradient → linear system → closed form.** No iterative optimizer needed. (The last step needs $X^\top X$ to be invertible — i.e. $X$ has full column rank; perfect collinearity among features breaks it, which is exactly where ridge regression earns its keep.)

That chain of reasoning has exactly one fragile link. Under MAE it breaks, and everything downstream of it falls apart.

### When MSE is the right choice

- Noise is approximately Gaussian (often a defensible default when residuals are due to many small independent effects — central-limit-theorem reasoning).
- Outliers are rare and you want them to pull the model toward them (e.g., you suspect they're real signal, not bad labels).
- You need a closed-form fit (small linear models, online learning with rapid retraining).
- The downstream cost of error is genuinely quadratic. Optimal portfolio variance, Bayesian quadratic loss, etc.

## Mean absolute error (MAE)

### Definition

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MAE}}(y, \hat{y}) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n |y_i - \hat{y}_i|$$

Take the size of every error, ignore the sign, average them. One change from MSE: absolute value instead of squaring. That one change moves everything.

Also called **L1 loss**.

### Derivation as MLE under Laplace noise

Same template as before. Swap the density.

The Laplace distribution (also "double-exponential") has density

$$p_\text{Laplace}(r) = \frac{1}{2b} \exp\!\left(-\frac{|r|}{b}\right)$$

Note what sits inside the exponential: $|r|$, not $r^2$. A Gaussian's log-density falls off like a parabola; a Laplace's falls off like a V.

The log-likelihood of one example is then $-\log(2b) - |r|/b$. Summing over examples and dropping constants:

$$\log L(\theta) = -\text{const} - \frac{1}{b} \sum_{i=1}^n |y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i)|$$

Again the first term has no $\theta$ in it, and the second is a negative constant times the sum of absolute residuals.

Maximizing this is equivalent to minimizing $\sum |r_i|$ — the MAE objective.

**Conclusion: minimizing MAE = MLE under Laplace noise.**

Laplace has *heavier tails* than Gaussian — it assigns much more probability to large residuals. So when you assume Laplace noise, you're saying "occasional large errors are normal; don't bend the model to chase them." A residual of 100 is not treated as evidence that the model is broken. It is treated as an ordinary day.

### Subgradient

The function $|r|$ is differentiable everywhere except at $r = 0$. Its derivative is the **sign function**:

$$\frac{d|r|}{dr} = \text{sign}(r) = \begin{cases} +1 & r > 0 \\ -1 & r < 0 \\ \text{undefined} & r = 0 \end{cases}$$

The slope is $+1$ on the right arm of the V and $-1$ on the left arm. It never depends on how far out along the arm you are.

At $r = 0$ specifically, $|r|$ has a kink (a corner), and the derivative isn't a single number. The **subgradient** at this kink is the *set* of all slopes of lines that lie below the function and touch it at zero: the interval $[-1, +1]$. Any value in $[-1, +1]$ is a valid subgradient at zero. Picture resting a straightedge in the notch of the V: you can tilt it through a whole range of angles and it still touches only at the bottom.

For the chain-rule gradient with respect to model parameters $\theta$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta} = -\text{sign}(r) \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{f}_\theta(x)}{\partial \theta}$$

Read it: which direction the residual points, times how the prediction responds to each parameter. The size of the residual never enters.

**Key property: the gradient magnitude is constant** (either $+1$ or $-1$ times the model-Jacobian). The gradient doesn't grow with the residual — large residuals exert the same force as small ones. That's the source of MAE's outlier robustness. Under MSE every example shouts in proportion to how wrong it is. Under MAE every example gets one vote.

### Outlier robustness, quantified

Back to the 999-vs-1 example. With MAE, the gradient contribution from the bulk (residuals $\approx 1$) is $999 \times 1 = 999$. The contribution from the outlier (residual = 100) is just $1 \times 1 = 1$. The outlier is now **0.1% of the gradient signal**, not 10%. 100× less influence than under MSE.

Same data, same model, same glitched sensor. The only thing that changed was the assumption about what noise looks like.

### Why MAE doesn't have a closed-form solution for linear models

This is the link that breaks. Trace the MSE derivation again and watch where it dies.

The MAE gradient is $-\text{sign}(r) X$ for linear regression, with $\text{sign}$ applied entry by entry to the residual vector $r$. Setting it to zero doesn't give a linear system — `sign` is non-linear. There is no matrix to invert, because there is no linear system to invert it in. The MAE optimum corresponds to the **conditional median** of $y$ given $X$, and there's no closed-form expression for the conditional-median parameters of a linear model.

You instead use iterative methods: linear programming, IRLS (iteratively reweighted least squares), or subgradient descent. Tractable but slower than MSE's matrix inverse. That is the bill for robustness.

### Why MAE is the median, MSE is the mean

A useful intuition: if you have to predict a single number $\hat{y}$ for a distribution of true labels $Y$:

- The value that minimizes $E[(Y - \hat{y})^2]$ is $\hat{y} = E[Y]$ — the **mean**.
- The value that minimizes $E[|Y - \hat{y}|]$ is $\hat{y} = \text{median}(Y)$ — the **median**.

This is why MSE-trained regression models predict conditional means and MAE-trained regression models predict conditional medians. In a skewed distribution (income, claim sizes, latency), these can be very different — and the right choice depends on what you're trying to measure.

Read that back as a sentence about the world. If you train on house prices with MSE, the number your model prints is its guess at the *average* price of a house like this one. Train with MAE and the same model prints its guess at the *typical* price of a house like this one. Those are different questions, and only one of them is the one your product asked.

### When MAE is the right choice

- Noise is heavy-tailed (Laplace-ish) or includes occasional gross errors.
- You're predicting a quantity whose median is more meaningful than its mean (latency p50, median home price).
- Labels are noisy or contain mistakes — MAE won't over-react.
- Downstream cost is roughly linear in error.

## Huber loss

### Motivation

Here is the case neither loss handles. You have noise that's *mostly* Gaussian but has a fat tail of gross outliers — e.g. sensor readings that are usually accurate but occasionally garbled.

Try MSE: it will be pulled around by the outliers, for exactly the reasons in the 999-vs-1 count above. Try MAE: it ignores the outliers, but it also throws away the smooth quadratic behavior on the 999 rows where the Gaussian assumption was fine. One loss over-reacts to the tail; the other gives up the efficiency of squared-loss optimization in the bulk. Neither one is describing your data, because your data is two things at once.

So describe it as two things. **Huber is the hybrid: quadratic in the middle, linear in the tails.**

Formally, with threshold $\delta > 0$:

$$\mathcal{L}_\delta(r) = \begin{cases} \tfrac{1}{2} r^2 & |r| \le \delta \\ \delta\bigl(|r| - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta\bigr) & |r| > \delta \end{cases}$$

In words: if the residual is small enough to be plausible noise, square it. If it's bigger than $\delta$, stop squaring and charge a flat rate per unit of error. $\delta$ is the line between "ordinary noise" and "something went wrong."

### Properties

Splicing two functions together is easy. Splicing them together *without introducing a seam* is the part worth checking.

**Continuity at the threshold.** At $r = \delta$:

- Quadratic branch: $\tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2$.
- Linear branch: $\delta(\delta - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta) = \tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2$.

Equal. The function joins smoothly at $\pm\delta$. That $-\tfrac{1}{2}\delta$ inside the linear branch exists for precisely this reason: it's the offset that makes the two pieces meet.

**Gradient continuity at the threshold.** Values matching isn't enough. The optimizer reads slopes, so the slopes have to match too. Differentiating each branch w.r.t. $r$:

- Quadratic: $\dfrac{d}{dr}(\tfrac{1}{2} r^2) = r$.
- Linear (for $r > \delta$): $\dfrac{d}{dr}(\delta r - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2) = \delta$.

At $r = \delta$, both branches give a derivative of $\delta$. The gradient is continuous — no kink, no jump, no subgradient pathology. Same on the negative side at $r = -\delta$. This is why Huber is *the* loss for gradient-based optimizers that don't want to deal with `sign(0)`.

**Behavior far from / near zero.** The two regimes read straight off the definition.

- $|r| \ll \delta$: behaves like MSE/2. Quadratic. Sensitive to gradient signal scaling with residual size.
- $|r| \gg \delta$: behaves like $\delta \cdot |r|$. Linear. Outlier-robust.

So $\delta$ is a dial between the two losses you already know, which leaves only the question of where to set it.

### Choosing $\delta$

The standard rule of thumb: pick $\delta$ to be the $\approx$95th percentile of "honest" residuals — so that 95% of residuals get squared treatment and the heavy-tailed 5% gets linear treatment. In practice:

1. Fit the model with MSE for a quick first pass.
2. Look at the residual distribution. Estimate the noise scale $\hat{\sigma}$ from the median absolute deviation (MAD) or the standard deviation of the bulk.
3. Set $\delta \approx 1.345 \hat{\sigma}$ (the value that achieves 95% asymptotic efficiency under pure Gaussian noise, from Huber's original paper).
4. Refit with Huber and the chosen $\delta$.

The number 1.345 isn't folklore; it's the constant that costs you only 5% efficiency in the case where the data really was pure Gaussian and you didn't need robustness at all. That's the insurance premium.

Now push the dial to both ends. If $\delta$ is too small, Huber approaches MAE — you lose the smooth-quadratic efficiency on the bulk. If $\delta$ is too large, Huber approaches MSE — outliers regain their grip. The sweet spot is data-dependent.

### When Huber is the right choice

- Noise is dominated by a Gaussian bulk but you have a known minority of outliers (sensor errors, fraud anomalies in regression labels).
- You want a smooth gradient everywhere (for gradient boosting or neural nets).
- You're willing to tune one extra hyperparameter ($\delta$).

## Beyond the basics

Three losses you should recognize even if you don't use them daily. Each one is still the same template: write down a penalty, and it implies a noise model.

### Quantile (pinball) loss

For a quantile level $q \in (0, 1)$:

$$\rho_q(r) = \begin{cases} q \cdot r & r \ge 0 \\ (q - 1) \cdot r & r < 0 \end{cases}$$

Both branches are positive (on the lower branch, $r$ is negative and $q-1$ is negative). The trick is that the two arms of the V have *different* slopes. Under-predicting costs $q$ per unit; over-predicting costs $1-q$ per unit. Set $q = 0.9$ and under-prediction is nine times as expensive, so the model climbs until only 10% of labels sit above it.

This is the loss that, minimized, gives a regression model predicting the **$q$-th quantile** of $Y \mid X$ rather than the mean or median. Useful when:

- You need *prediction intervals* — fit one model at $q = 0.05$ and another at $q = 0.95$ to get a 90% interval per row.
- The distribution is skewed and you want a specific quantile (e.g., p99 latency).
- Asymmetric error costs — under-predicting demand may be 3× worse than over-predicting; quantile loss handles this naturally.

**Special case $q = 0.5$:** $\rho_{0.5}(r) = 0.5 |r|$ — half of MAE. So minimizing pinball at $q = 0.5$ is equivalent to minimizing MAE, and you recover the median predictor. Symmetric arms, symmetric loss, median. Pinball is MAE with the arms allowed to tilt.

### log-cosh

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{logcosh}}(r) = \log(\cosh(r))$$

Recall $\cosh(r) = (e^r + e^{-r})/2$. Behavior:

- Near zero: Taylor expansion gives $\log(\cosh(r)) \approx r^2/2 - r^4/12 + \ldots$ — quadratic like MSE.
- Far from zero: $\log(\cosh(r)) \to |r| - \log 2$ — linear like MAE.

Quadratic in the middle, linear in the tails: that is the Huber description word for word, arrived at without ever writing a `case` statement. So log-cosh is a *smooth*, *everywhere-differentiable* hybrid of MSE and MAE. It has no kinks (unlike Huber's analytical join) and no threshold hyperparameter. Cost: slightly more compute per gradient step. You trade a hyperparameter you'd have to tune for an exponential you'd have to evaluate.

### ε-insensitive (Vapnik) loss

Used in support vector regression (SVR), with a tolerance $\epsilon > 0$ that you choose — the same letter as the noise term of the opening section, but here a fixed threshold rather than a random draw:

$$\mathcal{L}_\epsilon(r) = \max(0, |r| - \epsilon)$$

Predictions within $\epsilon$ of the true label contribute *zero* loss. Not "a little loss" — zero. Beyond that, the loss is linear in the residual. Draw a tube of half-width $\epsilon$ around the fitted curve: anything inside the tube is free. Encourages a model that "fits the tube" rather than the exact line. Used when you want a sparse-support, robust regression and don't care about residuals below a noise threshold.

Watch the far right of the second panel and ask which curves are still rising. Read the right panel as the thesis of the lesson: **outlier sensitivity is just the slope of the gradient curve in the tails.** MSE's gradient keeps climbing, so one residual of 100 pulls 100× harder than a residual of 1. MAE flattens to $\pm 1$ immediately, so every residual — outlier or not — pulls with the same unit force. Huber is the deliberate compromise: linear (MSE-like, statistically efficient) near zero, then clamped (MAE-like, robust) once $|r|$ passes $\delta$.

## Why losses, again, before models

> **Goal — see classification through the same lens as regression: the loss is the spec.** Every regression loss above turned out to be a noise model. Classification is the discrete-label case of the same principle. The loss decides the decision boundary, and it also decides the model's *confidence*, which is what production systems threshold, rank, and calibrate on.

A classifier rarely ships its argmax alone. It ships a *score* — and downstream code thresholds that score (fraud above 0.8 → block), ranks by it (show the top-10 most-likely-relevant), or feeds it into expected-value math (probability × dollar amount). All three break silently if the score is poorly calibrated. Nothing throws. The dashboard still shows accuracy. So the loss is doing two jobs at once: shaping the boundary, and shaping the probabilities. The second job is the one that gets skipped.

The words first, again.

> **Vocabulary primer.** The vocabulary the classification half of this lesson leans on.
>
> - **Logits** $z$ — the raw, unnormalized model outputs *before* softmax/sigmoid. Range over all of $\mathbb{R}$.
> - **Softmax** — turns a logit vector into a probability distribution over the $K$ classes: $\text{softmax}(z)_k = e^{z_k} / \sum_j e^{z_j}$, where $k$ and $j$ both index classes. **Sigmoid** $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$ is the two-class special case (it always carries a logit argument, so it is never the Gaussian noise scale $\sigma$ of the regression half).
> - **One-hot label** $y$ — the true class as a probability vector with a single 1.
> - **Calibration** — the property that among all predictions of confidence $c$, a fraction $c$ are actually correct.
> - **Proper scoring rule** — a loss whose expected value is uniquely minimized by reporting the *true* probabilities. (Not all losses are; accuracy isn't.)
> - **ECE** — expected calibration error, a scalar summary of miscalibration.

## The big idea: a classification loss is a noise model too

> **Goal — anchor cross-entropy as MLE, so it stops looking like an arbitrary formula.**

Cross-entropy usually arrives as a formula handed down from somewhere. It is not one. It falls out of the same three-step derivation carried out twice above for regression: assume a distribution for the label, write the likelihood, take a negative log.

Assume each label is drawn from a Categorical distribution over the $K$ classes whose parameters are your model's predicted probabilities $q = \text{softmax}(z)$ — from here on $q$ denotes that probability vector, not the quantile level of the pinball loss. The likelihood of one observed one-hot label $y$ is $\prod_k q_k^{y_k}$, with $k$ running over the classes, so its log-likelihood is $\sum_k y_k \log q_k$. That product looks scarier than it is: $y$ is one-hot, so every exponent is 0 except the true class, and the whole product collapses to "the probability you assigned to the class that actually happened." Maximizing the total log-likelihood over the dataset is identical to minimizing

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = -\sum_k y_k \log q_k$$

which is exactly **cross-entropy**. So CE is not a design choice pulled from a hat — it is MLE under a Categorical noise model, the discrete sibling of "MSE is MLE under Gaussian noise" from the regression half above.

There's a second, equally important reading. Write the cross-entropy between the label distribution $p$ and the prediction $q$:

$$\text{CE}(p, q) = -\sum_k p_k \log q_k = \underbrace{-\sum_k p_k \log p_k}_{H(p)} + \underbrace{\sum_k p_k \log \frac{p_k}{q_k}}_{\text{KL}(p\,\|\,q)} = H(p) + \text{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$$

That's an identity, not an approximation: add and subtract $\sum_k p_k \log p_k$ and regroup. The first brace depends only on the labels — it is their **entropy** $H(p)$, the uncertainty already sitting in the labels themselves, which no forecast can remove. The second brace is the only place $q$ appears: it is the **Kullback–Leibler divergence** $\text{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$, the surcharge you pay for scoring outcomes with $q$ when they actually arrive from $p$. That surcharge is zero when $q = p$ and positive otherwise.

The entropy $H(p)$ is fixed by the data, so **minimizing CE over $q$ is minimizing $\text{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$** — pulling your predicted distribution onto the label distribution. With one-hot labels $H(p)=0$, so CE *equals* the KL divergence. This framing is what makes label smoothing and distillation make sense later: both work by changing the *target* $p$.

## Cross-entropy

> **Goal — derive its gradient, its numerics, and why it dominates MSE.**

### Definition

Binary, with $q = \sigma(z)$ and label $y \in \{0,1\}$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} = -\bigl[\, y \log q + (1-y)\log(1-q)\,\bigr]$$

One of the two terms is always zero. If the label is 1 you're charged $-\log q$; if it's 0 you're charged $-\log(1-q)$. Either way: negative log of the probability you gave to what actually happened.

Multiclass, with $q = \text{softmax}(z)$ and one-hot $y$: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = -\log q_{c}$ where $c$ is the true class. (Cross-entropy of the true class only — every other term is multiplied by zero.)

### The gradient is astonishingly clean: $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial z = q - y$

Two ugly objects are about to meet and cancel. The softmax Jacobian is $\partial q_i/\partial z_j = q_i(\delta_{ij} - q_j)$, where $i$ and $j$ index classes and $\delta_{ij}$ is 1 when $i = j$ and 0 otherwise (this Kronecker $\delta$ is unrelated to Huber's threshold $\delta$). The log's derivative contributes a $1/q_i$. Chain-rule through $\mathcal{L} = -\sum_i y_i \log q_i$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_j} = \sum_i \left(-\frac{y_i}{q_i}\right) q_i(\delta_{ij} - q_j) = -\sum_i y_i(\delta_{ij} - q_j) = -y_j + q_j \sum_i y_i = q_j - y_j$$

Walk the steps: the $q_i$ from the Jacobian eats the $1/q_i$ from the log; the $\delta_{ij}$ collapses the sum to its $j$-th term; and the last sum is 1 because the label vector's entries add to one, using $\sum_i y_i = 1$.

So $\nabla_z \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = q - y$ — **predicted minus actual**, the same beautiful form as the linear-regression gradient. The $1/q_i$ from the log derivative cancels the $q_i$ in the softmax Jacobian. **That cancellation is the entire reason cross-entropy is the natural partner of softmax** (and BCE of sigmoid: $\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}/\partial z = \sigma(z) - y$). Pair CE with a different output nonlinearity and you lose it. The saturating factor that would otherwise multiply your gradient is not there at all.

### Train on logits, not probabilities

$\log \text{softmax}(z)_k = z_k - \operatorname{logsumexp}(z)$, and $\operatorname{logsumexp}(z) = m + \log\sum_j e^{z_j - m}$ with $m = \max_j z_j$. Read the second identity: pull out the largest logit before exponentiating, so the biggest thing you ever exponentiate is $e^0 = 1$. Computing softmax *then* taking the log overflows ($e^{1000} = \infty$) and underflows ($\log 0 = -\infty$); the fused, max-subtracted form is numerically stable. This is why PyTorch's `CrossEntropyLoss` takes **raw logits** (it is `LogSoftmax` + `NLLLoss` internally) and why `BCEWithLogitsLoss` exists. **Feeding probabilities into a logit loss — or applying softmax twice — is a silent correctness bug**, not an error. Your training run will complete. Your model will be worse and nothing will tell you why.

### Why CE beats MSE for classification — two arguments

You could put squared error on top of a sigmoid. It's a valid function, it's differentiable, it has a minimum in the right place. Nobody does, for two reasons.

**1. Vanishing gradients.** With sigmoid + squared error, $\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12(\sigma(z) - y)^2$ gives $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial z = (\sigma(z) - y)\,\sigma'(z)$, and $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(1-\sigma) \to 0$ when the unit saturates. That extra $\sigma'(z)$ factor is the one that cancelled away under CE. Now watch what it does. A **confidently wrong** example ($z = -10$, $y = 1$) has gradient magnitude $\approx e^{-10} \approx 4.5\times 10^{-5}$ — the model that most needs to learn barely moves. Cross-entropy's gradient is $\sigma(z) - y \to -1$ in that same situation: maximal push exactly when most wrong. Same example, same wrongness; one loss shrugs and the other shoves.

**2. Convexity.** For a linear model, BCE is convex in the weights; sigmoid + MSE is *non-convex* (a sigmoid composed with a quadratic), so it can trap optimization in bad local minima even in the simplest setting.

Hinge (red, previewing c1.8's SVM) is convex, kicks in below m=1, and vanishes on the safe side. Logistic (blue) is CE's margin form — always strictly positive, with a gradient that stays useful even for confidently-wrong points. Trace the amber curve leftward, into the confidently-wrong region, and notice that it goes flat. That flatness is the $4.5\times 10^{-5}$ you just computed.

### Cross-entropy is a *proper scoring rule*

> **Goal — establish the precise reason CE is a good training loss and accuracy is not.**

Both arguments above are about optimization. There's a third reason, and it's about what the minimizer *is*.

Hold $x$ fixed and ask which constant forecast $q$ minimizes the expected loss $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p}[-\log q_y]$, where $q_y$ is the probability the forecast puts on whichever label actually arrives. Split that expectation the way we just split cross-entropy and it reads $H(p) + \text{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$: a part fixed by the labels, plus the surcharge for forecasting $q$ when the truth is $p$. The surcharge is never negative, and it hits zero only when $q$ matches $p$ exactly — that fact is Gibbs' inequality ($\text{KL}(p\,\|\,q)\ge 0$, equality iff $q=p$). So the unique minimizer is $q = p$, the **true conditional distribution**. A loss with this property is a *strictly proper scoring rule*. Accuracy/0-1 loss is *not* proper — many different probability forecasts produce the same accuracy, so optimizing accuracy doesn't pin down the right probabilities, and its gradient is zero almost everywhere. That is the rigorous version of "use cross-entropy to train, accuracy to report."

*In plain English:* a proper scoring rule is one whose optimal forecast is *telling the truth* — no constant lie wins in expectation. If the true positive rate is 30%, the forecast that minimizes CE in expectation is exactly 0.30; hedging up or down loses on average. Accuracy is not like this: forecasting "0.51" and "0.99" score identically on every example the model would call positive.

If CE's minimizer is the truth, why do trained networks lie?

## Calibration: the part everyone skips

> **Goal — judge a classifier the way a quant does: the argmax has to be right, and the 0.9 has to really be a 0.9.**

CE's minimizer is the true posterior — *in the limit of infinite data and a correctly specified model*. Read the fine print in that sentence, because your model satisfies neither condition. Real deep nets violate both, and the failure mode is systematic: **modern networks are overconfident**. Guo et al. (2017) showed that increasing depth/width and adding batch normalization *worsen* calibration, and that networks overfit the negative log-likelihood (NLL), which is the quantity cross-entropy computes — test error keeps falling while test NLL rises, because the model inflates confidence on examples it already gets right. Your accuracy curve looks great the whole time this is happening.

Tools to know:

- **Reliability diagram.** Bin predictions by confidence, plot empirical accuracy vs. mean confidence; the diagonal is perfect calibration. It is a counting exercise: gather every prediction near 0.95, check what fraction were right, plot the pair.

<ReliabilityDiagram caption="Reliability diagram: predicted confidence (x) vs empirical accuracy (y). The dashed diagonal is perfect calibration. Modern nets (red) sag below the diagonal on the high-confidence bins — a 0.95 prediction is only ~0.82 accurate; that's the 'overconfident' Guo et al. signature. Temperature scaling (blue) is a one-parameter post-hoc fix that snaps the curve back onto the diagonal without changing the argmax — so the accuracy number is unchanged and the probabilities are usable." />
- **Expected Calibration Error (ECE).** $\text{ECE} = \sum_b \frac{|B_b|}{N}\,\bigl|\,\text{acc}(B_b) - \text{conf}(B_b)\,\bigr|$, where $B_b$ is the set of predictions falling in confidence bin $b$, $|B_b|$ is its size, $N$ is the total number of predictions, and $\text{acc}$ and $\text{conf}$ are the empirical accuracy and the mean predicted confidence inside a bin. In words: for each bin, how far the curve sits from the diagonal, averaged with each bin weighted by how many predictions landed in it. Caveat: ECE is sensitive to the binning scheme and only measures *top-label* calibration — class-wise ECE or the Brier score are more robust.
- **Brier score** $= \frac1N\sum_n \|q_n - y_n\|^2$, running over the $N$ predictions, with $q_n$ the predicted probability vector on prediction $n$ and $y_n$ its one-hot label — MSE on probabilities, itself a proper scoring rule. It splits cleanly into a **calibration** term and a **discrimination** term (plus an irreducible base-rate term); the formal version of that split is Murphy's reliability–resolution–uncertainty decomposition.
- **Temperature scaling** (Guo 2017). Post-hoc, learn a single scalar $T>0$ and predict $\text{softmax}(z/T)$, fitting $T$ by minimizing validation NLL. One parameter, fit after training, on held-out data. $T>1$ softens overconfidence. **It cannot change accuracy** — dividing all logits by a positive constant preserves the argmax — so it's a free calibration fix that never costs you decisions.

This is the stats/quant lens on classification: cross-entropy and Brier are *proper scoring rules*; perplexity (the language-model headline metric) is just $\exp(\text{CE})$; calibration is the bridge between a model's loss and a decision system's expected value.

## Class imbalance: distorting the objective on purpose

> **Goal — fix the rare-class problem by reshaping the loss, and know exactly what each reshape costs.**

Everything so far has treated CE as the honest objective. Now break it deliberately, and stay clear-eyed about what breaks along with it.

When positives are 1% of the data, plain CE lets the 99% majority dominate the gradient (see c1.1's gradient-share argument). Three standard levers:

### Reweighting

Scale each class's loss by a constant. The crudest possible intervention: multiply. In PyTorch, `CrossEntropyLoss(weight=...)` per class, or `BCEWithLogitsLoss(pos_weight=#neg/#pos)` for the positive term. Cheap, no extra data. Raising `pos_weight` trades precision for recall. Caveat: with `weight`, the `'mean'` reduction divides by the *sum of weights present*, not by the number of examples $N$ — an easy one to miss.

### Focal loss (Lin et al., ICCV 2017)

Reweighting treats every negative alike. But most of those 99% negatives are already handled: the model calls them negative with probability 0.99 and gets no useful learning signal from them, only volume. Focal loss down-weights by *how easy an example already is*, not by which class it belongs to.

Define $p_t = q$ if $y=1$ else $1-q$ (the probability assigned to the *true* class). So $p_t$ near 1 means "already correct and confident"; $p_t$ near 0 means "confidently wrong." Then

$$\text{FL}(p_t) = -\alpha_t\,(1 - p_t)^{\gamma}\,\log(p_t)$$

That's ordinary CE ($-\log p_t$) multiplied by a factor that goes to zero as the example gets easy, plus the usual class weight $\alpha_t$.

The **modulating factor** $(1-p_t)^\gamma$ shrinks the loss on easy examples; $\gamma$ (paper default **2**) is the focusing parameter; at $\gamma = 0$ focal loss *is* $\alpha$-balanced CE. Quantify it: with $\gamma = 2$, an example at $p_t = 0.9$ is down-weighted by $(0.1)^2 = 0.01$ — **100×** — and at $p_t \approx 0.968$ by **1000×**, while a hard example at $p_t = 0.1$ is barely touched ($(0.9)^2 \approx 0.81$). One example loses 99% of its voice, the other keeps 81% of it. Focal loss let RetinaNet solve the ~1:1000 foreground/background imbalance of dense detection, hitting **36.0 AP vs. 32.8** in average precision against the best online-hard-example-mining baseline.

<FocalDownweighting caption="Focal loss vs CE, as a function of the true-class probability p_t. CE (γ=0) treats a p_t=0.9 example (already confident and correct) the same weight as a p_t=0.1 example (hard). Focal's (1−p_t)^γ multiplier flattens the easy-side almost to zero: at γ=2 the p_t=0.9 point falls to 1% of its CE loss (100× down-weighting) and p_t≈0.968 falls to 0.1% (1000×), while p_t=0.1 keeps 81% of its weight. That's how you free the ~1:1000 foreground/background imbalance in dense detection: hard examples still drive learning, easy ones stop dominating." />

### Class-balanced loss (Cui et al., CVPR 2019)

Reweight by the inverse **effective number of samples**, $E_n = (1 - \beta^n)/(1 - \beta)$, where $n$ is the number of training examples in the class and $\beta$ is a hyperparameter between 0 and 1 that sets how fast the count saturates — in practice $\beta \in \{0.99, 0.999, 0.9999\}$. The intuition: as you add examples of a class they cover *overlapping* feature volume, so the marginal information saturates — the "useful count" is $E_n < n$. The thousandth photo of a golden retriever tells you less than the tenth did. It interpolates: $\beta \to 0$ gives no reweighting; $\beta \to 1$ gives inverse-frequency ($1/n$) weighting. It's *loss-agnostic* — you apply the $1/E_n$ factor on top of CE, BCE, or focal.

> **Stop and think.** Is reweighting the loss equivalent to resampling the data? *Hint: in expectation over a full epoch they can match — but oversampling changes the empirical distribution (more gradient steps, overfitting risk on duplicated rares) while reweighting changes per-example gradient magnitude (noisier steps). They diverge under minibatch SGD, and — see the failure modes — both may be the wrong lever entirely.*

## Label smoothing

> **Goal — understand a one-line trick that improves calibration and quietly breaks two other things.**

The three levers above all changed the weight on the loss. This one changes the target instead.

Replace the one-hot target with $y^{\text{LS}} = (1-\varepsilon)\,y + \varepsilon/K$ (typically $\varepsilon = 0.1$). Read it: take 10% of the probability mass off the true class and spread it evenly over all $K$ classes. Equivalently, the loss becomes $(1-\varepsilon)\,\text{CE}(\text{onehot}, q) + \varepsilon\,\text{CE}(\text{uniform}, q)$ — you're now minimizing KL to a softened target, so the model can no longer drive any logit to $\pm\infty$. There is no longer a reward for infinite confidence, because the target is no longer certain.

Müller et al. (2019) showed this improves calibration and often generalization. The costs, which are the interesting part: it **collapses each class's penultimate representations into tight clusters**, which (1) **hurts knowledge distillation** — the "dark knowledge" in soft logits is erased, so a smoothed teacher makes worse students — and (2) can backfire under heavy label noise. Label smoothing is a *trade*, not a free win.

## Worked decision walk-throughs

Now run the reasoning forward on concrete problems. In each one, the question to ask first is what the noise looks like, not which loss you used last time.

**Scenario A: predicting house sale prices.** Labels in the long upper tail (mansions, luxury sales) skew the distribution. **MSE** would chase those mansions and inflate predictions on ordinary homes. **MAE** predicts the median sale price — much closer to what you actually want for typical valuations. Most production models in this domain use MAE or quantile loss (for confidence intervals).

**Scenario B: forecasting daily ad revenue. Normal-day noise scale is about USD 10k/day ($\sigma \approx 10$, in units of USD 1k); once a month there's a USD 200k promotional spike.** Promotional spikes are real, not measurement errors — you don't want to entirely ignore them. **MSE** lets them dominate and biases the model. **MAE** treats them like normal residuals — under-fits them. **Huber** with $\delta \approx 15$ (i.e. USD 15k): squared treatment on normal days, linear treatment on promotional days. The model fits the bulk well and tolerates spikes without chasing them.

**Scenario C: tabular regression with XGBoost, you want MAE for outlier robustness.** (Gradient-boosted trees themselves — CART, bagging, boosting/GBDT — are the subject of **c1.6 (Trees & ensembles)**; here we only need the leaf-fitting step.) XGBoost's tree-fitting uses a second-order (Newton) step, and the leaf-value formula $v^\star = -\sum g_i / (\sum h_i + \lambda)$ — with $g_i$ and $h_i$ the gradient and Hessian of the loss on example $i$, and $\lambda$ a regularization constant — divides by the summed Hessian. MAE's Hessian is zero everywhere, so a *naive* MAE objective (gradient $\pm 1$, Hessian $0$) gives the optimizer nothing to size its steps with — the denominator collapses to the regularizer $\lambda$ and leaf values are junk. Two fixes: (1) **modern XGBoost ($\ge$ 1.7) ships native `reg:absoluteerror`**, which sidesteps the problem with *adaptive leaf values* — it grows the tree structure with a placeholder Hessian, then overwrites each leaf with the **median** of its residuals (exactly Friedman's classic LAD-TreeBoost). (2) **pseudo-Huber loss**, $\mathcal{L}(r) = \delta^2\bigl(\sqrt{1 + (r/\delta)^2} - 1\bigr)$ — a smooth Huber approximation with a strictly positive Hessian, shipped as `reg:pseudohubererror`.

**Scenario D: latency prediction, you care about p99 not p50.** Use **pinball loss at $q = 0.99$**. Reports the conditional 99th-percentile latency, not the median.

Same exercise on the classification side, where the extra question is what happens to the probabilities.

**Scenario A: balanced 10-class image classifier.** Cross-entropy. If you'll threshold or rank on the probabilities, add **temperature scaling** post-hoc (free calibration, no accuracy cost), and consider label smoothing $\varepsilon=0.1$ — *unless* this model is a distillation teacher, in which case skip it.

**Scenario B: dense object detection, ~1:1000 fg/bg.** **Focal loss** ($\gamma=2$, $\alpha=0.25$), via `torchvision.ops.sigmoid_focal_loss`. This is the problem it was invented for.

**Scenario C: 1:99 imbalanced tabular.** Start with `pos_weight ≈ 99` (or class weights) on BCE — cheapest lever. But before reaching for fancier losses, recall the decoupling result below: the loss may not be your bottleneck.

**Scenario D: you need trustworthy probabilities for expected-value decisions.** Train with CE, then **temperature-scale** on a validation set and verify with a reliability diagram + ECE. Loss engineering is secondary to post-hoc calibration here.


## Production failure modes

These are the shapes the mistakes actually take once code is running. Most of them throw no exception.

1. **MSE on rare-but-real spikes.** You're predicting daily revenue. The Black Friday outlier dominates training, your weekday predictions become inflated, weekday MAPE doubles.
2. **MAE chosen for "robustness," then a custom XGBoost objective won't learn.** A hand-rolled MAE objective returns Hessian $\equiv 0$, so the Newton leaf-value step has nothing to divide by. Fix: use the native `reg:absoluteerror` (adaptive leaf values, $\ge$ XGBoost 1.7) or pseudo-Huber.
3. **Huber with the wrong $\delta$.** $\delta$ chosen by intuition (e.g., $\delta = 1.0$) on a dataset where the residual scale is $\hat{\sigma} = 50$. Effectively pure MAE. The bulk of residuals never gets quadratic treatment, optimization is slower and less efficient than needed.
4. **Squared-error metric reported, MAE optimized.** Common when teams switch losses mid-project but keep reporting RMSE for legacy reasons. Reported metric stops corresponding to the model objective.
5. **Predicting medians, downstream system expects means.** A pricing pipeline trained with MAE produces median price predictions; an aggregator multiplies by volume to estimate revenue, which is a mean operation. Result: systematically biased revenue estimates because $\sum \text{median}_i \ne \text{median}\sum$.

6. **Double softmax / wrong loss input.** Applying softmax then passing to `CrossEntropyLoss` (which expects logits), or passing logits to `NLLLoss` (which expects log-probs). No error, just a quietly wrong model.
7. **Shipping an uncalibrated model.** Offline accuracy looks great; the 0.9-confidence bucket is right 70% of the time; every downstream EV calculation is biased. Fix: temperature scaling + monitor ECE.
8. **`pos_weight` cranked too high.** Recall climbs, precision collapses, the effective decision threshold drifts. Tune it against the actual cost ratio, not intuition.
9. **Inverse-frequency reweighting overfits the tail.** $1/n$ over-weights ultra-rare classes (Cui's point); class-balanced $\beta=0.99$–$0.999$ is gentler. The right $\beta$ is dataset-dependent — it *hurt* on fine-grained CIFAR-100.
10. **Loss-engineering when the classifier is the problem.** See below.


## Interview gotchas

- "Which loss should I use for regression?" — interviewers want you to answer "what does the noise look like?" not "MSE because it's the default."
- "Why is MAE more robust than MSE?" — they want the *gradient* explanation (constant vs linear-in-residual) plus the *probabilistic* one (heavier-tailed noise model).
- "How would you give me a prediction interval, not a point prediction?" — quantile loss at $q = 0.05$ and $q = 0.95$. Bonus: discuss calibration of the interval.
- "Your custom XGBoost MAE objective isn't training — what's wrong?" — the Hessian is zero, so the Newton leaf-value step has nothing to divide by. Use native `reg:absoluteerror` (adaptive leaf values) or pseudo-Huber. Being able to explain *why* MAE has zero Hessian on the spot (because $\partial^2 |r| / \partial r^2 = 0$ everywhere except the kink) — and that modern XGBoost works around it by recomputing leaf values as residual medians — is the differentiator.
- "When would you actively choose MSE despite outliers being present?" — when the outliers are real signal (e.g., financial returns: a 10% day matters disproportionately and you *want* the model to fit it).

- **"Why cross-entropy and not MSE for classification?"** — the gradient-saturation argument *and* the convexity argument. Bonus: CE is the MLE / a proper scoring rule.
- **"Derive the softmax–CE gradient."** — $q - y$; be ready to show the Jacobian cancellation.
- **"Is your model calibrated, and how would you check/fix it?"** — reliability diagram + ECE; temperature scaling (and that it doesn't change accuracy). This separates strong candidates.
- **"What does $\gamma$ do in focal loss, and does focal only help with imbalance?"** — down-weights easy examples; **no** — Mukhoti et al. (2020) show it improves *calibration* on balanced data, because it implicitly regularizes confidence.
- **"Reweighting vs. resampling vs. just fixing the classifier?"** — name the decoupling result: Kang et al. (2020) found imbalance barely hurts representation learning; retraining only the classifier (cRT / $\tau$-normalization) beats elaborate reweighted losses on long-tail benchmarks. Menon et al. (2021) **logit adjustment** — predict with $z_y + \log \pi_y$, where $\pi_y$ is class $y$'s base rate in the training data — is the Bayes-consistent, theoretically-grounded alternative to heuristic reweighting. The sophisticated answer is that the loss is often *not* the best lever.
