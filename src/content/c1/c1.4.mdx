## Part 2 — Logistic regression

> **Goal — preserve the interpretability of a linear score while producing valid probabilities, then connect that intuition to Bernoulli MLE, convexity, and IRLS.**

### Why a line is the wrong output for a binary target

Do the obvious thing first and watch it fail. You have a target column that is nothing but zeros and ones. You already know how to fit a line to a column of numbers, so fit one. Now feed the fitted model a row and read what comes out. It says $-0.4$. Feed it another row. It says $1.3$.

If $y\in\{0,1\}$, an OLS prediction can be $-0.4$ or $1.3$. Those values are not probabilities. There is no reading of "the chance this row belongs to class 1" that comes out negative, and none that comes out above one. The model has produced a number in the right units-free shape and the wrong range, and nothing in least squares was ever going to stop it.

The second failure is quieter and it is about the noise, not the prediction. The error variance also depends on the mean: for a Bernoulli outcome, $\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)=p(1-p)$, so the constant-variance Gaussian story is wrong by construction. Read that formula out loud and it says: how noisy a single observation is depends on where that observation's own probability sits. An example the model thinks is a coin flip carries a lot of noise. An example the model is nearly sure about carries almost none. OLS assumes every point is equally noisy, and here that assumption is not slightly off, it is contradicted by the definition of the outcome.

So we throw away the line? No, and this is the move worth remembering. The part of OLS you actually wanted was the linear score. We still want a linear, interpretable score $\eta=x^\top\beta$. It is one number per row, each feature contributes an additive amount, and you can read a coefficient and say something about the world. The only broken part is the range. We need only transform that unrestricted score into a number between 0 and 1. The sigmoid does exactly that:

$$p(x)=\sigma(\eta)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\eta}}$$

Say it in English. Take the score $\eta$, which is free to be any real number. Negate it, exponentiate, add one, and take the reciprocal. When $\eta$ is very negative, $e^{-\eta}$ is enormous and the whole fraction goes to 0. When $\eta$ is very positive, $e^{-\eta}$ collapses to nothing and the fraction goes to 1. When $\eta$ is exactly 0, $e^{0}=1$ and you get $1/2$. The score is a dial from minus infinity to plus infinity, and the sigmoid is the gearbox that turns it into a probability without ever running off either end.

Equivalently,

$$\log\frac{p(x)}{1-p(x)}=x^\top\beta$$

This is the same statement with the gearbox run backwards, and it is the more useful one to carry around. The inner ratio $p/(1-p)$ is the odds: the probability of the event divided by the probability of not-the-event. Take its logarithm and you get the log-odds. The equation says the log-odds is exactly the linear score, with no squashing in sight. The nonlinearity has not disappeared, it has been moved to where you can ignore it while you interpret coefficients.

Which sets up the thing people get wrong in interviews. Logistic regression is linear in **log-odds**, not in probability. A one-unit increase in $x_j$, holding other features fixed, adds $\beta_j$ to log-odds and multiplies odds by $e^{\beta_j}$. It does not add a constant amount to probability: the probability change is largest near $p=0.5$ and small near 0 or 1. The same coefficient, applied to a borderline example and to an example the model has already made up its mind about, moves the probability by wildly different amounts. That is not a defect. It is the gearbox doing its job.

Now write the model down one more time, this time as an object you are going to optimize rather than an object you are going to read. Model the log-odds as linear: $\text{logit}(p(x)) = x^\top\beta$, i.e. $p(x) = \sigma(x^\top\beta)$. The Bernoulli log-likelihood is $\ell(\beta) = \sum_i[y_i\log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)]$ — the negative of this is **binary cross-entropy** (c1.2). Read the sum term by term: for a row whose true label is 1, the second piece vanishes and you are left with $\log p_i$, so you are rewarded for putting probability mass on 1. For a row whose true label is 0, the first piece vanishes and you are left with $\log(1-p_i)$. One of the two terms is always switched off by the label. The score and Hessian are

$$\nabla\ell = X^\top(y - p), \qquad \nabla^2\ell = -X^\top W X,\quad W=\text{diag}(p_i(1-p_i))$$

Two objects, and each is worth pronouncing. The first is the gradient of the log-likelihood: take the vector of residuals $y - p$ and project it onto each column of $X$. The second is the matrix of second derivatives, and it is $X^\top W X$ with a minus sign in front, where $W$ is a diagonal matrix holding one weight per row, that weight being $p_i(1-p_i)$.

That minus sign is the whole convexity argument. $W \succeq 0 \Rightarrow$ the NLL is **globally convex** (no bad local minima). Every weight $p_i(1-p_i)$ is a probability times one minus itself, so it can never be negative, which makes $X^\top W X$ positive semidefinite, which makes the Hessian of the log-likelihood negative semidefinite, which makes the negative log-likelihood convex. There is one basin. Whatever optimizer you point at it, if it converges, it converged to the answer.

Convex is not the same as solvable in one shot, and here is where the analogy with OLS finally breaks. But the score equation $X^\top(y-p)=0$ is **nonlinear in $\beta$** (because $p=\sigma(X\beta)$), so there is **no closed form**. In OLS you set the gradient to zero and $\beta$ falls out of the algebra, because the residual is linear in $\beta$. Here $\beta$ is buried inside a sigmoid inside the residual, and no amount of rearranging pulls it back out. You solve it iteratively with Newton–Raphson, which here takes the form of **iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS)** — each step is a weighted OLS fit. The decision boundary $\{x: x^\top\beta=0\}$ is a hyperplane (linear classifier), and $e^{\beta_j}$ is the **odds ratio** for a unit increase in $x_j$.

Go back to the gradient for a moment, because it is more intuitive than it looks. The gradient $X^\top(y-p)$ has an intuitive reading: observed outcomes minus predicted probabilities, aggregated along each feature. At the optimum, those feature-weighted probability errors balance. Setting it to zero is a statement about bookkeeping: for every feature, the surplus and the deficit in predicted probability have to cancel out. The diagonal weight $p_i(1-p_i)$ is largest for uncertain examples near $0.5$ and tiny for already-certain examples. So the Newton step is not listening to every row equally. It leans hard on the rows near the boundary and barely hears the rows it has already settled. IRLS repeatedly builds a local weighted least-squares approximation, solves it, and updates the probabilities. Fit a weighted line, recompute the probabilities, recompute the weights, fit again.

> **Why not squared error on $\sigma(x^\top\beta)$?** It's non-convex in $\beta$ (sigmoid inside a quadratic) and saturates (c1.2's vanishing-gradient argument). Cross-entropy is the Bernoulli MLE *and* convex — use it.

Everything so far says the objective is well behaved and the optimizer will find its bottom. There is one case where that bottom is not anywhere.

**Perfect separation breaks the MLE.** If a hyperplane perfectly separates the classes, scaling $\beta \to c\beta$ with $c\to\infty$ drives every $p_i \to \{0,1\}$ and the likelihood toward its supremum without ever attaining it — **the MLE is at infinity and does not exist** (Albert & Anderson, *Biometrika* 1984); coefficients and standard errors blow up. Trace the mechanism on the sigmoid picture above. Every point is on the correct side of the boundary, so multiplying $\beta$ by 2 pushes each score further out into the correct tail, which pushes each $p_i$ closer to its own label, which raises the likelihood. Multiply by 2 again and it rises again. The objective is still convex, and it is still true that there are no bad local minima, but there is no minimum at all. Fixes: any penalty (ridge), or **Firth's penalized likelihood** (a Jeffreys prior) that guarantees finite estimates. This is why an unregularized logistic fit can "diverge" on clean, easily-separable data — a counterintuitive interview favorite. Note how backwards that sounds. The data is not messy, it is too clean, and it is the cleanliness that breaks the fit.

One last piece of inherited machinery, and one warning attached to it. Logistic coefficients also have SEs, Wald statistics, likelihood-ratio tests, and confidence intervals, but their sampling covariance comes from inverse likelihood curvature rather than $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$. Curvature is the operative word: the Hessian measures how sharply the log-likelihood falls away from its peak, and a sharp peak means a well-pinned coefficient. As in OLS, statistical significance does not guarantee useful classification, good calibration, or causality. A coefficient can be beyond any doubt nonzero and still leave you with a model whose probabilities nobody should act on.

## Worked decision walk-throughs

**Scenario C: binary classification, want probabilities + interpretable odds ratios.** Logistic regression. Watch for **perfect separation** (regularize or use Firth) and remember sklearn's default is *penalized* (below) — calibrate if you need true probabilities.

## Production failure modes

1. **`sklearn.LogisticRegression` is regularized by default.** It applies an **L2 penalty with `C=1.0`** out of the box — it is *not* textbook MLE. This one bites people who are comparing a sklearn fit against a textbook derivation and cannot work out why the coefficients are smaller than they should be. For unregularized logistic regression use `statsmodels.Logit`, or sklearn `C=np.inf`. (Version note: in **scikit-learn 1.8** the `penalty` parameter is **deprecated in favor of `l1_ratio`** — `penalty='l2'`→`l1_ratio=0`, `'l1'`→`1`, `None`→`C=np.inf` — with removal in 1.10. Most environments are still on ≤1.7 where `penalty=` is live; check the installed version.)
2. **Unregularized logistic on separable data diverges.** Coefficients run to $\pm\infty$; add a penalty or use Firth. What you see is the optimizer still climbing when it stops: enormous coefficients with enormous standard errors.

## Interview gotchas

- **"Why does logistic regression have no closed form, and is the objective convex?"** — score equation is nonlinear in $\beta$ (no closed form); Hessian $-X^\top W X \preceq 0$ for the log-likelihood → NLL convex → IRLS/Newton finds the global optimum.
- **"What happens under perfect separation?"** — MLE diverges to $\pm\infty$; regularize or use Firth. (Cite Albert–Anderson.)
