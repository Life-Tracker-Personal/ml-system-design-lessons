## TL;DR

Linear regression does two different jobs: it draws the best linear summary of a relationship, and it tells us how uncertain that summary is. **OLS** chooses the coefficients that make the squared residuals as small as possible. The **beta estimator** is the rule that turns a random sample into those coefficients. Because a different sample would produce different coefficients, every estimate needs a **standard error**. A **$t$-statistic** expresses the estimate in standard-error units; a **$p$-value** asks how surprising a statistic at least that extreme would be if a stated null hypothesis were true. None of these numbers proves causality or practical importance.

The rest of the chapter grows from that foundation. **BLUE** says OLS has the smallest variance among *linear, unbiased* estimators under the Gauss–Markov assumptions—not that it is the best conceivable predictor. **Ridge and lasso** deliberately introduce bias to stabilize estimates, with Gaussian and Laplace priors as their Bayesian interpretations. **VIF** measures how much redundant predictors inflate coefficient uncertainty. **PCA** rotates features into variance-ranked directions; PCR drops some directions, while ridge shrinks them smoothly. **Logistic regression** carries the same estimation logic to binary outcomes using a Bernoulli likelihood.

## What you'll be able to do after this lesson

- Derive the OLS normal equations two ways (calculus and orthogonal projection) and state when a closed form exists.
- Show OLS is MLE under Gaussian noise, and state the Gauss–Markov theorem precisely (including what it does *not* require).
- Write the sampling distribution of $\hat\beta$ and use it for standard errors, $t$-tests, and confidence intervals.
- Explain why logistic regression has no closed form, why its NLL is convex, and what happens under perfect separation.
- Derive ridge's closed form, explain lasso's sparsity, and identify both as MAP estimation under Gaussian/Laplace priors.
- Define VIF, explain variance inflation, and separate the *inference* damage of collinearity from its (often negligible) *prediction* damage.
- Relate PCA, principal-component regression, and ridge — and say when PCA helps a model and when it quietly hurts.

## Why these two models, before the fancy ones

> **Goal — master the substrate every other model generalizes, and the statistical vocabulary interviews probe.** Trees, boosting, and nets are elaborations on "fit a function, define a loss, estimate parameters." Linear and logistic regression are the cases where you can do the estimation theory exactly — closed forms, sampling distributions, provable optimality — so they're where interviewers test whether you understand *estimation*, not just `model.fit`.

Quant-research interviews in particular live here: factor models are OLS, "betas" are regression slopes, and the failure modes (collinear factors, overfit backtests, heteroskedastic returns) are the failure modes of regression. So we treat the statistics as first-class.

> **Vocabulary primer.** The "rusty staff engineer" assumption from c1.1 still holds.
>
> - **Design matrix** $X$ — the $n \times p$ matrix of features (a column of 1s gives the intercept).
> - **Estimator** — a function of the data that produces a parameter estimate $\hat\beta$. It's a *random variable* (it depends on the random sample), so it has a distribution.
> - **Unbiased / consistent / efficient / BLUE** — $\mathbb{E}[\hat\beta]=\beta$; $\hat\beta\to\beta$ as $n\to\infty$; minimum variance among a class; **B**est **L**inear **U**nbiased **E**stimator.
> - **Logit / odds** — $\text{logit}(p)=\log\frac{p}{1-p}$, the log-odds. Logistic regression makes the log-odds linear in $x$.
> - **MAP** — maximum a posteriori: the Bayesian point estimate that maximizes likelihood × prior.

## Part 1 — Ordinary least squares

> **Goal — understand the problem OLS solves before deriving it, then follow the chain from a fitted line to standard errors, $t$-statistics, $p$-values, and confidence intervals.**

### Start with the picture: signal plus noise

Suppose we want to relate hours studied, $x$, to exam score, $y$. A linear model says

$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i$$

The line $\beta_0+\beta_1x$ is the systematic part. The error $\varepsilon_i$ collects everything the line does not explain: sleep, prior knowledge, luck, measurement error, and genuine randomness.

Three objects that look similar must stay separate:

- $\beta_1$ is the unknown **population parameter**: the slope in the data-generating model.
- $\hat\beta_1$ is an **estimate** computed from one observed sample.
- $b_1$ is sometimes used for the realized numerical value, such as $2.3$ points per hour.

We never observe the true $\beta$. We observe data and use an **estimator**—a recipe—to produce $\hat\beta$. If we repeatedly sampled new students, the recipe would return a slightly different slope each time. Regression inference is about that repeated-sample variation.

> **Core mental model.** A coefficient is not just a number attached to a feature. It is a noisy measurement of an unknown relationship, conditional on the other features in the model.

### What “best-fitting line” means

For a candidate line, the residual is

$$e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$$

It is the vertical gap between the observed point and the line. Some residuals are positive, some negative. Adding raw residuals would let them cancel, so OLS adds their squares:

$$\operatorname{RSS}(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)^2$$

**Ordinary least squares chooses the line with the smallest residual sum of squares.** Squaring does three useful things: it prevents cancellation, penalizes large misses heavily, and produces a smooth convex objective with a closed-form optimum. It also makes OLS sensitive to outliers; that is the price of the choice.

OLS chooses the line that minimizes the sum of the squared vertical gaps — not the perpendicular distances, and not the raw signed gaps (which would cancel)." />

For one centered predictor, the slope has an especially intuitive form:

$$\hat\beta_1=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)}$$

The numerator asks whether $x$ and $y$ move together. The denominator asks how much $x$ moved. If $x$ barely varies, the data cannot reveal how $y$ changes with $x$, so the slope becomes unstable. This “signal divided by available variation” intuition will return in standard errors and VIF.

The intercept is $\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x$, so the OLS line passes through $(\bar x,\bar y)$. Its literal interpretation—expected $y$ when $x=0$—may be meaningless if zero is outside the observed range. Centering $x$ makes the intercept the expected outcome at an ordinary, in-range value.

### From one line to many predictors

With $p$ coefficients, including an intercept if present, write

$$y=X\beta+\varepsilon$$

Each row of $X$ is one observation; each column is one predictor. A coefficient $\beta_j$ means the expected change in $y$ for a one-unit change in $x_j$, **holding the other included predictors fixed**. That final clause is the source of both the usefulness and danger of multiple regression: adding a control can isolate a relationship, but a bad control can also change the question or introduce bias.

The objective becomes

$$\operatorname{RSS}(\beta)=\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2$$

### The normal equations, two ways

*Calculus.* Differentiate the total squared error and set its slope to zero:

$$\nabla_\beta \operatorname{RSS}=-2X^\top(y-X\beta)=0$$

Therefore

$$
X^\top X\hat\beta=X^\top y
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
\boxed{\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y}
$$

This formula exists when $X$ has full column rank: no column is an exact combination of the others. The Hessian is $2X^\top X\succeq0$, so RSS is convex and this stationary point is a global minimum. In numerical software, do not literally form the inverse; QR or SVD solvers are more stable. The inverse is valuable because it exposes the statistics.

*Projection (the geometric intuition).* The columns of $X$ span every prediction vector the model can produce. Usually $y$ is not exactly in that space. OLS chooses the reachable vector $\hat y=X\hat\beta$ closest to $y$: its orthogonal projection. At the closest point, the leftover residual $e=y-\hat y$ is perpendicular to every column of $X$:

$$X^\top e=0$$

These are the same normal equations. They say the model has extracted every linear pattern available in its included columns; no predictor has any remaining *sample correlation* with the residual.

The fitted values are $\hat y=Hy$, where

$$H=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$$

is the **hat matrix** because it puts the “hat” on $y$. It is a projection: symmetric, idempotent ($H^2=H$), with $\operatorname{trace}(H)=p$. The diagonal $h_{ii}$ measures **leverage**—how unusual observation $i$ is in feature space and how strongly it can pull its own fitted value.

### Fit is not truth

OLS always finds the best linear fit *within the chosen features and sample*. That does not imply:

- the relationship is causal;
- the conditional mean is truly linear;
- omitted variables are harmless;
- predictions will generalize;
- the coefficient is precisely estimated.

A tiny coefficient can be estimated precisely; a large coefficient can be mostly noise. This is why regression tables report both estimates and uncertainty.

### OLS is MLE under Gaussian noise — the c1.2 throughline

Assume $\varepsilon_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$. The log-likelihood is

$$\log L(\beta,\sigma^2) = -\tfrac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i - x_i^\top\beta)^2$$

For fixed $\sigma^2$, every term except RSS is constant in $\beta$. Maximizing likelihood is therefore identical to minimizing RSS. **OLS is MLE under independent Gaussian noise.** The probabilistic story explains the loss: Gaussian noise gives squared loss; Laplace noise gives absolute loss; heavy-tailed noise motivates robust alternatives.

Gaussian errors are *not* needed to draw the OLS line. They make the usual finite-sample inference exact. That distinction matters constantly in interviews.

### Why the beta estimator is random

Substitute the data-generating model into the OLS formula:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat\beta
&=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top(X\beta+\varepsilon)\\
&=\beta+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the central identity of linear regression. It says:

$$\text{estimate}=\text{truth}+\text{weighted noise}$$

The true $\beta$ is fixed. The noise changes from sample to sample, so $\hat\beta$ changes too. If $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$, then

$$\mathbb E[\hat\beta\mid X]=\beta$$

and OLS is unbiased. Unbiased does **not** mean this sample's estimate equals truth. It means the estimator is centered on truth across repeated samples.

If $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I$, then

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta\mid X)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$$

Coefficient uncertainty is larger when outcomes are noisier ($\sigma^2$ is large), when the sample is smaller, when a predictor has little variation, or when predictors are redundant. This one covariance matrix anticipates standard errors, VIF, and regularization.

### BLUE: a carefully limited promise

BLUE means **Best Linear Unbiased Estimator**:

- **Estimator:** a rule for turning data into an estimate.
- **Linear:** the estimator is a linear function of the observed outcomes $y$; it does not mean merely that the fitted relationship is linear.
- **Unbiased:** its repeated-sample mean is the true $\beta$.
- **Best:** it has the smallest variance among estimators satisfying the preceding two restrictions.

Under linear specification, full column rank, exogeneity $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$, and spherical errors $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I$, the **Gauss–Markov theorem** says OLS is BLUE.

The word “best” is fenced in. Gauss–Markov does not say OLS has the lowest prediction error among all models. It does not compare OLS with biased estimators such as ridge. It does not establish causality. And it does **not require Normal errors**. Normality is needed for exact small-sample $t$ and $F$ distributions, not for BLUE.

The proof idea is intuitive: any other linear unbiased estimator equals OLS plus an extra linear piece that cannot add signal without introducing bias. Under spherical noise, that extra piece adds a positive-semidefinite amount of variance: $\sigma^2DD^\top\succeq0$.

### Standard error: how much the estimate would move

Under Gaussian errors,

$$\hat\beta \sim \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\beta,\ \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}\bigr)$$

(and it is often approximately Normal in large samples without Gaussian errors). The **standard error** is the estimated standard deviation of this sampling distribution:

$$\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)=\sqrt{\hat\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}_{jj}}$$

We estimate the unknown noise variance using

$$\hat\sigma^2=\frac{\operatorname{RSS}}{n-p}$$

Why $n-p$? Fitting $p$ coefficients uses $p$ degrees of freedom. Dividing by $n$ would treat fitted residuals as if no parameters had been learned and would underestimate the noise variance. (The Gaussian MLE uses RSS/$n$ and is biased downward; RSS/$(n-p)$ is unbiased under the classical model.)

A standard error answers: **if we repeatedly collected comparable samples and refit the same model, roughly how much would this coefficient estimate vary?** It is not the standard deviation of the predictor, not the residual standard deviation, and not the typical prediction error.

For simple regression, the slope SE is roughly

$$\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}}$$

This makes the levers visible: less outcome noise, more observations, and a wider spread of $x$ all produce a more precise slope.

### The $t$-statistic: signal measured in noise units

> **Slower lane.** This subsection and the two after it assume less background than the rest of the lesson: if "sampling distribution" and "null hypothesis" are words you have nodded along to without ever pinning down, start here.

**The question a $t$-statistic answers is "is this number big?" — and the whole trick is that "big" is meaningless until you say *big compared to what*.**

Suppose you fit sales against ad spend across $n=12$ stores and get a slope of $\hat\beta_1 = 4.2$: each extra \$1k of ads is associated with \$4.2k more sales. Is that real, or did the fit just chase noise?

You cannot answer from $4.2$ alone. Run the same experiment in a parallel universe — 12 *different* stores drawn from the same population — and you would get a different number: maybe $3.6$, maybe $5.1$. The slope is a random quantity, because the sample is random. The spread of those hypothetical re-runs is exactly what the standard error estimates. So:

- If $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = 1.5$, re-runs would routinely land anywhere from about $1$ to $7$. A slope of $4.2$ is a meaningful distance from zero.
- If $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = 6.0$, re-runs would scatter from $-8$ to $+16$. The same $4.2$ is now indistinguishable from a fluke.

The $t$-statistic just makes that comparison explicit. It measures the estimate in standard-error units — it converts "\$4.2k per \$1k" into "how many noise-widths from zero":

$$t_j=\frac{\hat\beta_j-\beta_{j,0}}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)}$$

Here $\beta_{j,0}$ is the value the **null hypothesis** proposes — the boring claim you are trying to rule out, almost always "this predictor does nothing," $\beta_{j,0}=0$. With that null, the formula is just estimate ÷ SE:

$$t_1 = \frac{4.2}{1.5} = 2.8 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad t_1 = \frac{4.2}{6.0} = 0.7$$

Same slope, same business story, opposite verdicts. A $t$ of $2.8$ says the estimate sits nearly three noise-widths above zero, which random sampling rarely manages by accident. A $t$ of $0.7$ says it sits well inside the range noise produces all the time.

Two things a $t$-statistic is *not*. It is not a measure of effect size — a huge $t$ can accompany an economically trivial slope, if $n$ is large enough to pin down a tiny number precisely. And it is not evidence of causality; it is a statement about one coefficient inside one assumed model.

#### Why the reference curve is Student's $t$, not a Normal

Under the classical model the standardised estimate follows

$$t_j\sim t_{n-p}$$

and the reason it is a $t$ rather than a standard Normal is worth understanding, because it is a stock interview question.

If we somehow *knew* the true noise level $\sigma$, then $(\hat\beta_j-\beta_j)/\operatorname{SE}$ would be exactly standard Normal — a known constant in the denominator just rescales a Normal. But we do not know $\sigma$. We estimate it from the same small sample, as $\hat\sigma^2=\operatorname{RSS}/(n-p)$, and that estimate is itself noisy.

So the denominator wobbles too. Formally the statistic has the shape

$$t = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/\nu}},\qquad Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\quad V\sim\chi^2_\nu \ \text{independent of } Z$$

Dividing a Normal by a random, sometimes-too-small denominator produces occasional large values that a fixed denominator never would. Those extra large values are precisely the $t$ distribution's heavier tails — it is the price of not knowing $\sigma$.

<TvsNormalTails />

**Properties worth carrying into an interview.**

- **Symmetric and centred at zero**, like the Normal, and bell-shaped — the difference is entirely in the tails, not the location.
- **Heavier tails, lower peak.** More probability sits far from zero, so the same cutoff captures less area.
- **One parameter: degrees of freedom $\nu = n-p$** — the sample size minus the coefficients you spent estimating the fit.
- **Variance is $\nu/(\nu-2)$ for $\nu>2$** — always greater than the Normal's $1$, and it *blows up* as $\nu\to2$. For $\nu\le2$ the variance is infinite, and at $\nu=1$ the $t$ is the Cauchy distribution, which has no mean at all.
- **It converges to the standard Normal as $\nu\to\infty$.** By $\nu\approx30$ the two are close enough that the distinction rarely changes a decision.

That convergence is what licenses the familiar "$|t|$ above $2$ means significant" shortcut — and it also shows when the shortcut lies:

<TCriticalVsDf />

With $\nu=10$, a $t$ of exactly $2.0$ gives a two-sided $p$ of $0.073$: *not* significant at 5%, despite clearing the folk threshold. You would need $|t|>2.23$. At $\nu=1$ you would need $|t|>12.7$. The rule of thumb is a large-sample convenience, not a law — which is why the ad-spend example above, with $\nu=12-2=10$ and $t=2.8$, is genuinely significant ($p=0.019$) while a $t$ of $2.0$ from the same study would not have been.

### The $p$-value: a probability under the null, not of the null

For a two-sided test, the $p$-value is

$$p=\Pr\left(|T_{n-p}|\ge |t_{\text{observed}}|\mid H_0\right)$$

It asks: **if the true coefficient were the null value and the model assumptions held, how often would random sampling produce a $t$-statistic at least this extreme?**

Concretely: draw the curve of $t$-values the null would generate, mark where your $t$ landed, and shade everything at least that far out. The shaded area *is* the $p$-value.

<PValueAsArea />

Read the picture in the order the logic runs. You start by *assuming* the null — the curve is what the world looks like if $\beta$ really is zero. Your observed $t$ is one draw. If it lands in the fat middle, the null explains your data comfortably. If it lands out in a thin tail, then either the null is true and you got unlucky, or the null is wrong. The $p$-value only quantifies the first branch: how unlucky you would have had to be. It never tells you the probability of the second.

That asymmetry is the source of nearly every misreading below.

It is not:

- the probability that $H_0$ is true;
- the probability the result occurred “by chance”;
- the size or business importance of the effect;
- evidence of causality;
- the probability the result will replicate.

A small $p$-value is evidence against the null *within the assumed model*. With huge $n$, trivial effects can have tiny $p$-values. With small or noisy data, important effects can miss a conventional threshold. Always report the estimate and uncertainty, not a significance star alone.

### Confidence intervals: plausible parameter values on the same scale

A $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval is

$$\hat\beta_j\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-p}\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)$$

A 95% procedure means that, over repeated samples, 95% of intervals constructed this way cover the fixed true parameter. After observing one interval, frequentist language does not assign a 95% probability to the fixed $\beta$ being inside it. Practically, the interval is more informative than a binary test: it displays the effect sizes compatible with the data and assumptions.

For a two-sided test at level $\alpha$, “the interval excludes zero” and “$p<\alpha$” are the same decision.

### One worked regression-table reading

Imagine a factor loading estimate of $0.30$ with SE $0.10$:

$$t=0.30/0.10=3.0$$

The estimate is three SEs from zero, producing a small two-sided $p$-value and an approximate 95% interval $0.30\pm1.96(0.10)=[0.10,0.50]$ in a large sample. The conclusion is not “the factor definitely matters.” It is: conditional on the model, this sample is inconsistent with a zero loading, and values around $0.10$ to $0.50$ remain compatible with the data.

Now imagine adding a nearly duplicate factor. The point estimate might remain $0.30$, but its SE could jump to $0.40$. Then $t=0.75$ and the interval crosses zero. The observed association did not disappear; our ability to assign it uniquely to this factor did. That is multicollinearity, developed below.

### Six perturbations: what actually moves in the regression table

Interviewers love this family of questions because each one isolates a different part of the machinery, and because the intuitive answer is wrong about half the time. The pattern to internalise: **manipulations that add no new information leave the point estimates alone and corrupt the uncertainty.**

| Manipulation | Slopes $\hat\beta$ | Intercept | $R^2$ | Standard errors | $t$-stats | Unbiased / BLUE? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Add a pure-noise predictor | ~unchanged | ~unchanged | weakly **↑** (never ↓) | **↑** | **↓** | Yes, but less efficient |
| Duplicate a predictor column | **not identified** | unchanged | unchanged | **∞ / undefined** | undefined | Sum is estimable; individuals are not |
| Duplicate every row | **exactly unchanged** | unchanged | **exactly unchanged** | **× ≈ 1/√2** | **× ≈ √2** | Unbiased, but inference invalid |
| Duplicate one observation | **changes** | changes | changes | change | change | Unbiased; SE understated |
| Regress $x$ on $y$ instead | $\rho\,\sigma_x/\sigma_y$ (not $1/\hat\beta$) | changes | **same $\rho^2$** | change | same $t$ for $H_0$ of no relation | Answers a different question |
| Orthogonal (total) least squares | between the two OLS lines | changes | n/a | n/a | n/a | Not scale-invariant |

**1. Adding a pure-noise predictor.** $R^2$ can only go up, never down — the old fit is still available to the optimiser, so RSS cannot increase. That makes plain $R^2$ useless for model selection. Adjusted $R^2$ has a crisp rule that is worth memorising: **it increases if and only if the new variable's $|t|$ exceeds $1$** — a much lower bar than significance. The other coefficients stay unbiased (including an irrelevant regressor does not violate exogeneity), but they lose precision: you spend a degree of freedom and, unless the noise column happens to be exactly orthogonal to the rest in-sample, you inflate their variance. Estimates honest, $t$-stats quieter.

**2. Duplicating a predictor column.** Now $X$ has two identical columns, $X^\top X$ is singular, and there is no unique $\hat\beta$: if $(\beta_1,\beta_2)$ fits, so does $(\beta_1+c,\ \beta_2-c)$ for any $c$. What *is* pinned down is the column space, so the fitted values, residuals, RSS and $R^2$ are all completely unchanged. Only the attribution breaks. What software returns depends on the solver: a pseudoinverse (`numpy.linalg.lstsq`, scikit-learn) returns the minimum-norm solution, which splits the coefficient **equally** between the twins; R's `lm()` drops one and reports `NA`. Ridge also splits it equally — with an $L^2$ penalty, $(\beta/2,\beta/2)$ costs strictly less than $(\beta,0)$. Lasso, whose penalty is indifferent between those splits, has a genuinely non-unique solution and typically picks one arbitrarily. The estimable quantity throughout is the **sum** $\beta_1+\beta_2$; that combination is unbiased, and only it has a finite standard error.

**3. Duplicating every row.** Stack the dataset on top of itself. Both $X^\top X$ and $X^\top y$ exactly double, and the factor of two cancels: $\hat\beta$ is **identical**, to the last digit. TSS and RSS both double, so $R^2$ is identical too. But the variance formula sees $2n$ observations: $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta)$ picks up $(2X^\top X)^{-1}$, and

$$\frac{\operatorname{SE}_{\text{doubled}}}{\operatorname{SE}_{\text{original}}}=\sqrt{\frac{n-p}{2n-p}}\ \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\ \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$

so every standard error shrinks by about $30\%$ and every $t$-statistic inflates by about $\sqrt2$. Nothing was learned; the model merely counted the same evidence twice. The estimates stay unbiased, but the inference is fraudulent, because the errors of duplicated rows are perfectly correlated — the independence assumption behind the SE formula is gone. **This is the cleanest illustration of the whole section: point estimates survive fake data, uncertainty does not.** (It is also why clustered standard errors exist: repeated measurements on the same entity are a softer version of the same problem.)

**4. Duplicating a single observation.** Different beast. Copying one row is exactly **weighted least squares with weight 2 on that point**, so it *does* move the fit — toward that observation. How far depends on leverage: duplicating a point sitting at the centre of the $x$ cloud barely moves the line, while duplicating a high-leverage point can visibly tilt it. Everything in the table changes. The estimate remains unbiased (any fixed set of weights preserves unbiasedness given exogeneity), but if the row is a *copy* rather than a genuinely new draw, the standard errors are again understated.

> **Stop and think.** Duplicating *all* rows leaves $\hat\beta$ untouched, yet duplicating *one* row changes it. Reconcile these. *Hint: uniform reweighting rescales the normal equations by a constant that cancels; non-uniform reweighting changes their relative weighting, which is a different optimisation problem.*

**5. Regressing $x$ on $y$ instead of $y$ on $x$.** The two slopes are

$$\hat\beta_{y\sim x}=\rho\,\frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_x},\qquad \hat\beta_{x\sim y}=\rho\,\frac{\sigma_x}{\sigma_y},\qquad\text{so}\qquad \hat\beta_{y\sim x}\cdot\hat\beta_{x\sim y}=\rho^2$$

The second slope is **not** the reciprocal of the first — it equals the reciprocal only when $\rho^2=1$, i.e. a perfect fit. Since $\rho^2\le1$, each line is *flatter* than inverting the other would suggest. That gap is regression to the mean, and it has a mechanical cause: OLS minimises vertical distances, so swapping the roles of the axes minimises horizontal distances instead — a different objective, hence a different line. Both lines do pass through $(\bar x,\bar y)$, and both report the same $R^2=\rho^2$. The lesson for practice: "which variable goes on the left" is a modelling decision about what you want to predict, not a symmetric description of association.

**6. Orthogonal regression, and why PCA is a third answer.** If neither variable is naturally the response — both are noisy measurements of something — you can minimise *perpendicular* distance instead. That is **total least squares**, and its solution is the **first principal component** of the joint $(x,y)$ cloud. Its slope lies between the $y\sim x$ line and the inverted $x\sim y$ line. It is consistent under symmetric errors-in-variables — the regime where OLS instead suffers **attenuation bias**, shrinking its slope toward zero by the reliability ratio $\sigma_x^2/(\sigma_x^2+\sigma_u^2)$ because noise in $x$ inflates the denominator of the slope without touching its numerator ([derived in the quiz](/c/c1/c1.4/quiz)). The catch is that perpendicular distance mixes the two axes' units, so **TLS is not scale-invariant**: rescale $x$ from dollars to cents and the fitted line changes, whereas OLS slopes simply rescale in the obvious way. Standardise first, or do not use it.

Do not confuse that with **principal component regression**, which is a different procedure with a different purpose: project $X$ onto its top-$k$ principal components and regress $y$ on those. PCR is a variance-reduction method — biased, but stabilising when predictors are collinear, since it discards exactly the low-variance directions that inflate $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta)$. Its known weakness is that the components are chosen using $X$ alone, so a low-variance direction that happens to carry all the signal about $y$ gets thrown away; partial least squares exists to fix precisely that.

### When the usual standard error is wrong

> **Stop and think.** Your residuals are heteroskedastic (e.g. volatility clustering in returns). Is $\hat\beta$ still unbiased? What breaks? *Hint: $\hat\beta$ stays unbiased — exogeneity, not homoskedasticity, controls bias — but $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ is the wrong covariance, so your $t$-stats are wrong. Fix with **White/HC** robust standard errors (HC3 for small samples), or Newey–West (HAC) for autocorrelation.*

This distinction is central: coefficient assumptions and inference assumptions can fail separately.

- **Heteroskedasticity:** OLS may stay unbiased, but classical SEs are wrong. Use heteroskedasticity-consistent HC standard errors.
- **Autocorrelation:** time-series errors move together, so IID SEs are usually too optimistic. Use HAC/Newey–West or an explicit time-series model.
- **Clustering:** observations within a firm, user, school, or day share shocks. Cluster the SEs at the level where dependence occurs.
- **Endogeneity:** $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]\ne0$ due to omitted variables, simultaneity, selection, or measurement error. Robust SEs do not fix coefficient bias.

Robust SEs change the estimated covariance, $t$-statistics, $p$-values, and intervals. They do not change the OLS point estimates.

### $R^2$ vs adjusted $R^2$

$R^2 = 1 - \text{RSS}/\text{TSS}$ is **monotonically non-decreasing** in the number of predictors: adding *any* column (even pure noise) can only shrink the min-RSS, because you're minimizing over a larger column space. So $R^2$ rises when you add junk features. **Adjusted $R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{RSS}/(n-p)}{\text{TSS}/(n-1)}$** penalizes $p$ and *can* fall. Reporting plain $R^2$ to argue model quality is a classic tell.

$R^2$ measures the fraction of *in-sample outcome variation* accounted for by the fitted linear model. It is not accuracy, causality, calibration, or out-of-sample performance. A low $R^2$ can coexist with a precisely estimated scientific effect; a high $R^2$ can coexist with biased coefficients or terrible extrapolation.

## Part 2 — Logistic regression

> **Goal — preserve the interpretability of a linear score while producing valid probabilities, then connect that intuition to Bernoulli MLE, convexity, and IRLS.**

### Why a line is the wrong output for a binary target

If $y\in\{0,1\}$, an OLS prediction can be $-0.4$ or $1.3$. Those values are not probabilities. The error variance also depends on the mean: for a Bernoulli outcome, $\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)=p(1-p)$, so the constant-variance Gaussian story is wrong by construction.

We still want a linear, interpretable score $\eta=x^\top\beta$. We need only transform that unrestricted score into a number between 0 and 1. The sigmoid does exactly that:

$$p(x)=\sigma(\eta)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\eta}}$$

Equivalently,

$$\log\frac{p(x)}{1-p(x)}=x^\top\beta$$

Logistic regression is linear in **log-odds**, not in probability. A one-unit increase in $x_j$, holding other features fixed, adds $\beta_j$ to log-odds and multiplies odds by $e^{\beta_j}$. It does not add a constant amount to probability: the probability change is largest near $p=0.5$ and small near 0 or 1.

<Sigmoid caption="The sigmoid squashes the linear score η = xᵀβ into a probability in (0,1). The decision boundary xᵀβ = 0 corresponds exactly to p = 0.5. Read the flat tails as the source of vanishing gradients for very confident scores — and, later, as the reason perfect separation lets β run to infinity: the curve keeps getting steeper at zero without ever reaching a maximum." />

Model the log-odds as linear: $\text{logit}(p(x)) = x^\top\beta$, i.e. $p(x) = \sigma(x^\top\beta)$. The Bernoulli log-likelihood is $\ell(\beta) = \sum_i[y_i\log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)]$ — the negative of this is **binary cross-entropy** (c1.3). The score and Hessian are

$$\nabla\ell = X^\top(y - p), \qquad \nabla^2\ell = -X^\top W X,\quad W=\text{diag}(p_i(1-p_i))$$

$W \succeq 0 \Rightarrow$ the NLL is **globally convex** (no bad local minima). But the score equation $X^\top(y-p)=0$ is **nonlinear in $\beta$** (because $p=\sigma(X\beta)$), so there is **no closed form**. You solve it iteratively with Newton–Raphson, which here takes the form of **iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS)** — each step is a weighted OLS fit. The decision boundary $\{x: x^\top\beta=0\}$ is a hyperplane (linear classifier), and $e^{\beta_j}$ is the **odds ratio** for a unit increase in $x_j$.

The gradient $X^\top(y-p)$ has an intuitive reading: observed outcomes minus predicted probabilities, aggregated along each feature. At the optimum, those feature-weighted probability errors balance. The diagonal weight $p_i(1-p_i)$ is largest for uncertain examples near $0.5$ and tiny for already-certain examples. IRLS repeatedly builds a local weighted least-squares approximation, solves it, and updates the probabilities.

> **Why not squared error on $\sigma(x^\top\beta)$?** It's non-convex in $\beta$ (sigmoid inside a quadratic) and saturates (c1.3's vanishing-gradient argument). Cross-entropy is the Bernoulli MLE *and* convex — use it.

**Perfect separation breaks the MLE.** If a hyperplane perfectly separates the classes, scaling $\beta \to c\beta$ with $c\to\infty$ drives every $p_i \to \{0,1\}$ and the likelihood toward its supremum without ever attaining it — **the MLE is at infinity and does not exist** (Albert & Anderson, *Biometrika* 1984); coefficients and standard errors blow up. Fixes: any penalty (ridge), or **Firth's penalized likelihood** (a Jeffreys prior) that guarantees finite estimates. This is why an unregularized logistic fit can "diverge" on clean, easily-separable data — a counterintuitive interview favorite.

Logistic coefficients also have SEs, Wald statistics, likelihood-ratio tests, and confidence intervals, but their sampling covariance comes from inverse likelihood curvature rather than $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$. As in OLS, statistical significance does not guarantee useful classification, good calibration, or causality.

## Part 3 — Regularization (and its Bayesian meaning)

> **Goal — feel why unstable models need shrinkage before learning ridge and lasso as formulas, geometry, and priors.**

### The problem: many coefficients can explain the same sample

OLS is committed to unbiasedness. That sounds ideal until the data provide weak information about individual coefficients. With many features, little data, or correlated columns, a small change in the sample can cause a large change in $\hat\beta$. Training fit may look excellent while coefficients and future predictions swing wildly.

Imagine two almost identical predictors, $x_1$ and $x_2$. The data may strongly support the *combined* effect $\beta_1+\beta_2$, but barely distinguish how the total should be split. Solutions such as $(5,-4)$, $(2,-1)$, and $(0.6,0.4)$ can produce similar fitted values. OLS has no preference among large offsetting coefficients if they shave RSS. Regularization adds one: among similarly fitting solutions, prefer the simpler, smaller one.

The general objective is

$$\text{data-fit loss}+\lambda\times\text{coefficient complexity}$$

$\lambda$ is the strength of skepticism. At $\lambda=0$ we recover OLS; as $\lambda$ grows, coefficients are pulled harder toward zero. Choose $\lambda$ using validation or cross-validation—not the training RSS and not the test set.

> **Why shrinkage can win.** Prediction error depends on bias and variance. OLS has zero bias under its assumptions but can have enormous variance. Accepting a small systematic pull toward zero can reduce random sample-to-sample movement enough to lower total MSE.

### Ridge: share credit and stabilize

**Ridge (L2)** solves

$$\min_\beta\ \frac12\lVert y-X\beta\rVert^2+\frac\lambda2\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2$$

and has the closed form

$$\hat\beta_{\text{ridge}}=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$$

The $+\lambda I$ lifts small eigenvalues away from zero. The inverse therefore cannot explode along weakly identified directions, even when $p>n$ or $X$ is rank-deficient. Correlated features tend to **share** credit rather than receive unstable offsetting weights. Ridge usually keeps every coefficient nonzero.

Geometrically, ridge asks for the lowest-RSS contour that touches a round L2 ball. The smooth boundary rarely touches an axis, so exact zeros are rare.

<RegularizationBalls caption="The one picture that answers 'why does lasso zero coefficients but ridge doesn't?' Blue: the RSS contours are ellipses centred on β̂ᴼᴸˢ (they're circles only when features are orthogonal — see the note below). Orange: the penalty ball. The regularized solution is where the smallest RSS ellipse first touches the ball. Left: the L1 diamond has corners on the axes; a typical ellipse hits a corner, so β₂ = 0. Right: the L2 disc is smooth; a typical ellipse touches at a generic interior direction, so both coefficients stay nonzero. The corner is the mechanism — nothing about orthonormality is needed to see it." />

### Lasso: spend a limited coefficient budget

**Lasso (L1)** solves

$$\min_\beta\ \frac12\lVert y-X\beta\rVert^2+\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_1$$

The L1 constraint has diamond-shaped corners on the coordinate axes. An expanding RSS contour often touches a corner, making one or more coefficients exactly zero. Lasso therefore performs estimation and feature selection together.

There is no general closed form because $|\beta_j|$ is nondifferentiable at zero; coordinate descent and LARS are standard solvers.

> **Why orthonormal, and why doesn't anyone talk about the general case?** The reason we specialize to $X^\top X = I$ is not a modelling assumption — it's an algebraic trick to *see* the mechanism. Under a general design, RSS is a coupled quadratic in $\beta$: the elliptical RSS contours are stretched and tilted (as in the figure above), and moving $\beta_j$ changes what the optimal $\beta_k$ should be. Orthonormality diagonalizes $X^\top X$, so RSS decouples into $p$ independent scalar problems in $(\beta_j - \hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}})^2$, and each 1D subproblem has a closed form. The sparsity picture (corner-touching) is fully general — but the tidy $\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}/(1+\lambda)$ and soft-thresholding formulas only survive in the decoupled case. The general case *is* handled by the KKT subgradient condition below; it just doesn't reduce to a per-coordinate formula because the coefficients are coupled through the residual. This is exactly why textbook derivations start orthonormal: they're the shortest path to the mechanism, not the story about your actual data.

With orthonormal columns, the contrast is transparent:

$$\hat\beta_j^{\text{ridge}}=\frac{\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}}{1+\lambda}$$

$$\hat\beta_j^{\text{lasso}}=\operatorname{sign}(\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}})\max\left(|\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}|-\lambda,0\right)$$

Ridge proportionally shrinks; lasso **soft-thresholds**, subtracting a fixed amount and setting weak coefficients exactly equal to zero. Here “exactly zero” means $\hat\beta_j=0$, so feature $j$ contributes $x_j\hat\beta_j=0$ to every prediction—the fitted model has excluded that feature.

<ShrinkageCurves lambda={0.6} caption="Estimated coefficient as a function of its OLS value, under an orthonormal design and λ = 0.6. Dashed gray: OLS (identity). Blue: ridge shrinks every coordinate proportionally — the line tilts down but never touches zero for a nonzero input. Red: lasso subtracts λ, then clips to zero — every OLS coefficient in the shaded [−λ, λ] band gets snapped exactly to 0. That flat interval is the 'dead zone' the L1 kink creates; ridge has no analogue." />

**Orthonormality is only what makes the closed form simple.** Lasso can produce exact-zero coefficients for a general design matrix too. Let $r=y-X\hat\beta$ be the residual at the lasso optimum. Its KKT/subgradient condition for feature $j$ is

$$
x_j^\top r=
\begin{cases}
\lambda, & \hat\beta_j>0,\\
-\lambda, & \hat\beta_j<0,\\
\text{some value in }[-\lambda,\lambda], & \hat\beta_j=0.
\end{cases}
$$

Thus a feature whose remaining correlation with the residual is too weak to overcome the penalty, $|x_j^\top r|\le\lambda$, can optimally receive coefficient zero. Unlike the orthonormal case, the coefficients are coupled: changing one coefficient changes $r$ and therefore changes the conditions for all the others. There is no general rule that independently thresholds each OLS coefficient.

Ridge, by contrast, has a smooth penalty and **no mechanism designed to create sparsity**. Its solution is generally dense. A ridge coefficient can coincidentally equal zero because of symmetry or cancellation, so “ridge never produces zeros” is too absolute; the accurate claim is that ridge does not map a whole region of weak signals to zero and is not a feature-selection method.

With a group of highly correlated features, lasso may choose one somewhat arbitrarily and drop the rest. That can be useful for a compact predictor but dangerous for scientific interpretation: “selected” does not mean uniquely important. **Elastic net** combines L1 and L2, encouraging sparsity while making correlated features more likely to enter together.

### Scaling and the intercept

Penalties act on coefficient size, so feature scale changes the effective punishment. A coefficient for a feature measured in dollars will have a different numerical scale from one measured in millions of dollars. **Standardize predictors before ridge or lasso**, typically inside a pipeline fit only on training data. The intercept is normally not penalized; centering the data separates the baseline level from shrinkage of slopes.

**The Bayesian view (ties straight back to c1.2).** MAP estimation maximizes $\log p(y\mid\beta) + \log p(\beta)$ — the negative log-prior *is* the penalty, exactly as the negative log-likelihood is the data loss. Gaussian likelihood + **Gaussian prior** $\beta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2 I)$ → **ridge** (with $\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2$). Gaussian likelihood + **Laplace prior** → **lasso**. The Laplace prior's heavier tail and sharp peak at 0 are *why* L1 induces sparsity while L2 doesn't. So: L2 penalty ⟺ Gaussian prior, L1 penalty ⟺ Laplace prior — the same "every loss is a noise model" lens, applied to the parameters.

The prior view supplies intuition for $\lambda$: larger $\lambda$ means a tighter prior around zero—stronger prior belief that large coefficients are unlikely. MAP is still a point estimate; a full Bayesian analysis keeps the posterior distribution and propagates parameter uncertainty.

**Bias for variance — and why unbiased isn't sacred.** For a single coefficient, the decomposition is

$$
\mathbb E\left[(\hat\beta_j-\beta_j)^2\right]
=\left(\mathbb E[\hat\beta_j]-\beta_j\right)^2
+\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j)
$$

In words: coefficient MSE equals squared bias plus variance. For the full coefficient vector, the corresponding total squared-error decomposition is

$$
\mathbb E\left[\lVert\hat\beta-\beta\rVert_2^2\right]
=\left\lVert\mathbb E[\hat\beta]-\beta\right\rVert_2^2
+\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta)\right)
$$

The trace appears because $\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta)$ is a matrix; its diagonal entries are the component variances, and their sum is the estimator's total variance. Ridge is biased, but its reduction in total variance can outweigh its squared bias and produce lower total MSE than OLS. OLS minimizes variance only *among linear unbiased estimators*, an often-overlooked restriction. That shrinkage can beat the unbiased estimate on *total* MSE is a real effect, not a numerical hack. (The classic formal result is James–Stein (1961) on shrinkage dominance; for the precise inadmissibility statement and proof, ask an AI assistant like Claude or ChatGPT — an interviewer won't.) Ridge applies that bias–variance logic to regression.

Regularized coefficients do not inherit ordinary OLS $p$-values. The penalty changes their bias and sampling distribution, while data-driven tuning adds selection uncertainty. Use regularization primarily for prediction and stability; use carefully designed post-selection or Bayesian methods when inference is the goal.

## Part 4 — Multicollinearity and VIF

> **Goal — understand collinearity as an information problem, derive what VIF measures, and separate unstable explanations from potentially stable predictions.**

### The intuition: the model cannot assign credit

Suppose salary is predicted from age and years of experience. These predictors carry overlapping information. If they move almost together, the model may know that their *combined direction* predicts salary while being unable to determine which variable deserves the credit.

This is **multicollinearity**: one predictor is well approximated by a linear combination of the others. It does not mean the predictor is unrelated to $y$. It means the data contain little independent movement in that predictor after controlling for the others.

Return to the simple-regression slope intuition: denominator variation makes a coefficient estimable. In multiple regression, what matters for $\beta_j$ is the variation in $x_j$ left after removing the part predictable from all other columns. If almost nothing unique remains, a small amount of outcome noise can cause a large swing in $\hat\beta_j$.

Perfect collinearity—for example, including temperature in Celsius and Fahrenheit—makes $X^\top X$ singular. There are infinitely many coefficient vectors with identical predictions, so individual coefficients are not identified. Near-collinearity is less obvious: an answer exists, but it is unstable.

### VIF: compare actual uncertainty with an orthogonal world

The **variance inflation factor** for predictor $j$ is

$$\text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}$$

where $R_j^2$ is the $R^2$ from regressing $x_j$ on *all the other predictors*. It multiplies the coefficient's variance: $\text{Var}(\hat\beta_j) \propto \text{VIF}_j$, so $\text{SE}(\hat\beta_j)$ scales as $\sqrt{\text{VIF}_j}$. Rules of thumb: $\text{VIF}>5$ moderate, $>10$ serious ($R_j^2>0.9$); $\text{VIF}=1$ means orthogonal.

That auxiliary regression asks: **how replaceable is $x_j$ using the other features?**

- If $R_j^2=0$, none of $x_j$ is linearly predictable from the others. VIF $=1$: no inflation.
- If $R_j^2=0.8$, VIF $=5$. The coefficient variance is five times its orthogonal-design baseline and its SE is $\sqrt5\approx2.24$ times larger.
- If $R_j^2=0.99$, VIF $=100$. Only 1% of the predictor's variance is unique; its SE is inflated tenfold.

The useful identity is

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j\mid X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{\operatorname{SST}_j(1-R_j^2)}$$

where $\operatorname{SST}_j=\sum_i(x_{ij}-\bar x_j)^2$. Uncertainty grows because of noisy outcomes ($\sigma^2$), little raw variation in the predictor (small SST), or little *unique* variation (large $R_j^2$). VIF isolates the third cause.

Thresholds such as 5 and 10 are triage rules, not laws. A high VIF is evidence about the design matrix, not an automatic command to delete a variable. Context decides whether individual coefficient attribution matters.

**Inference vs prediction.** Collinearity inflates coefficient variances → **unstable, sign-flipping coefficients and wide confidence intervals** → coefficient *inference* is wrecked. But the model's *predictions* on similar $X$ can be perfectly fine — collinearity introduces no bias, it just makes the individual coefficients un-identifiable. A tree model doesn't care for prediction at all (it just splits on one of the redundant features). This is the precise resolution to "is multicollinearity bad?": **bad for interpretation, often harmless for prediction.** (This connects directly to c1.1: redundant/collinear features are exactly what defeats the drop-column leakage test and split tree-importance.)

Why can predictions survive? If two features move together in both train and test data, many unstable coefficient splits yield nearly the same combined prediction. Prediction becomes fragile when the future breaks that correlation or when we extrapolate in a direction the training data never explored.

### What to do about high VIF

The response follows the goal:

- **Inference on a specific coefficient:** collect data with more independent variation, remove a genuinely redundant measurement, combine variables using domain knowledge, or redesign the study. No algebra can create missing information.
- **Prediction:** validate out of sample. Ridge often stabilizes predictions without discarding useful signal.
- **A group-level question:** jointly test related coefficients with an $F$-test rather than demanding that each be individually significant.
- **Dimensionality reduction:** PCA/PCR can replace correlated features with orthogonal components, at the cost of original-feature interpretation.

Do not mechanically drop the feature with the largest VIF until all VIFs fall below a threshold. Removing a confounder merely to reduce VIF can introduce omitted-variable bias. Centering helps with nonessential collinearity in polynomial or interaction terms, but it does not remove genuine redundancy between distinct variables.

## Part 5 — PCA and principal-component regression

> **Goal — see PCA first as a rotation that finds the data cloud's main axes, then connect variance, reconstruction, PCR, and ridge through SVD.**

### The picture: rotate the coordinate system

Imagine a two-dimensional cloud where height and weight rise together. The original horizontal and vertical axes are not aligned with the cloud. One diagonal direction captures most of its spread; the perpendicular direction captures the small remaining spread.

PCA rotates to those directions:

- **PC1** is the unit direction along which projected data have maximum variance.
- **PC2** is the highest-variance unit direction orthogonal to PC1.
- Later PCs continue the same rule, each orthogonal to those before it.

PCA does not initially “delete features.” It creates a new orthogonal coordinate system. Dimensionality reduction happens only when we keep the first $m$ component scores and discard the rest.

<PCARotation caption="A correlated 2D cloud with the two principal directions overlaid. PC1 points along the direction of largest projected variance; PC2 is orthogonal to it and captures what's left. Arrow lengths scale with the sample standard deviations along each axis, so the length ratio is the story: nearly all the spread lives on PC1. Keeping only PC1 replaces two correlated features with one uncorrelated summary — cheap compression, but 'high variance ≠ predictive of y', which is why PCR is target-blind." />

Why keep high-variance directions? For a fixed number of linear dimensions, they minimize squared reconstruction error. The discarded variance is exactly the information lost under that geometric criterion. But reconstruction of $X$ is not prediction of $y$—the crucial limitation.

#### The two objectives are the same objective

PCA gets described two different ways — "find the direction of maximum variance" and "find the line that the points are closest to" — and it is not obvious those are the same instruction. They are, and the reason is Pythagoras.

Center the data so the centroid is the origin. Fix a unit direction $v$. Every point splits into a piece along $v$ and a piece perpendicular to it, and those two pieces are the legs of a right triangle with $x_i$ as the hypotenuse:

$$\lVert x_i\rVert^2=\underbrace{(v^\top x_i)^2}_{\text{along }v}+\underbrace{d_i^2}_{\text{perpendicular}}$$

Sum over all points. The left side, $\sum_i\lVert x_i\rVert^2$, does not depend on $v$ at all — it is a property of the data. So the two terms on the right trade off exactly: every unit of squared projection you gain is a unit of squared distance you lose.

$$\max_{\lVert v\rVert=1}\ \sum_i(v^\top x_i)^2\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \min_{\lVert v\rVert=1}\ \sum_id_i^2$$

<PCAPointSpace caption="The same 70-point cloud, twice, with the same PC1 line drawn through the centroid (red dot). Left: each gray point drops to a blue dot on the line — that is its score vᵀxᵢ — and the blue double arrow spans ±2 standard deviations of those scores. PCA maximizes their spread. Right: the identical drops, drawn as the segments they are. PCA minimizes the sum of their squared lengths. The orange point is annotated in both panels: its score on the left, its perpendicular distance dᵢ on the right, with the right-angle marker showing the two are legs of one right triangle. The dashed gray segment is the vertical gap — that is the kind of distance OLS minimizes, to its own different line, which is why the OLS fit and PC1 are not the same line." />

This is also the cleanest way to see why PCA is not regression. Both fit a line through a cloud, but they measure error along different directions: OLS measures **vertically**, because $y$ is special and $x$ is taken as given. PCA measures **perpendicularly**, because no variable is special — it is describing the cloud, not predicting one coordinate from the others. Swap $y$ and $x$ and the OLS line changes; the PCA line does not.

#### The dual picture: feature space

The scatter plot above is **point space** — $n$ points living in $\mathbb R^d$, one axis per feature. There is a second, less familiar picture that the same matrix supports, and it is worth carrying because some facts are obvious in it that are invisible in the scatter.

Turn the matrix on its side. Instead of $n$ points in $d$ dimensions, look at $d$ vectors in $\mathbb R^n$ — one arrow per *column* of $X$, living in a space whose axes are the observations. This is **feature space** (classically, "variable space").

Two facts make it readable. For centered columns,

$$\lVert x_j\rVert^2=\sum_i x_{ij}^2=(n-1)s_j^2,\qquad \cos\angle(x_j,x_k)=\frac{x_j^\top x_k}{\lVert x_j\rVert\,\lVert x_k\rVert}=r_{jk}$$

So **length is standard deviation and angle is correlation**. Two strongly correlated features are two long arrows at a narrow angle. Two uncorrelated features are perpendicular. A feature with no variance is a point at the origin.

<PCAFeatureSpace caption="Two standardized features with correlation r = 0.8, drawn as vectors in observation space. Because they are standardized the arrows have equal length, so the correlation shows up purely as the angle between them: cos θ = 0.8, about 37°. PCA finds an orthogonal pair inside the plane they span. z₁ bisects them and is long; z₂ is perpendicular and short. For this matrix λ₁ = 1.8 and λ₂ = 0.2, so z₁ is exactly √(1.8/0.2) = 3 times the length of z₂ — the whole compression argument, visible as a length ratio." />

Read the compression story off the picture. The two feature arrows nearly coincide, so almost everything they span is captured by one direction; $z_2$ is the small leftover. Push $r_{12}\to1$ and the arrows merge, $\lambda_2\to0$, and the second component vanishes — perfect collinearity is *literally* two arrows lying on top of each other, with a genuinely one-dimensional span. Push $r_{12}\to0$ and the arrows go perpendicular, $\lambda_1=\lambda_2=1$, and there is no preferred direction to find: PCA has nothing to compress.

The final row of the figure is the conservation law that makes PCA a rotation rather than a reduction. Since $V$ is orthogonal it preserves total squared length,

$$\sum_j\lVert x_j\rVert^2=\sum_k\lVert z_k\rVert^2$$

so the components redistribute variance among directions without creating or destroying any of it. Reduction happens later, and only when you delete columns.

### The mechanics: center, usually scale, then decompose

Center $X$, then take the **SVD**

$$X=UDV^\top$$

Equivalently, eigendecompose the sample covariance:

$$\frac{1}{n-1}X^\top X=V\Lambda V^\top$$

The columns $v_j$ of $V$ are the **loadings** or principal directions. The new feature values are the **scores** $z_j=Xv_j=d_ju_j$. The eigenvalue $\lambda_j=d_j^2/(n-1)$ is the variance captured by component $j$, so its explained-variance ratio is

$$\frac{d_j^2}{\sum_kd_k^2}$$

Centering is mandatory because PCA describes variation around the mean. Scaling is a modeling choice but usually essential when units differ: without standardization, a feature measured in dollars can dominate one measured in percentages simply because its numerical variance is larger. Fit the scaler and PCA on training data only; fitting them before a split leaks validation/test distribution information.

The sign of a component is arbitrary: $v_j$ and $-v_j$ describe the same axis. Interpret relative loading magnitudes and directions, not the sign in isolation.

### How the decomposition is actually computed

"Eigendecompose the covariance matrix" hides the part that matters. You do **not** solve the eigenvalue equation symbolically. You turn it into an iterative optimization problem. Because the covariance matrix is symmetric positive semidefinite, that turns out to be especially clean.

The whole hierarchy below comes from one idea: **repeated linear transformations reveal invariant directions.** Everything else is bookkeeping to make that idea fast and to stop the directions collapsing onto each other.

#### PCA is the maximizer of a quadratic form

Let

$$C=\frac{1}{n}X^\top X$$

be the covariance matrix. The first principal component solves

$$\max_{\lVert v\rVert=1} v^\top Cv$$

Theory says the answer is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue. But suppose you do not know the eigenvectors. How do you find it numerically?

Because $C$ is symmetric it has an orthonormal eigenbasis,

$$C=V\Lambda V^\top,\qquad \lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_d\ge0$$

Take literally any starting vector $q_0$. The eigenvectors form a basis, so

$$q_0=c_1v_1+c_2v_2+\cdots+c_dv_d$$

Multiply by $C$. Each eigenvector is just scaled by its own eigenvalue:

$$Cq_0=c_1\lambda_1v_1+c_2\lambda_2v_2+\cdots+c_d\lambda_dv_d$$

Multiply again:

$$C^2q_0=c_1\lambda_1^2v_1+c_2\lambda_2^2v_2+\cdots+c_d\lambda_d^2v_d$$

After $t$ multiplications:

$$C^tq_0=c_1\lambda_1^tv_1+c_2\lambda_2^tv_2+\cdots+c_d\lambda_d^tv_d$$

Factor out $\lambda_1^t$:

$$C^tq_0=\lambda_1^t\left[c_1v_1+c_2\left(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right)^tv_2+\cdots\right]$$

If $\lambda_1>\lambda_2$ then every ratio $(\lambda_j/\lambda_1)^t\to0$. So after normalizing,

$$\boxed{\ \frac{C^tq_0}{\lVert C^tq_0\rVert}\longrightarrow\pm v_1\ }$$

provided $c_1\neq0$. That is **power iteration**. There is no determinant anywhere.

#### The algorithm for the top direction

Start with any unit vector $q$. Repeat:

$$z=Cq,\qquad q\leftarrow\frac{z}{\lVert z\rVert}$$

Eventually $q\approx v_1$.

How do you get the eigenvalue? Once $q\approx v_1$ we have $Cq\approx\lambda_1q$. Left-multiply by $q^\top$:

$$q^\top Cq\approx\lambda_1q^\top q=\lambda_1$$

because $q$ is normalized. So

$$\boxed{\ \lambda_1\approx q^\top Cq\ }$$

This is the **Rayleigh quotient**. The whole top eigenpair falls out of *multiply, normalize, repeat*.

The convergence rate is $\lvert\lambda_2/\lambda_1\rvert$ per step, which tells you exactly when this is slow: when the top two eigenvalues are close, the second term dies slowly and you need many iterations to separate the directions.

#### A concrete run

Take

$$C=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}$$

and pretend you know nothing about its eigenvalues. Start from $q_0=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}$.

$$Cq_0=\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}\quad\Rightarrow\quad q_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

Keep multiplying, ignoring normalization for a moment since it does not change direction:

$$C\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}10\\6\end{bmatrix},\qquad C\begin{bmatrix}10\\6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}36\\28\end{bmatrix},\qquad C\begin{bmatrix}36\\28\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}136\\120\end{bmatrix}$$

Watch the ratio of the two entries: $1/3$, then $0.6$, then $0.78$, then $120/136\approx0.88$. It is heading to $1$, so the direction is heading to $\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}$. Normalized,

$$v_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad v_1^\top Cv_1=4$$

The top eigenpair, found numerically, with no polynomial solved. (The other eigenpair here is $\lambda_2=2$ at $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}[1,-1]^\top$, so the convergence rate was $\lambda_2/\lambda_1=0.5$ — the error halves each step, which is what the table of ratios shows.)

#### PCA needs several eigenvectors, so stop them collapsing

Power-iterate several vectors independently and they all converge to $v_1$. You need something that stops them landing on the same direction.

Say you want $k$ components. Instead of one vector, keep a matrix of $k$ orthonormal columns:

$$Q=\begin{bmatrix}q_1&q_2&\cdots&q_k\end{bmatrix}$$

Multiply all of them at once, $Z=CQ$. Every column starts drifting toward $v_1$, so immediately re-orthogonalize the columns by taking a QR decomposition $Z=QR$, throwing away $R$ and keeping the new orthonormal $Q$. Repeat:

$$\boxed{\ Z_t=CQ_t,\qquad Q_{t+1}=\operatorname{orth}(Z_t)\ }$$

This is **orthogonal iteration**, also called simultaneous power iteration. Why does it work? If the starting block is $Q_0=VA$ — its columns are combinations of eigenvectors — then

$$C^tQ_0=V\Lambda^tA$$

and because $\lambda_1^t,\ldots,\lambda_k^t$ dominate the smaller powers, the *subspace* spanned by those columns converges to $\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_k)$. The QR step is what keeps the individual columns from all becoming the same vector.

That is already a working algorithm for PCA's top $k$ directions.

#### The same idea, rewritten, is the QR eigensolver

Now suppose you want *all* eigenvalues. Start with $A_0=C$, factor it, then multiply the factors back in the opposite order:

$$A_0=Q_0R_0,\qquad A_1=R_0Q_0$$

Why would reversing the factors help? Because $A_0=Q_0R_0$ implies $R_0=Q_0^\top A_0$, and therefore

$$\boxed{\ A_1=R_0Q_0=Q_0^\top A_0Q_0\ }$$

That is a **similarity transformation**, and similarity transformations preserve eigenvalues. The one-line proof: if $Av=\lambda v$ and $B=Q^\top AQ$, set $w=Q^\top v$. Then

$$Bw=Q^\top AQQ^\top v=Q^\top Av=\lambda Q^\top v=\lambda w$$

so $A$ and $B$ have exactly the same eigenvalues. Repeat the step:

$$A_t=Q_tR_t,\qquad A_{t+1}=R_tQ_t$$

For a symmetric matrix the result is remarkable — the iterates converge to

$$A_t\longrightarrow\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1&&0\\&\ddots&\\0&&\lambda_d\end{bmatrix}$$

The eigenvalues literally appear on the diagonal. That is the **QR algorithm**.

#### Why it drives toward diagonal form

Repeated QR iteration is closely related to running simultaneous power iteration on all the basis vectors at once. In simultaneous power iteration, $C^tQ_0$ becomes dominated by the eigenvectors of the largest eigenvalues; QR repeatedly extracts an orthonormal basis for those increasingly eigenvector-aligned directions.

If you accumulate the rotations,

$$\bar Q_t=Q_0Q_1\cdots Q_t$$

then the algorithm is effectively driving $\bar Q_t\to V$, the eigenvector matrix. So

$$\bar Q_t^\top C\bar Q_t\longrightarrow V^\top CV$$

and since $CV=V\Lambda$,

$$V^\top CV=V^\top V\Lambda=\Lambda$$

giving

$$\boxed{\ \bar Q_t^\top C\bar Q_t\longrightarrow\Lambda\ }$$

The QR algorithm is iteratively rotating the coordinate system until the matrix becomes diagonal. That is the entire idea.

#### The cost problem, and how symmetry fixes it

A dense $d\times d$ QR decomposition costs about $O(d^3)$. Doing that at every iteration would be wasteful. But covariance matrices are symmetric, and you can exploit that *before* iterating.

Transform $C$ into a **tridiagonal** matrix

$$T=\begin{bmatrix}a_1&b_1&0&\cdots\\b_1&a_2&b_2&\cdots\\0&b_2&a_3&\cdots\\\vdots&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}$$

built so that $T=Q^\top CQ$ — again a similarity transformation, so $T$ has exactly the same eigenvalues as $C$.

#### Householder reflections do the tridiagonalization

Suppose the first column of $C$ is $[c_{11},x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots]^\top$. You want to reflect the lower piece $x=[x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots]^\top$ onto the first coordinate direction:

$$Hx=\begin{bmatrix}\pm\lVert x\rVert\\0\\0\\\vdots\end{bmatrix}$$

Construct $u=x\pm\lVert x\rVert e_1$, normalize to $v=u/\lVert u\rVert$, and define

$$H=I-2vv^\top$$

Why is that a reflection? Split any vector into parts parallel and perpendicular to $v$: $x=x_\perp+\alpha v$. Then since $v^\top x_\perp=0$ and $\lVert v\rVert=1$,

$$Hx=(I-2vv^\top)(x_\perp+\alpha v)=x_\perp+\alpha v-2\alpha v=x_\perp-\alpha v$$

It leaves the perpendicular component alone and flips the parallel one. Choose $v$ and you can zero every entry below whichever entry you want to keep.

Apply the reflection on both sides. Since $H^\top=H$,

$$C_1=H^\top CH=HCH$$

which is a similarity transformation, so eigenvalues are preserved. Work column by column, $C\to C_1\to C_2\to\cdots\to T$. Because the matrix is symmetric, killing the entries below the subdiagonal simultaneously kills the matching entries above the superdiagonal:

$$\boxed{\ \text{symmetric dense matrix}\longrightarrow\text{symmetric tridiagonal matrix}\ }$$

#### Why tridiagonal is such a big win

A dense matrix has roughly $d^2$ nonzero entries. A tridiagonal one has about $3d$. Matrix-vector multiplication drops from $O(d^2)$ to $O(d)$, and the QR steps exploit the same sparsity.

So the expensive part of a full dense eigendecomposition is the **one-time** reduction $C\to T$ at $O(d^3)$. Everything after that is vastly cheaper. This is why the standard symmetric eigensolver is "reduce, then iterate" rather than "iterate on the dense matrix."

#### PCA usually does not want all the eigenvectors

Usually $k\ll d$. You might have $10{,}000$ features and want $50$ components. Computing all $10{,}000$ eigenvectors to throw away $9{,}950$ of them is wasteful. You only want the subspace $\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_k)$.

Power iteration already told you something useful: the sequence $q,\ Cq,\ C^2q,\ C^3q,\ldots$ carries progressively more information about the dominant eigenvectors. So keep all of it. Form the **Krylov subspace**

$$\boxed{\ \mathcal K_m(C,q)=\operatorname{span}\lbrace q,Cq,C^2q,\ldots,C^{m-1}q\rbrace\ }$$

Instead of solving a $d\times d$ eigenproblem, find an orthonormal basis $Q_m$ for this much smaller $m$-dimensional space and project $C$ into it:

$$T_m=Q_m^\top CQ_m$$

Now $T_m$ is only $m\times m$. Solve the small problem $T_my=\theta y$, then lift the answer back:

$$v\approx Q_my$$

That is the core of **Lanczos**: use repeated $Cq$ operations to discover the small subspace containing the dominant eigenvectors, then solve the eigenproblem only inside that subspace. Because $C$ is symmetric, the orthogonalization simplifies dramatically and $T_m$ comes out tridiagonal on its own.

#### PCA lets you skip building $C$ entirely

Recall $C=\frac1nX^\top X$. Lanczos only ever needs the *action* of $C$ on a vector, never $C$ itself. And

$$Cv=\frac1nX^\top Xv=\frac1nX^\top(Xv)$$

So you can go $v\xrightarrow{\ X\ }Xv\xrightarrow{\ X^\top\ }X^\top Xv$ without ever forming the $d\times d$ covariance matrix.

This is a large practical win. If $d$ is $10{,}000$, then $C$ is a hundred million entries you never have to store. It is also more numerically accurate: forming $X^\top X$ squares the condition number, so you lose roughly half your significant digits before you start. This is exactly why PCA implementations run SVD on $X$ directly rather than eigendecomposing $X^\top X$, and it is what truncated and randomized SVD solvers are built on.

#### The hierarchy to keep in your head

PCA asks for $\max_{\lVert v\rVert=1}v^\top Cv$, which is an eigenvector problem. But numerically you never solve $\det(C-\lambda I)=0$. Instead:

| Goal | Method | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| Top eigenpair | Power iteration | Repeated multiplication amplifies the dominant eigendirection |
| Top $k$ | Orthogonal iteration | Power iteration on a block, plus QR to stop columns collapsing |
| All eigenvalues | QR algorithm | Write that iteration as repeated similarity transforms |
| Full symmetric problem | Householder $\to$ tridiagonal, then QR | Pay $O(d^3)$ once so every iteration is cheap |
| Top $k$, $d$ huge | Krylov / Lanczos | Solve the eigenproblem inside a small subspace |
| PCA specifically | Apply it to $X$, not $X^\top X$ | Only $Xv$ and $X^\top u$ are ever needed |

The deepest idea is not "find the roots of the characteristic polynomial." It is that **repeated linear transformations reveal invariant directions**, and every algorithm above is a way of exploiting that without letting the directions collapse or the cost explode.

### PCA versus feature selection

Feature selection keeps original columns; PCA replaces them with mixtures. Ten correlated measurements may compress into one stable component, but “PC1 increased” is harder to explain than “revenue increased.” PCA is attractive for compression, visualization, de-noising, and conditioning; it is costly when original-variable interpretation matters.

### Principal-component regression: predict after rotating

**PCR** performs PCA on $X$, keeps the top $m$ scores, then regresses $y$ on those scores. Orthogonality removes collinearity, and choosing $m<p$ reduces variance. It is a hard decision: directions $1$ through $m$ get full access to the fit; directions after $m$ get none.

The number of components is a supervised hyperparameter even though the PCA transformation is unsupervised. Choose $m$ by cross-validation on the full pipeline, not solely by a “95% explained variance” rule. Explained variance concerns reconstruction of $X$, not predictive information about $y$.

**Ridge is "soft PCR."** With $X=UDV^\top$, the ridge fit is

$$\hat y_{\text{ridge}} = \sum_{j=1}^p u_j\,\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}\,u_j^\top y$$

OLS uses shrinkage factor $1$ on every principal direction; ridge multiplies direction $j$ by $\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}\in(0,1)$ — so it **shrinks most along the low-variance (small $d_j$) directions**, which are exactly the unstable, collinear ones. **Principal-component regression (PCR)** is the hard-threshold version: keep the top $m$ components fully, drop the rest. This is the exact sense in which "ridge ≈ smooth PCR."

This spectral view explains regularization at a deeper level. OLS divides by singular values; a very small $d_j$ can amplify noise enormously. Ridge replaces the dangerous inversion with a shrinkage factor that approaches 0 for weak directions and 1 for strong directions. PCR uses a step function; ridge uses a smooth dial. Neither asks whether the direction predicts $y$ before deciding how strongly to retain it, although ridge's $\lambda$ is chosen using predictive validation.

**When PCA hurts.** PCA is **unsupervised — target-blind**. It keeps high-*variance* directions, which may be exactly the *irrelevant* ones; the predictive signal can live in a low-variance component that PCA discards. It also assumes linearity and "variance = importance," and destroys interpretability (components are blends of features). It helps when collinearity/dimensionality is the problem *and* signal aligns with high-variance directions; otherwise supervised methods (PLS, or just ridge) are better.

An interview-quality counterexample: let a nuisance feature vary wildly while a low-variance sensor almost perfectly determines the target. PC1 follows the nuisance variance; truncating to PC1 destroys the signal. PCA worked perfectly at its stated job—reconstructing $X$—and failed at the different job we cared about.

PCA also captures only linear directions, can be dominated by outliers, and makes components dependent on the training distribution. When feature covariance shifts, old loadings may no longer summarize the new population well.

## The stats / quant-research lens (read this before a quant interview)

- **Estimator ladder.** *Unbiased* ($\mathbb{E}[\hat\beta]=\beta$), *consistent* ($\hat\beta\to^p\beta$), *efficient* (attains the Cramér–Rao bound; MLE is asymptotically efficient), *BLUE* (min-variance among linear unbiased — Gauss–Markov). The gaps matter: unbiased $\not\Rightarrow$ consistent and vice-versa; BLUE is "best" only within the linear class — a biased nonlinear estimator (ridge) can beat it in MSE.
- **Factor models are OLS.** CAPM / Fama–French / Carhart: $r_i - r_f = \alpha + \beta\cdot(\text{factors}) + \varepsilon$. The "betas" are regression slopes; $\alpha$ (the intercept) is tested for $\ne 0$ via its $t$-stat. Value/quality/size factors are correlated → high VIF → **unstable, sign-flipping factor loadings** even when portfolio *prediction* is fine.
- **Heteroskedastic, autocorrelated returns** → must use White (HC) or Newey–West (HAC) standard errors, or you "discover" spurious alpha.
- **Overfit backtests.** More factors mechanically raise in-sample $R^2$ → inflated backtest Sharpe. This is the multiple-testing / data-snooping problem — cross-reference c1.1 leakage, adjusted $R^2$, and CV.

## Tradeoff matrix

| Method | Estimates | Closed form? | Handles collinearity | Sparse? | Bayesian prior |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| OLS | Conditional mean (Gaussian MLE) | Yes | No (singular if rank-deficient) | No | None (flat) |
| Ridge | Shrunk mean | Yes: $(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$ | Yes (always invertible) | No | Gaussian |
| Lasso | Sparse mean | No (coordinate descent) | Partially | Yes | Laplace |
| Logistic | $P(y=1\mid x)$ (Bernoulli MLE) | No (IRLS) | via penalty | via L1 | via penalty |
| PCR | Mean on top-$m$ PCs | Yes (per $m$) | Yes (drops low-variance) | No (in PC space) | — |

## Worked decision walk-throughs

**Scenario A: you need interpretable coefficients with $p$-values (which factors matter?).** Use **statsmodels OLS** (or Logit) — it reports SEs, $t$-stats, $p$-values, CIs. Check VIFs first; if factors are collinear, the individual coefficients are untrustworthy even if the fit is good.

**Scenario B: $p > n$ or heavy collinearity, you care about prediction.** **Ridge.** It's always invertible and shrinks the unstable low-variance directions. If you also want feature selection, **lasso** or **elastic net**.

**Scenario C: binary classification, want probabilities + interpretable odds ratios.** Logistic regression. Watch for **perfect separation** (regularize or use Firth) and remember sklearn's default is *penalized* (below) — calibrate if you need true probabilities.

**Scenario D: high-dimensional correlated features, mostly nuisance variance.** Consider **PCA/PCR** — but verify the target correlates with the top components (it's target-blind); ridge is often the safer "soft PCR."

## Production failure modes

1. **`sklearn.LogisticRegression` is regularized by default.** It applies an **L2 penalty with `C=1.0`** out of the box — it is *not* textbook MLE. For unregularized logistic regression use `statsmodels.Logit`, or sklearn `C=np.inf`. (Version note: in **scikit-learn 1.8** the `penalty` parameter is **deprecated in favor of `l1_ratio`** — `penalty='l2'`→`l1_ratio=0`, `'l1'`→`1`, `None`→`C=np.inf` — with removal in 1.10. Most environments are still on ≤1.7 where `penalty=` is live; check the installed version.)
2. **Wanting $p$-values from sklearn.** sklearn gives a penalized point estimate and **no inference** (no SEs/$p$-values). Use statsmodels for inference; remember to **`add_constant`** (statsmodels does *not* add an intercept, unlike sklearn).
3. **VIF computed without an intercept.** `statsmodels`' `variance_inflation_factor` needs a constant column added first, or the VIFs are wrong (the implicit regressions are forced through the origin).
4. **Ridge vs Lasso $\alpha$ aren't on the same scale.** sklearn `Ridge` puts `alpha` on the raw RSS; `Lasso`/`ElasticNet` carry a $1/(2n)$ factor. Don't copy an $\alpha$ between them.
5. **Reporting in-sample $R^2$ as model quality.** It only goes up with more features; use adjusted $R^2$ or out-of-sample/CV.
6. **Unregularized logistic on separable data diverges.** Coefficients run to $\pm\infty$; add a penalty or use Firth.

## Interview gotchas

- **"Is OLS the minimum-variance unbiased estimator, and does it need Gaussian errors?"** — It's BLUE (best *linear* unbiased) by Gauss–Markov; normality is **not** required for BLUE, only for finite-sample $t$/$F$ inference. A nonlinear or biased estimator can beat it.
- **"When is a biased estimator better than OLS?"** — bias-variance: ridge trades bias for a larger variance cut → lower MSE. (The formal shrinkage-dominance result is James–Stein; ask an AI for the precise statement — an interviewer won't.)
- **"Why does logistic regression have no closed form, and is the objective convex?"** — score equation is nonlinear in $\beta$ (no closed form); Hessian $-X^\top W X \preceq 0$ for the log-likelihood → NLL convex → IRLS/Newton finds the global optimum.
- **"What happens under perfect separation?"** — MLE diverges to $\pm\infty$; regularize or use Firth. (Cite Albert–Anderson.)
- **"Ridge vs lasso — and what priors are they?"** — L2 shrinks / Gaussian prior; L1 zeros / Laplace prior; show soft-thresholding.
- **"Two predictors have VIF 50 — what's affected?"** — coefficient SEs ($\times\sqrt{50}$), sign stability, interpretation — but not necessarily prediction.
- **"How does ridge relate to PCA?"** — ridge is soft PCR: it shrinks most along low-variance principal directions; PCR hard-drops them.
