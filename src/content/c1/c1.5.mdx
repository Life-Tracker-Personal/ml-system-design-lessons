## TL;DR

PCA is one move: **rotate the coordinate system so the axes line up with the data's own directions of spread**, then keep the first few. Everything worth knowing follows from that move plus one fact — the rotation is chosen to reconstruct $X$, and it never looks at $y$. Maximizing projected variance and minimizing squared reconstruction error turn out to be the same objective. The eigendecomposition that finds the axes is never computed the way a linear-algebra course teaches. Ridge turns out to be a soft version of the same truncation. And the failure that matters — PCA deleting exactly the signal you needed — is not a bug but the direct consequence of optimizing for the wrong target.

- **State** the exact ceiling on how many nonzero components can exist, and say what it implies when $d \gg n$.
- **Derive** how an eigendecomposition is actually computed, from power iteration through orthogonal iteration, QR, Householder tridiagonalization and Lanczos.
- **Explain** why implementations run SVD on $X$ rather than eigendecomposing $X^\top X$, on both memory and accuracy grounds.
- **Decide** whether to center, whether to scale, and how many components to keep — and say why "95% of the variance" is the wrong criterion for a supervised pipeline.
- **Diagnose** loadings that flip sign or reshuffle between retrainings, and name the two distinct mechanisms.
- **Relate** PCA, principal-component regression and ridge — and say when PCA helps a model and when it quietly hurts.
- **Construct** a dataset where keeping the top components destroys predictive power, and name the supervised method built to fix it.
- **Judge** when PCA is the wrong tool entirely: one-hot categoricals, tree models, and interpretation-critical settings.
- **Read** the components of a financial return matrix as factors, and say what PC1 almost always is and why.

## Why this one earns a lesson

PCA is the most-used and most-misused preprocessing step in applied machine learning, and it sits at the exact intersection of the two things a quant or research interview probes hardest: linear algebra you can actually execute, and estimator behavior you can reason about. "Explain PCA" is a warm-up. The questions that follow it are not — *how would you actually compute those eigenvalues*, *how many components can exist*, *why did the loadings change overnight*, *what is PC1 of a return matrix* — and each has a precise answer that most candidates do not have.

It also has the highest ratio of confident-sounding wrong answers of anything in this chapter. PCA is not feature selection. Explained variance is not predictive power. The sign of a loading means nothing. Ninety-five percent is not a criterion. Each of those is a sentence you will hear in a real code review, and each is wrong for a reason worth being able to state.

> **Vocabulary primer.** *Covariance matrix* — the $d\times d$ table of pairwise covariances between features; its diagonal holds the variances. *Eigenvector / eigenvalue* — a direction the matrix only stretches rather than rotates, and the factor by which it stretches. *SVD* — the singular value decomposition $X=UDV^\top$, a factorization every real matrix has. *Singular value* — a diagonal entry of $D$; its square is proportional to a covariance eigenvalue. *Loadings* — the columns of $V$, meaning the recipe that mixes original features into a component. *Scores* — the new coordinates $Xv_j$, one number per row per component. *Rank* — the number of linearly independent directions a matrix actually spans. *Orthogonal* — at right angles, so the components carry non-overlapping information. *PCR* — principal-component regression, ordinary regression run on the scores.

> **Goal — see PCA first as a rotation that finds the data cloud's main axes, then connect variance, reconstruction, PCR, and ridge through SVD.**

### The picture: rotate the coordinate system

Imagine a two-dimensional cloud where height and weight rise together. Taller people weigh more, on average, so the points do not form a round blob. They form a tilted ellipse.

Now look at where your axes are pointing. The original horizontal and vertical axes are not aligned with the cloud. Horizontal means height, vertical means weight, and the cloud runs diagonally between them. One diagonal direction captures most of its spread; the perpendicular direction captures the small remaining spread.

Those two axes came from how somebody chose to record the measurements. Nothing about the cloud asked for them. So try the obvious thing: pick new axes that do line up with the cloud.

PCA rotates to those directions:

- **PC1** is the unit direction along which projected data have maximum variance.
- **PC2** is the highest-variance unit direction orthogonal to PC1.
- Later PCs continue the same rule, each orthogonal to those before it.

Every word of that first bullet is load-bearing. Pick a direction. Drop every point straight down onto it, the way a flagpole casts a shadow at noon. Each point is now a single number: how far along that direction its shadow landed. Those numbers have a spread, and the spread has a variance. PC1 is whichever direction makes that variance as large as it can be. PC2 asks the same question again, with the extra rule that it must sit at a right angle to PC1.

Count what you have after the rotation. Two features before, two coordinates after. Nothing was thrown away. PCA by itself deletes no features. It creates a new orthogonal coordinate system. Dimensionality reduction happens only when we keep the first $m$ component scores, for some chosen $m$ smaller than the number of original features, and discard the rest.

<PCARotation caption="A correlated 2D cloud with the two principal directions overlaid. Look at the two arrows before anything else. PC1 points along the direction of largest projected variance; PC2 is orthogonal to it and captures what's left. Arrow lengths scale with the sample standard deviations along each axis, so the length ratio is the story: nearly all the spread lives on PC1. Keeping only PC1 replaces two correlated features with one uncorrelated summary — cheap compression, but 'high variance ≠ predictive of y', which is why PCR is target-blind." />

Why keep the high-variance directions in particular, rather than any other pair of axes? Because of a guarantee. For a fixed number of linear dimensions, they minimize squared reconstruction error. Keep $m$ of them, throw the rest away, rebuild each point from what you kept, and no other choice of $m$ linear directions would have rebuilt the points more accurately. The discarded variance is exactly the information lost under that geometric criterion. The Pythagorean argument in the next subsection is where the guarantee comes from. But rebuilding the feature matrix $X$ — the table of $n$ observations by $d$ features that PCA is given — is a different job from predicting a target $y$, and that gap is the crucial limitation. Hold on to that sentence: the gap between reconstructing $X$ and predicting $y$ is where every PCA failure comes from.

#### The two objectives are the same objective

PCA gets described two different ways — "find the direction of maximum variance" and "find the line that the points are closest to" — and it is not obvious those are the same instruction. One of them is about spreading shadows out. The other is about hugging the points. They sound like different jobs. They are the same job, and the reason is Pythagoras.

Center the data so the centroid is the origin. Fix a unit direction $v$, meaning a vector of length one. Write $x_i$ for the $i$-th centered observation, one of the $n$ points in $\mathbb R^d$, and $d_i$ for its perpendicular distance to the line through the origin in direction $v$ — that $d_i$ is a distance, unrelated to the feature count $d$, and the subscript is the only thing separating them. Every point splits into a piece along $v$ and a piece perpendicular to it, and those two pieces are the legs of a right triangle with $x_i$ as the hypotenuse:

$$\lVert x_i\rVert^2=\underbrace{(v^\top x_i)^2}_{\text{along }v}+\underbrace{d_i^2}_{\text{perpendicular}}$$

Take that one term at a time. On the left, the squared distance from the origin out to the point. On the right, first the squared length of the point's shadow on the line, then the squared distance from the point down to the line. Hypotenuse squared equals leg squared plus leg squared. That is all it is.

Sum over all points. The left side, $\sum_i\lVert x_i\rVert^2$, does not depend on $v$ at all — it is a property of the data. So the two terms on the right trade off exactly: every unit of squared projection you gain is a unit of squared distance you lose.

That fixed left side is what makes the argument work. It is a budget. Rotate $v$ and you cannot change the total, you can only move it between the two columns.

$$\max_{\lVert v\rVert=1}\ \sum_i(v^\top x_i)^2\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \min_{\lVert v\rVert=1}\ \sum_id_i^2$$

The double arrow reads "these two problems have the same solution." Maximizing the shadows and minimizing the distances are the same instruction, arrived at from opposite ends.

Left: each gray point drops to a blue dot on the line — that is its score vᵀxᵢ — and the blue double arrow spans ±2 standard deviations of those scores. PCA maximizes their spread. Right: the identical drops, drawn as the segments they are. PCA minimizes the sum of their squared lengths. The dashed gray segment is the vertical gap — that is the kind of distance OLS minimizes, to its own different line, which is why the OLS fit and PC1 are not the same line." />

This is also the cleanest way to see why PCA is not regression. Both fit a line through a cloud, but they measure error along different directions: OLS measures **vertically**, because $y$ is special and $x$ is taken as given. PCA measures **perpendicularly**, because no variable is special — it is describing the cloud, not predicting one coordinate from the others. Swap $y$ and $x$ and the OLS line changes; the PCA line does not.

That asymmetry is a reliable test. If relabelling which column is the target changes the answer, you are doing regression. If it cannot possibly change the answer, you are describing a cloud.

#### The dual picture: feature space

The scatter plot above is **point space** — $n$ points living in $\mathbb R^d$, one axis per feature. The same matrix supports a second, less familiar picture, and facts that are invisible in the scatter are plain in it.

The scatter plot reads the data matrix row by row: each row is a person, each person is a dot. Nothing stops you reading it column by column instead.

Turn the matrix on its side. Instead of $n$ points in $d$ dimensions, look at $d$ vectors in $\mathbb R^n$ — one arrow per *column* of $X$, living in a space whose axes are the observations. This is **feature space** (classically, "variable space"). Ten features means ten arrows, no matter how many rows you have.

Two facts make it readable. Index the features by $j$ and $k$, each running from $1$ to $d$, and write $x_j$ for the $j$-th column of $X$, $x_{ij}$ for the entry in row $i$ and column $j$, $s_j$ for the sample standard deviation of feature $j$, and $r_{jk}$ for the sample correlation between features $j$ and $k$. For centered columns,

$$\lVert x_j\rVert^2=\sum_i x_{ij}^2=(n-1)s_j^2,\qquad \cos\angle(x_j,x_k)=\frac{x_j^\top x_k}{\lVert x_j\rVert\,\lVert x_k\rVert}=r_{jk}$$

Take those one at a time. The first says the squared length of a feature's arrow is its sum of squared deviations, which is $(n-1)$ times its sample variance. The second says the cosine of the angle between two feature arrows is their correlation, because the cosine formula from geometry and the correlation formula from statistics are the same expression once the columns are centered.

So **length is standard deviation and angle is correlation**. Two strongly correlated features are two long arrows at a narrow angle. Two uncorrelated features are perpendicular. A feature with no variance is a point at the origin.

Two statistical quantities you normally compute have become two things you can see.

<PCAFeatureSpace caption="Two standardized features with correlation r = 0.8, drawn as vectors in observation space. Watch the angle, not the arrows' positions. Because they are standardized the arrows have equal length, so the correlation shows up purely as the angle between them: cos θ = 0.8, about 37°. PCA finds an orthogonal pair inside the plane they span. z₁ bisects them and is long; z₂ is perpendicular and short. For this matrix λ₁ = 1.8 and λ₂ = 0.2, so z₁ is exactly √(1.8/0.2) = 3 times the length of z₂ — the whole compression argument, visible as a length ratio." />

Read the compression story off the picture. Call the two component arrows $z_1$ and $z_2$, and write $\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2$ for the variances of the data along them. The two feature arrows nearly coincide, so almost everything they span is captured by one direction; $z_2$ is the small leftover. Push $r_{12}\to1$ and the arrows merge, $\lambda_2\to0$, and the second component vanishes — perfect collinearity is *literally* two arrows lying on top of each other, with a genuinely one-dimensional span. Push $r_{12}\to0$ and the arrows go perpendicular, $\lambda_1=\lambda_2=1$, and there is no preferred direction to find: PCA has nothing to compress.

Turning the correlation knob from 1 down to 0 walks you from "one arrow pretending to be two" to "two arrows with nothing in common," and PCA's usefulness dies at the far end. It compresses exactly to the extent that your features were saying the same thing twice.

The final row of the figure is the conservation law that makes PCA a rotation rather than a reduction. The rotation is carried by a matrix $V$ whose columns are the principal directions; because those columns are orthonormal, $V$ preserves total squared length. Summing over the $d$ features on the left and the $d$ components on the right,

$$\sum_j\lVert x_j\rVert^2=\sum_k\lVert z_k\rVert^2$$

so the components redistribute variance among directions without creating or destroying any of it. In words: add up the squared lengths of the original feature arrows, then add up the squared lengths of the component arrows, and you get the same number both times. Reduction happens later, and only when you delete columns.

### The mechanics: center, usually scale, then decompose

The recipe that produces that picture is three lines of code and one line of linear algebra.

Center $X$, then take the **SVD**

$$X=UDV^\top$$

Here $U$ and $V$ have orthonormal columns and $D$ is diagonal with nonnegative entries. Read it right to left, as a sequence of operations on the data: $V^\top$ rotates into the principal coordinate system, $D$ stretches each new axis by its own amount, and $U$ carries the result back out. The $d_j$ below are the diagonal entries of $D$, called the **singular values**, ordered $d_1\ge d_2\ge\cdots$, one per component; $u_j$ and $v_j$ are the $j$-th columns of $U$ and $V$. That $d_j$ is a singular value, not the perpendicular distance $d_i$ from the Pythagorean argument and not the feature count $d$ — the letter does three jobs in this lesson and only the subscript separates them.

Equivalently, eigendecompose the sample covariance:

$$\frac{1}{n-1}X^\top X=V\Lambda V^\top$$

Same $V$ in both lines, which is the point of calling them equivalent. One route factors the data matrix; the other factors the covariance matrix; both hand back the same set of directions.

The divisor is $n-1$ rather than $n$ because centering with the sample mean uses up one degree of freedom, so $\frac{1}{n-1}X^\top X$ is the unbiased sample covariance while $\frac1nX^\top X$ is the maximum-likelihood version, smaller by a factor $(n-1)/n$. The choice moves nothing PCA reports as a direction: scaling a matrix by a positive constant leaves its eigenvectors where they are and multiplies every eigenvalue by that same constant, so principal directions and explained-variance ratios come out identical either way, and only the absolute eigenvalues differ. `numpy.cov` divides by $n-1$ by default, and scikit-learn's PCA reports `explained_variance_` on the same convention.

The columns $v_j$ of $V$ are the **loadings** or principal directions. The new feature values are the **scores** $z_j=Xv_j=d_ju_j$. The eigenvalue $\lambda_j=d_j^2/(n-1)$ is the variance captured by component $j$, so its explained-variance ratio is

$$\frac{d_j^2}{\sum_kd_k^2}$$

That fraction is "this component's share of the total spread." Sum it over the first $m$ components and you have the familiar "the top 3 PCs explain 91% of the variance" sentence.

Centering is mandatory because PCA describes variation around the mean. Scaling is a modeling choice but usually essential when units differ: without standardization, a feature measured in dollars can dominate one measured in percentages only because its numerical variance is larger. Change that feature's unit from dollars to cents and its variance grows by a factor of 10,000, which would move PC1 without a single data point changing. Fit the scaler and PCA on training data only; fitting them before a split leaks validation/test distribution information.

### How many components can even exist

**Goal — get the hard ceiling on the number of components, and see why it bites hardest exactly where people most want to use PCA.**

Ask a concrete question. You have $n=200$ rows and $d=5000$ features. How many principal components can PCA return?

Not 5000. The number of nonzero components is the rank of the centered data matrix, and

$$\text{rank}(X_{\text{centered}}) \le \min(n-1,\; d)$$

The $d$ is obvious — you cannot have more independent directions than coordinates. The $n-1$ is the one people miss, and the $-1$ is worth deriving. The $n$ rows span a subspace of dimension at most $n$. Centering subtracts the column mean from every row, which forces the rows to sum to zero, so they satisfy one linear constraint and now span a space of dimension at most $n-1$. Since $C=\frac{1}{n-1}X^\top X$ has the same rank as $X$, at most $\min(n-1,d)$ of its eigenvalues can be nonzero. So the answer is **199 components**, and the remaining 4801 eigenvalues are not merely small — they are exactly zero, up to floating point.

<ScreeRankCeiling caption="A scree plot for n = 12 rows and many more columns. Eleven bars carry variance and every bar past the eleventh is exactly zero, not small — the rank ceiling at n − 1 = 11 is a fact of arithmetic, not a property of this dataset. Follow the amber cumulative curve: it hits 100% at the cliff for the same reason. When d ≫ n, 'the components explain 100% of the variance' is a statement about your sample size, and choosing k by the 95% line is choosing it from a curve whose shape the data barely constrains." />

Two consequences that matter more than the arithmetic.

**Reaching 100% explained variance means nothing when $d \gg n$.** With 200 rows, *any* 199 directions reconstruct your sample perfectly, because 199 dimensions is enough to pass through 200 points exactly. The explained-variance curve is guaranteed to reach 1.0 by component 199 regardless of whether there is any structure at all. This is the same overfitting arithmetic as fitting a degree-199 polynomial to 200 points, wearing different notation.

**The estimated components themselves are unreliable in this regime, and the failure is quantifiable.** The theory is the classical one for sample covariance matrices. If $d/n \to \gamma \in (0,1)$ and the data are pure isotropic noise with variance $\sigma^2$, the sample eigenvalues do not concentrate at $\sigma^2$; they spread over $[\,\sigma^2(1-\sqrt\gamma)^2,\ \sigma^2(1+\sqrt\gamma)^2\,]$ — the **Marchenko–Pastur** distribution. At $\gamma=0.5$ that interval is $[0.086,\,2.91]\,\sigma^2$: a 34-fold spread between the largest and smallest sample eigenvalue, produced by data with no structure whatsoever. A scree plot of pure noise looks exactly like a scree plot of real factor structure. Worse, the **BBP phase transition** (Baik–Ben Arous–Péché; Johnstone's spiked-covariance model) says a genuine signal spike is only detectable at all if its strength exceeds $\sqrt\gamma$; below that threshold the top sample eigenvector is asymptotically uncorrelated with the true direction, so you get a confident-looking component that points nowhere in particular.

The practical reading: when $d$ is an appreciable fraction of $n$, treat the top eigenvalues as biased upward and the tail as noise, and either shrink the covariance estimate (Ledoit–Wolf) or filter the spectrum against the Marchenko–Pastur bulk before believing any component past the first few. This is the same problem, with the same fixes, as estimating a covariance matrix for portfolio construction (c10.2) — where $d=500$ stocks against $n=252$ trading days is a completely ordinary setup.

> **Stop and think.** If 199 components reconstruct 200 rows perfectly no matter what, what would a *useful* stopping criterion for $m$ actually be measuring? Hint: it cannot be a property of the training reconstruction, so it has to be a property of something held out — and if the components feed a supervised model, "held out" should probably be judged on the thing you actually care about.

### Why the loadings changed overnight

**Goal — explain the most common production complaint about PCA, which has two completely different causes and two different fixes.**

You refit a production PCA every week. The downstream coefficients keep flipping sign and jumping around, even though the data barely moved. There are two distinct mechanisms, and telling them apart is the whole diagnosis.

**Mechanism 1: sign indeterminacy.** The sign of a component is arbitrary — $v_j$ and $-v_j$ describe the same axis and give the same reconstruction, with scores negated. Nothing in the objective prefers one, so which one a solver returns depends on implementation details and starting vectors. This is cosmetic but it wrecks anything that stores or compares loadings across runs. The fix is a deterministic sign convention applied after the fit: scikit-learn's `svd_flip` forces the largest-magnitude entry of each component to be positive, or you can pin the sign so that a chosen reference feature always loads positively. Never interpret a loading's sign in isolation; only relative signs within a component mean anything.

**Mechanism 2: near-degenerate eigenvalues, which is the serious one.** When two eigenvalues are close, $\lambda_j \approx \lambda_{j+1}$, the individual eigenvectors are not well determined — only the *subspace* they jointly span is. Any orthonormal pair inside that plane is an almost equally good answer, so a tiny perturbation in the data rotates them arbitrarily within it and can swap their order. This is not solver flakiness; the population quantity genuinely is not identified. The **Davis–Kahan** theorem makes it precise: the rotation of an estimated eigenvector under a perturbation $E$ is bounded by roughly $\lVert E\rVert$ divided by the **eigengap** $\min_{k\ne j}|\lambda_j - \lambda_k|$. Small gap, huge sensitivity. If $\lambda_3=4.02$ and $\lambda_4=3.98$, components 3 and 4 will trade places on noise alone.

The fixes differ from Mechanism 1's entirely: do not interpret individual components inside a near-degenerate block — use the projection onto the block's subspace, which *is* stable; or fit the transform once and freeze it, refitting on a deliberate cadence rather than every run; or align each refit to the previous loadings with a Procrustes rotation before comparing. And monitor the eigengaps as a first-class quantity, because they tell you in advance which components are safe to talk about.

### How the decomposition is actually computed

"Eigendecompose the covariance matrix" hides the part that matters. You do **not** solve the eigenvalue equation symbolically. You turn it into an iterative optimization problem. Because the covariance matrix is symmetric positive semidefinite, that optimization is especially clean.

The linear algebra course answer is to form the characteristic polynomial and find its roots. For a 10,000-by-10,000 covariance matrix that is a degree-10,000 polynomial, and nobody does this.

The whole hierarchy below comes from one idea: **repeated linear transformations reveal invariant directions.** Everything else is bookkeeping to make that idea fast and to stop the directions collapsing onto each other.

#### PCA is the maximizer of a quadratic form

Let

$$C=\frac{1}{n-1}X^\top X$$

be the covariance matrix. The first principal component solves

$$\max_{\lVert v\rVert=1} v^\top Cv$$

In words: among all directions of length one, find the one that makes the number $v^\top Cv$ as large as possible. This is the "maximum spread of the shadows" instruction from the first section, written in matrix form.

The answer is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue. But suppose you do not know the eigenvectors. How do you find it numerically?

Because $C$ is symmetric it has an orthonormal eigenbasis — an orthogonal matrix $V$ whose columns $v_1,\ldots,v_d$ are the eigenvectors, and a diagonal matrix $\Lambda$ holding the corresponding eigenvalues, ordered largest first:

$$C=V\Lambda V^\top,\qquad \lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_d\ge0$$

An orthonormal eigenbasis means the eigenvectors are mutually perpendicular unit vectors, so they work as a coordinate system. That coordinate system is also where the claim about the maximizer comes from, and the argument is two lines. Write any unit vector in those coordinates, $v=\sum_ja_jv_j$, where the weights $a_j$ satisfy $\sum_ja_j^2=1$ because $v$ has length one. Then $v^\top Cv=\sum_ja_j^2\lambda_j$: the quantity being maximized is a weighted average of the eigenvalues, with weights that are forced to sum to one. A weighted average is as large as it can be when all the weight sits on the largest item, so the maximizer puts $a_1=1$ and the rest at zero, which is $v=v_1$. That coordinate system is the lever for everything that follows.

Take literally any starting vector $q_0$. The eigenvectors form a basis, so

$$q_0=c_1v_1+c_2v_2+\cdots+c_dv_d$$

The $c_j$ are the coordinates of $q_0$ in that eigenbasis. You do not know them and you never will; you only need to know that they exist.

Multiply by $C$. Each eigenvector is just scaled by its own eigenvalue:

$$Cq_0=c_1\lambda_1v_1+c_2\lambda_2v_2+\cdots+c_d\lambda_dv_d$$

Nothing mixed. Each eigenvector stayed pointing where it was and only got longer or shorter, which is what being an eigenvector means.

Multiply again, and the same thing happens a second time — every eigenvalue picks up one more factor of itself:

$$C^2q_0=c_1\lambda_1^2v_1+c_2\lambda_2^2v_2+\cdots+c_d\lambda_d^2v_d$$

The pattern is now fixed. Each further multiplication raises every eigenvalue to one higher power and leaves the directions where they were, so after $t$ multiplications:

$$C^tq_0=c_1\lambda_1^tv_1+c_2\lambda_2^tv_2+\cdots+c_d\lambda_d^tv_d$$

Every coefficient is a raw eigenvalue raised to the same power. The largest one is winning the race, and it wins by an exponentially growing margin. Make that precise by measuring everything against it.

Factor out $\lambda_1^t$:

$$C^tq_0=\lambda_1^t\left[c_1v_1+c_2\left(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right)^tv_2+\cdots\right]$$

If $\lambda_1>\lambda_2$ then every ratio $(\lambda_j/\lambda_1)^t\to0$. Each of those ratios is a number smaller than 1 raised to the power $t$, so every term except the first is dying off. So after normalizing,

$$\boxed{\ \frac{C^tq_0}{\lVert C^tq_0\rVert}\longrightarrow\pm v_1\ }$$

provided $c_1\neq0$. That is **power iteration**. There is no determinant anywhere.

The normalization is there to strip off the $\lambda_1^t$ out front, which would otherwise blow up. The $\pm$ is the sign ambiguity from earlier, showing up again. And that proviso is the one assumption in the whole argument: your starting vector must have some component along $v_1$. A random start satisfies it with probability one.

#### The algorithm for the top direction

Start with any unit vector $q$, and let $z$ be a scratch vector holding the result of one multiplication. Repeat:

$$z=Cq,\qquad q\leftarrow\frac{z}{\lVert z\rVert}$$

Multiply, then divide by the length to bring it back to unit size. Two lines of code, no matrix factorization.

Eventually $q\approx v_1$.

How do you get the eigenvalue? Once $q\approx v_1$ we have $Cq\approx\lambda_1q$. Left-multiply by $q^\top$:

$$q^\top Cq\approx\lambda_1q^\top q=\lambda_1$$

because $q$ is normalized. So

$$\boxed{\ \lambda_1\approx q^\top Cq\ }$$

This is the **Rayleigh quotient**. The whole top eigenpair falls out of *multiply, normalize, repeat*.

The convergence rate is $\lvert\lambda_2/\lambda_1\rvert$ per step, which tells you exactly when this is slow: when the top two eigenvalues are close, the second term dies slowly and you need many iterations to separate the directions. A ratio of 0.5 kills half the error per step. A ratio of 0.99 barely moves.

#### A concrete run

Take

$$C=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}$$

and pretend you know nothing about its eigenvalues. Start from $q_0=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}$.

$$Cq_0=\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}\quad\Rightarrow\quad q_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

Keep multiplying, ignoring normalization for a moment since it does not change direction:

$$C\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}10\\6\end{bmatrix},\qquad C\begin{bmatrix}10\\6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}36\\28\end{bmatrix},\qquad C\begin{bmatrix}36\\28\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}136\\120\end{bmatrix}$$

Watch the ratio of the two entries: $1/3$, then $0.6$, then $0.78$, then $120/136\approx0.88$. It is heading to $1$, so the direction is heading to $\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}$. Normalized,

$$v_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad v_1^\top Cv_1=4$$

The top eigenpair, found numerically, with no polynomial solved. (The other eigenpair here is $\lambda_2=2$ at $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}[1,-1]^\top$, so the convergence rate was $\lambda_2/\lambda_1=0.5$ — the error halves each step, which is what the sequence of ratios shows.)

#### PCA needs several eigenvectors, so stop them collapsing

One direction is rarely enough. You wanted several components, so try the obvious extension.

Power-iterate several vectors independently and they all converge to $v_1$. You need something that stops them landing on the same direction.

That failure is not a bug in the code. It is the algorithm doing precisely what it was designed to do, $k$ times over, on $k$ different starting vectors that all satisfy $c_1\neq0$.

Say you want $k$ components. Instead of one vector, keep a matrix of $k$ orthonormal columns:

$$Q=\begin{bmatrix}q_1&q_2&\cdots&q_k\end{bmatrix}$$

Multiply all of them at once, $Z=CQ$. Every column starts drifting toward $v_1$, so immediately re-orthogonalize the columns by taking a QR decomposition $Z=QR$ — which rewrites the columns of $Z$ as an orthonormal set $Q$ spanning the same space, times an upper-triangular bookkeeping factor $R$ — throwing away $R$ and keeping the new orthonormal $Q$. Repeat:

$$\boxed{\ Z_t=CQ_t,\qquad Q_{t+1}=\operatorname{orth}(Z_t)\ }$$

One step of drift, one step of pushing the columns back apart, forever.

This is **orthogonal iteration**, also called simultaneous power iteration. Why does it work? If the starting block is $Q_0=VA$ for some coefficient matrix $A$, which is another way of saying its columns are combinations of eigenvectors, then

$$C^tQ_0=V\Lambda^tA$$

and because $\lambda_1^t,\ldots,\lambda_k^t$ dominate the smaller powers, the *subspace* spanned by those columns converges to $\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_k)$. Note what is converging and what is not: the subspace is the thing with the guarantee. The QR step is what keeps the individual columns from all becoming the same vector.

That is already a working algorithm for PCA's top $k$ directions.

#### The same idea, rewritten, is the QR eigensolver

Now suppose you want *all* eigenvalues. Start with $A_0=C$ — the first in a sequence of matrices $A_0,A_1,A_2,\ldots$, unrelated to the coefficient matrix $A$ just above — factor it, then multiply the factors back in the opposite order:

$$A_0=Q_0R_0,\qquad A_1=R_0Q_0$$

Factor, then swap. That is the entire move, and on first sight it looks like a party trick.

Why would reversing the factors help? Because $A_0=Q_0R_0$ implies $R_0=Q_0^\top A_0$, and therefore

$$\boxed{\ A_1=R_0Q_0=Q_0^\top A_0Q_0\ }$$

Substituting that expression for $R_0$ is the whole step, and it uses only $Q_0^\top Q_0=I$.

That is a **similarity transformation**, and similarity transformations preserve eigenvalues. The one-line proof: if $Av=\lambda v$ and $B=Q^\top AQ$, set $w=Q^\top v$. Then

$$Bw=Q^\top AQQ^\top v=Q^\top Av=\lambda Q^\top v=\lambda w$$

so $A$ and $B$ have exactly the same eigenvalues. The middle step is where $QQ^\top$ collapses to the identity, which is the only reason the chain closes.

Repeat the step:

$$A_t=Q_tR_t,\qquad A_{t+1}=R_tQ_t$$

So you can do this as many times as you like and the eigenvalues never move. Only the matrix's appearance changes.

For a symmetric matrix the result is remarkable — the iterates converge to

$$A_t\longrightarrow\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1&&0\\&\ddots&\\0&&\lambda_d\end{bmatrix}$$

The eigenvalues literally appear on the diagonal. That is the **QR algorithm**. You never asked for them. You just kept factoring and swapping, and they showed up.

#### Why it drives toward diagonal form

Repeated QR iteration is orthogonal iteration run on all $d$ basis vectors at once, written in a different form. In orthogonal iteration, $C^tQ_0$ becomes dominated by the eigenvectors of the largest eigenvalues; QR repeatedly extracts an orthonormal basis for those increasingly eigenvector-aligned directions.

So this is not a new algorithm. It is orthogonal iteration with $k=d$, written down differently.

If you accumulate the rotations,

$$\bar Q_t=Q_0Q_1\cdots Q_t$$

then the algorithm is effectively driving $\bar Q_t\to V$, the eigenvector matrix. That product is the total rotation applied so far, from the original coordinate system to the current one. So

$$\bar Q_t^\top C\bar Q_t\longrightarrow V^\top CV$$

Conjugating $C$ by the accumulated rotation therefore converges to conjugating it by $V$ itself. Evaluate that limit using the eigenvector equation $CV=V\Lambda$, then use $V^\top V=I$, which holds because the eigenvectors are orthonormal:

$$V^\top CV=V^\top V\Lambda=\Lambda$$

The first line says what the algorithm approaches; the second says what that limit is equal to. Putting them together,

$$\boxed{\ \bar Q_t^\top C\bar Q_t\longrightarrow\Lambda\ }$$

The QR algorithm is iteratively rotating the coordinate system until the matrix becomes diagonal. That is the entire idea. Diagonal is what a matrix looks like when you are finally standing in its own coordinate system.

#### The cost problem, and how symmetry fixes it

The algorithm works. Now price it.

A dense $d\times d$ QR decomposition costs about $O(d^3)$. Hundreds of iterations at $O(d^3)$ each is a bill nobody wants to pay. But covariance matrices are symmetric, and you can exploit that *before* iterating.

Transform $C$ into a **tridiagonal** matrix

$$T=\begin{bmatrix}a_1&b_1&0&\cdots\\b_1&a_2&b_2&\cdots\\0&b_2&a_3&\cdots\\\vdots&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}$$

with diagonal entries $a_1,a_2,\ldots$ and off-diagonal entries $b_1,b_2,\ldots$, built so that $T=Q^\top CQ$ for some orthogonal $Q$ — again a similarity transformation, so $T$ has exactly the same eigenvalues as $C$. Tridiagonal means nonzero on the diagonal and on the two bands next to it, zero everywhere else.

That is the eigenvalue-preserving property of similarity transformations, reused. You are allowed to change the matrix as long as you change it by a similarity transformation, because the answer you want rides through untouched.

#### Householder reflections do the tridiagonalization

The tool for creating those zeros is a mirror.

Suppose the first column of $C$ is $[c_{11},x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots]^\top$. You want to reflect the lower piece $x=[x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots]^\top$ onto the first coordinate direction:

$$Hx=\begin{bmatrix}\pm\lVert x\rVert\\0\\0\\\vdots\end{bmatrix}$$

A reflection cannot change a vector's length, so all of $x$'s length has to end up in the single surviving entry. That is why $\lVert x\rVert$ appears there.

Construct $u=x\pm\lVert x\rVert e_1$, where $e_1$ is the first standard basis vector $[1,0,0,\ldots]^\top$, normalize to $v=u/\lVert u\rVert$, and define

$$H=I-2vv^\top$$

Why is that a reflection? Split any vector into parts parallel and perpendicular to $v$: $x=x_\perp+\alpha v$, where $x_\perp$ is the perpendicular part and the scalar $\alpha=v^\top x$ says how far the vector runs along $v$. Here $u$ and $v$ are the reflection's own vectors and have nothing to do with the singular vectors of the same name. Then since $v^\top x_\perp=0$ and $\lVert v\rVert=1$,

$$Hx=(I-2vv^\top)(x_\perp+\alpha v)=x_\perp+\alpha v-2\alpha v=x_\perp-\alpha v$$

Follow the two pieces separately. The perpendicular piece is annihilated by $v^\top$ and passes through unchanged. The parallel piece $\alpha v$ has twice itself subtracted, so it comes out as $-\alpha v$.

It leaves the perpendicular component alone and flips the parallel one. That is a mirror with $v$ as its normal. Choose $v$ and you can zero every entry below whichever entry you want to keep.

Apply the reflection on both sides. Since $H^\top=H$,

$$C_1=H^\top CH=HCH$$

which is a similarity transformation, so eigenvalues are preserved. Applying it on one side only would create the zeros and wreck the eigenvalues, which is why it goes on both.

Work column by column, $C\to C_1\to C_2\to\cdots\to T$. Because the matrix is symmetric, killing the entries below the subdiagonal simultaneously kills the matching entries above the superdiagonal:

$$\boxed{\ \text{symmetric dense matrix}\longrightarrow\text{symmetric tridiagonal matrix}\ }$$

Symmetry means every zero you create costs one operation and buys two.

#### Why tridiagonal is such a big win

Count entries.

A dense matrix has roughly $d^2$ nonzero entries. A tridiagonal one has about $3d$. Matrix-vector multiplication drops from $O(d^2)$ to $O(d)$, and the QR steps exploit the same sparsity.

At $d=10{,}000$ that is a hundred million entries against about thirty thousand.

So the expensive part of a full dense eigendecomposition is the **one-time** reduction $C\to T$ at $O(d^3)$. Everything after that is vastly cheaper. This is why the standard symmetric eigensolver is "reduce, then iterate" rather than "iterate on the dense matrix."

#### PCA usually does not want all the eigenvectors

Step back and notice you have been solving a harder problem than the one you have.

Usually $k\ll d$. You might have $10{,}000$ features and want $50$ components. Computing all $10{,}000$ eigenvectors to throw away $9{,}950$ of them is wasteful. You only want the subspace $\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_k)$.

Power iteration already told you something useful: the sequence $q,\ Cq,\ C^2q,\ C^3q,\ldots$ carries progressively more information about the dominant eigenvectors. Plain power iteration throws away every one of those intermediate vectors and keeps only the newest. So keep all of it. Form the **Krylov subspace**

$$\boxed{\ \mathcal K_m(C,q)=\operatorname{span}\lbrace q,Cq,C^2q,\ldots,C^{m-1}q\rbrace\ }$$

That is the span of everything power iteration produced along the way, discarded work reclaimed as a subspace. Its dimension $m$ is how many multiplications you are willing to do — a different $m$ from the count of retained components in the modeling sections.

Instead of solving a $d\times d$ eigenproblem, find an orthonormal basis $Q_m$ for this much smaller $m$-dimensional space and project $C$ into it:

$$T_m=Q_m^\top CQ_m$$

Read that as "what does $C$ look like to someone who can only see inside this subspace."

Now $T_m$ is only $m\times m$. Solve the small problem $T_my=\theta y$, whose eigenvalue $\theta$ and eigenvector $y$ both live entirely inside the subspace, then lift the answer back:

$$v\approx Q_my$$

The small eigenvector $y$ gives coordinates inside the subspace; multiplying by $Q_m$ converts those coordinates back into a direction in the original $d$-dimensional space.

That is the core of **Lanczos**: use repeated $Cq$ operations to discover the small subspace containing the dominant eigenvectors, then solve the eigenproblem only inside that subspace. Because $C$ is symmetric, the orthogonalization simplifies dramatically and $T_m$ comes out tridiagonal on its own.

#### PCA lets you skip building $C$ entirely

One more thing to delete, and it is the biggest object in the room.

Recall $C=\frac1{n-1}X^\top X$. Lanczos only ever needs the *action* of $C$ on a vector, never $C$ itself. And

$$Cv=\frac1{n-1}X^\top Xv=\frac1{n-1}X^\top(Xv)$$

The parentheses are the whole trick. Multiply by $X$ first to get a short vector, then multiply that by $X^\top$.

So you can go $v\xrightarrow{\ X\ }Xv\xrightarrow{\ X^\top\ }X^\top Xv$ without ever forming the $d\times d$ covariance matrix.

This is a large practical win. If $d$ is $10{,}000$, then $C$ is a hundred million entries you never have to store. It is also more numerically accurate: forming $X^\top X$ squares the condition number — the factor by which a relative error in the input can be magnified in the answer — so you lose roughly half your significant digits before you start. This is exactly why PCA implementations run SVD on $X$ directly rather than eigendecomposing $X^\top X$, and it is what truncated and randomized SVD solvers are built on.

#### The hierarchy to keep in your head

PCA asks for $\max_{\lVert v\rVert=1}v^\top Cv$, which is an eigenvector problem. But numerically you never solve $\det(C-\lambda I)=0$. Instead:

| Goal | Method | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| Top eigenpair | Power iteration | Repeated multiplication amplifies the dominant eigendirection |
| Top $k$ | Orthogonal iteration | Power iteration on a block, plus QR to stop columns collapsing |
| All eigenvalues | QR algorithm | Write that iteration as repeated similarity transforms |
| Full symmetric problem | Householder $\to$ tridiagonal, then QR | Pay $O(d^3)$ once so every iteration is cheap |
| Top $k$, $d$ huge | Krylov / Lanczos | Solve the eigenproblem inside a small subspace |
| PCA specifically | Apply it to $X$, not $X^\top X$ | Only $Xv$ and $X^\top u$ are ever needed |

Read the table top to bottom and each row is a repair of the row above it.

The deepest idea is not "find the roots of the characteristic polynomial." It is that **repeated linear transformations reveal invariant directions**, and every algorithm above is a way of exploiting that without letting the directions collapse or the cost explode.

### PCA versus feature selection

Back up to the modeling question. You have too many columns and you want fewer. There are two different ways to want that.

Feature selection keeps original columns; PCA replaces them with mixtures. Ten correlated measurements may compress into one stable component, but “PC1 increased” is harder to explain than “revenue increased.”

That trade is the whole comparison. PCA is attractive for compression, visualization, de-noising, and conditioning; it is costly when original-variable interpretation matters.

### Principal-component regression: predict after rotating

Once the rotation exists, using it for prediction is the obvious next move.

**PCR** performs PCA on $X$, keeps the top $m$ scores, then regresses $y$ on those scores. Orthogonality removes collinearity, and choosing $m<p$ reduces variance, where $p$ is the number of predictor columns — the quantity written $d$ in the geometric sections above. The rule is a hard threshold: directions $1$ through $m$ get full access to the fit; directions after $m$ get none. There is no in-between, and no appeal.

The number of components is a supervised hyperparameter even though the PCA transformation is unsupervised. Choose $m$ by cross-validation on the full pipeline, not solely by a “95% explained variance” rule. Explained variance concerns reconstruction of $X$, not predictive information about $y$. That is the gap between reconstructing $X$ and predicting $y$, showing up as a concrete tuning mistake.

Now compare that all-or-nothing rule against what ridge does to the same directions.

**Ridge is "soft PCR."** With $X=UDV^\top$, and writing $\lambda>0$ for the ridge penalty — a different $\lambda$ from the eigenvalues above, and unrelated to them — the fitted values $\hat y_{\text{ridge}}$ are

$$\hat y_{\text{ridge}} = \sum_{j=1}^p u_j\,\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}\,u_j^\top y$$

Read the sum one principal direction at a time. The rightmost piece, $u_j^\top y$, is how much of $y$ lies along direction $j$. The middle fraction scales that number down. The leading $u_j$ puts it back as a direction in prediction space. Watch the fraction as $d_j$ moves: when $d_j$ is large, $\lambda$ is negligible next to $d_j^2$ and the factor sits near 1; when $d_j$ is tiny, $\lambda$ dominates the denominator and the factor sits near 0.

OLS uses shrinkage factor $1$ on every principal direction; ridge multiplies direction $j$ by $\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}\in(0,1)$ — so it **shrinks most along the low-variance (small $d_j$) directions**, which are exactly the unstable, collinear ones. **Principal-component regression (PCR)** is the hard-threshold version: keep the top $m$ components fully, drop the rest. This is the exact sense in which "ridge ≈ smooth PCR."

Two methods, one axis, two shapes of decision: a step and a slope.

Seen this way, regularization is a decision about singular directions. OLS divides by singular values; a very small $d_j$ can amplify noise enormously. Ridge replaces the dangerous inversion with a shrinkage factor that approaches 0 for weak directions and 1 for strong directions. PCR uses a step function; ridge uses a smooth dial. Neither asks whether the direction predicts $y$ before deciding how strongly to retain it, although ridge's $\lambda$ is chosen using predictive validation.

That target-blindness is where PCA goes badly wrong.

**When PCA hurts.** PCA is **unsupervised — target-blind**. It keeps high-*variance* directions, which may be exactly the *irrelevant* ones; the predictive signal can live in a low-variance component that PCA discards. It also assumes linearity and "variance = importance," and destroys interpretability (components are blends of features). It helps when collinearity/dimensionality is the problem *and* signal aligns with high-variance directions; otherwise supervised methods (PLS, or just ridge) are better.

A counterexample makes it concrete. Let a nuisance feature vary wildly while a low-variance sensor almost perfectly determines the target. PC1 follows the nuisance variance; truncating to PC1 destroys the signal. PCA worked perfectly at its stated job—reconstructing $X$—and failed at the different job we cared about. It was never told there was a target.

PCA also captures only linear directions, can be dominated by outliers, and makes components dependent on the training distribution. When feature covariance shifts, old loadings may no longer summarize the new population well.

The named supervised fix is **partial least squares (PLS)**, which builds components maximizing $\text{Cov}(Xv, y)$ rather than $\text{Var}(Xv)$ — the same geometry with the target admitted into the objective. It exists precisely because target-blindness is a real defect and not a philosophical quibble.

### Two places PCA is simply the wrong tool

**Goal — recognize the two settings where the whole construction stops applying, rather than merely underperforming.**

**One-hot categoricals.** PCA on a design matrix that is mostly one-hot indicators is a category error, for four separate reasons, and the interview answer should name at least three.

1. *The variance scale is set by frequency, not importance.* A binary column with category frequency $q$ has variance $q(1-q)$. A category appearing in 1% of rows carries variance $0.0099$; a 50/50 category carries $0.25$, twenty-five times more. PCA will chase the balanced categories and discard the rare ones, and rarity has no relationship to predictive value — in fraud and churn problems the rare category is usually the whole point.
2. *Centering destroys sparsity.* One-hot matrices are stored sparse; subtracting a column mean makes every zero entry nonzero, so a matrix that fit in memory as $10^6\times10^5$ sparse becomes a dense $10^{11}$-entry object. This is why text pipelines use `TruncatedSVD` (latent semantic analysis), which factors the sparse matrix *without* centering, rather than PCA.
3. *Guaranteed degeneracy.* The one-hot columns of a single variable sum to the all-ones vector, so with an intercept-like constant present they are linearly dependent by construction — a direction of exactly zero variance per categorical variable, contributing structural zero eigenvalues that mean nothing about the data.
4. *The geometry is not meaningful.* PCA is built on covariance, which presumes squared Euclidean distance is the right notion of dissimilarity. For nominal categories it is not: the distance between "red" and "blue" is not a quantity, and any component mixing indicator loadings is uninterpretable.

The right tools instead: **multiple correspondence analysis (MCA)** for purely categorical data, **FAMD** for mixed types, `TruncatedSVD` for sparse high-cardinality data where you only need a compressed representation, learned entity embeddings when a model can supply them, or simply a model with native categorical support (c1.6's GBDTs) so the question does not arise.

**Before a tree model.** Would you run PCA before a gradient-boosted tree? Almost never, and the reason is about inductive bias rather than accuracy.

Trees split on axis-aligned thresholds of single features, so their inductive bias is that the interesting structure lines up with the original coordinates. PCA replaces those coordinates with dense linear mixtures, and a split on PC1 is now a threshold on a combination of every feature at once. The tree can no longer isolate a single variable, so a relationship that took one split now takes a staircase of them. Trees are also invariant to any monotone transform of an individual feature, so PCA's standardization step buys them nothing that they did not already have. Expect accuracy flat to slightly worse, and expect feature importances and SHAP attributions to become uninterpretable, because they now describe components rather than measurements.

The genuine exception is worth knowing because it is the follow-up question: when the true boundary is *oblique* to the feature axes, axis-aligned splits approximate it with a staircase that costs depth and variance, and rotating the axes onto the boundary lets a shallow tree cut it in one split. So PCA helps a tree exactly when the data's natural axes are rotated relative to the recorded ones — a rare enough situation that it should be demonstrated on a validation set, not assumed. The other honest use is pure cost: at very large $d$, a truncated projection makes training tractable, which is a compute argument rather than an accuracy one.

### Reading the components of a return matrix

**Goal — apply everything above to the setting where PCA is not a preprocessing step but the model itself.**

Run PCA on the correlation matrix of daily returns for 500 stocks. PC1 comes back explaining around 40% of the variance, with every loading carrying the same sign. What is it?

**It is the market factor.** Equity returns are pervasively positively correlated, and for a matrix with all-positive entries the Perron–Frobenius theorem guarantees the top eigenvector can be taken to have all-positive entries. So the first component is a roughly equal-weighted long position in everything, and its score time series tracks the broad market return. The 40% figure is the ordinary quiet-market number; in a crisis, correlations converge toward one and PC1's share rises sharply — which is itself a usable regime indicator, and the mechanism behind "diversification fails exactly when you need it."

The components after the first are where the content is. PC2 and PC3 typically have loadings that *change sign* across the cross-section — long one group of names, short another — and those contrasts usually align with sector or style (growth against value, cyclicals against defensives), though the alignment is a post-hoc reading rather than something the method knows. These are **statistical factors**: derived from the covariance structure alone, unlabelled, and not guaranteed to be stable. Neutralizing the top $k$ components and trading the residual is the basic construction behind statistical arbitrage, and the same eigendecomposition is the backbone of the risk models in c10.2.

Three cautions follow directly from earlier sections, which is why this example is a good exam of whether the lesson landed:

1. **You are almost certainly in the $d \gtrsim n$ regime.** With 500 names and 252 trading days in a year, $d>n$: the sample correlation matrix is singular, at most 251 components are nonzero, and by the Marchenko–Pastur result the eigenvalues are badly biased with a wide noise bulk. Laloux et al. 1999 showed that for real market data the great majority of the spectrum is statistically indistinguishable from noise, with only the top handful of eigenvalues above the bulk. Shrinkage (Ledoit–Wolf) or random-matrix filtering is not optional here.
2. **Eigengaps decide what you may interpret.** PC2 and PC3 in equity data frequently have close eigenvalues, so by Davis–Kahan they rotate into each other between estimation windows. Interpreting "the value factor is component 2" across two quarters is exactly the mistake that section warned about; the stable object is the subspace, not the individual axis.
3. **Correlation, not covariance.** Use the correlation matrix unless you specifically want high-volatility names to dominate — running PCA on the covariance of raw prices or of returns with wildly different volatilities makes PC1 a statement about which stock moves most in absolute terms, which is the unit-sensitivity problem from the mechanics section wearing a finance costume.

## Tradeoff matrix

| Method | What it optimizes | Sees $y$? | Keeps original features? | Main cost | Reach for it when |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PCA / PCR | Reconstruction of $X$ | No | No — dense mixtures | Target-blind; interpretability gone | Collinearity or dimensionality is the problem and signal tracks variance |
| Ridge | Prediction, shrinking low-variance directions | Yes (via $\lambda$) | Yes | Coefficients biased | Almost always the safer default over PCR |
| PLS | $\text{Cov}(Xv,\,y)$ | Yes | No | Can overfit the target with many components | Signal sits in low-variance directions |
| Feature selection | A chosen criterion on original columns | Usually yes | Yes | Unstable under collinearity | Interpretation in original units is required |
| TruncatedSVD | Reconstruction, uncentered | No | No | No centering, so not strictly PCA | Sparse high-cardinality data (text, one-hot) |
| MCA / FAMD | Categorical or mixed-type association | No | No | Less tooling, less familiar | The design matrix is categorical |
| Autoencoder | Nonlinear reconstruction | No | No | Needs data and tuning; no orthogonality guarantee | Structure is genuinely nonlinear and $n$ is large |

## Worked decision walk-throughs

**Scenario A: 60 correlated macro indicators, 4,000 monthly rows, predicting a continuous target.** High collinearity, $n \gg d$, interpretation not required. Standardize, then prefer **ridge** over PCR as the default — it shrinks the same unstable low-variance directions but does so with $\lambda$ chosen by cross-validation against $y$, so it is not target-blind. If you do use PCR, choose $m$ by cross-validating the *full pipeline* on predictive error, never by a 95%-variance rule, and check explicitly that $y$ correlates with the components you kept.

**Scenario B: 20,000 gene-expression features, 150 patients, binary outcome.** Here $d \gg n$, so at most 149 components exist and the explained-variance curve reaching 100% is arithmetic rather than evidence. By the spiked-covariance argument the leading directions are estimated with large error, so treat any single component as unreliable. Use PCA for visualization and de-noising, but put the supervised work on a penalized model (L1 or elastic net) fit inside a proper cross-validation loop, with the PCA fit *inside* the loop as well — fitting it on all the data before splitting leaks test-set covariance structure into training.

**Scenario C: 500 equity return series, daily, building a risk model.** Use the **correlation** matrix. Expect PC1 to be the market with all-same-sign loadings and roughly 40% of variance. Because $d>n$ on any reasonable window, shrink the covariance (Ledoit–Wolf) or filter against the Marchenko–Pastur bulk before trusting anything past the top few components, and check eigengaps before naming any individual component as a factor. Neutralize the components you believe in and treat the residual as the tradable signal (c10.2).

**Scenario D: 300 mostly one-hot categorical features feeding a GBDT.** Do not run PCA. Centering destroys the sparsity, component variance tracks category frequency rather than importance, and the tree's axis-aligned splits are made worse by a dense rotation. Use native categorical handling or target encoding, and if you need compression on a sparse matrix use `TruncatedSVD` without centering.

## Production failure modes

1. **PCA fitted before the train/test split.** The rotation is estimated from the whole dataset, so test-set covariance structure leaks into the training representation and validation scores come out optimistic. The tell is a validation-to-production gap with no other explanation. Fit the scaler and PCA inside the cross-validation pipeline (c1.1).
2. **`k` chosen by the 95% rule for a supervised model.** Explained variance measures reconstruction of $X$, and the model is judged on prediction of $y$; the two criteria are unrelated, and the 95% rule can keep 40 useless components or drop the one that mattered. Cross-validate $m$ on predictive error end-to-end.
3. **Unstandardized features with mixed units.** A feature in dollars swamps one in percentages purely through numerical magnitude, and switching that column from dollars to cents moves PC1 without a single data point changing. Standardize unless the units are already commensurable and you deliberately want variance weighting.
4. **Loadings compared across retrainings.** Sign flips and near-degenerate rotations make raw loading comparisons meaningless; teams read this as model drift and go looking for a data bug that does not exist. Apply a sign convention, monitor eigengaps, and align refits by Procrustes rotation before comparing anything.
5. **Believing a scree plot in the $d\gtrsim n$ regime.** Pure noise produces a wide, structured-looking eigenvalue spread (Marchenko–Pastur), so an elbow is not evidence of factors. Compare the spectrum against the noise bulk, or against a permutation baseline, before deciding how many components are real.
6. **Outliers steering the rotation.** PCA minimizes *squared* reconstruction error, so a handful of extreme rows can capture PC1 entirely — the same sensitivity as OLS, from the same squared-error term. Inspect the extreme scores, and use a robust PCA variant if the tails are genuinely heavy.

## Interview gotchas

- **"How does ridge relate to PCA?"** — Ridge is soft PCR: with $X=UDV^\top$ it multiplies principal direction $j$ by $d_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda)$, shrinking most along the low-variance directions, where PCR applies a hard 0/1 cut at $m$. The strong answer adds that ridge chooses its shrinkage using $y$ through cross-validated $\lambda$, which is why it is usually the safer default.
- **"How would you actually compute the eigenvalues?"** — Not by $\det(C-\lambda I)=0$: no radical formula exists past degree 4, and polynomial root-finding is numerically catastrophic. Walk the chain — power iteration, orthogonal iteration with QR to stop columns collapsing, the QR algorithm, Householder tridiagonalization to make each step cheap, Lanczos when $d$ is huge — and finish on running SVD against $X$ directly, since only $Xv$ and $X^\top u$ products are needed.
- **"How many components can PCA return for $n=200$, $d=5000$?"** — At most $\min(n-1,d)=199$; centering costs the one degree of freedom. Then add the consequence: reaching 100% explained variance is guaranteed and therefore uninformative.
- **"Is PCA feature selection?"** — No. Selection keeps a subset of original columns; PCA replaces all of them with dense mixtures, so every original feature is still required at inference time. You have reduced dimensionality without reducing data collection, and you have spent interpretability to do it.
- **"Why did our loadings flip overnight?"** — Two mechanisms: sign indeterminacy (cosmetic, fix with a sign convention) and small eigengaps (substantive — Davis–Kahan says sensitivity scales as $1/\text{gap}$, so only the subspace is identified). Naming both, and distinguishing them, is the answer that separates candidates.
- **"PC1 of 500 stocks' return correlations explains 40% with all-positive loadings — what is it?"** — The market factor, with Perron–Frobenius explaining why the top eigenvector of an all-positive matrix has one sign. Then note $d>n$ on any normal window, so the rest of the spectrum needs shrinkage or random-matrix filtering before it means anything.
- **"When does PCA destroy your signal?"** — When the target lives in a low-variance direction, since PCA is target-blind by construction. Give the concrete construction — a high-variance nuisance feature and a low-variance near-deterministic one — and name PLS as the supervised method built for exactly this.

- Marchenko, V. A., Pastur, L. A. 1967. "Distribution of eigenvalues for some sets of random matrices." *Matematicheskii Sbornik* 72(4):507–536. The noise bulk that a scree plot cannot distinguish from structure.
- Baik, J., Ben Arous, G., Péché, S. 2005. "Phase transition of the largest eigenvalue for non-null complex sample covariance matrices." *Annals of Probability* 33(5):1643–1697. The detectability threshold for a signal spike.
- Johnstone, I. M. 2001. "On the distribution of the largest eigenvalue in principal components analysis." *Annals of Statistics* 29(2):295–327. The spiked-covariance model.
- Davis, C., Kahan, W. M. 1970. "The rotation of eigenvectors by a perturbation. III." *SIAM Journal on Numerical Analysis* 7(1):1–46. Eigenvector sensitivity as inverse eigengap.
- Laloux, L., Cizeau, P., Bouchaud, J.-P., Potters, M. 1999. "Noise dressing of financial correlation matrices." *Physical Review Letters* 83(7):1467–1470. Most of an equity correlation spectrum is noise.
- Ledoit, O., Wolf, M. 2004. "A well-conditioned estimator for large-dimensional covariance matrices." *Journal of Multivariate Analysis* 88(2):365–411. Shrinkage for the $d\gtrsim n$ regime.
- Wold, S., Sjöström, M., Eriksson, L. 2001. "PLS-regression: a basic tool of chemometrics." *Chemometrics and Intelligent Laboratory Systems* 58(2):109–130. The supervised counterpart to PCR.
- Halko, N., Martinsson, P.-G., Tropp, J. A. 2011. "Finding structure with randomness." *SIAM Review* 53(2):217–288. The randomized SVD behind large-scale truncated solvers.
