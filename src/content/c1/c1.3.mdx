## TL;DR

Linear regression does two different jobs, and most of the confusion around it comes from running them together. It draws the best linear summary of a relationship. It also tells us how uncertain that summary is.

**OLS** chooses the coefficients that make the squared residuals as small as possible. The **beta estimator** is the rule that turns a random sample into those coefficients. Because a different sample would produce different coefficients, every estimate needs a **standard error**. A **$t$-statistic** expresses the estimate in standard-error units; a **$p$-value** asks how surprising a statistic at least that extreme would be if a stated null hypothesis were true. None of these numbers proves causality or practical importance.

The rest of the chapter grows from that foundation. **BLUE** says OLS has the smallest variance among *linear, unbiased* estimators under the Gauss–Markov assumptions—not that it is the best conceivable predictor. **Ridge and lasso** deliberately introduce bias to stabilize estimates, with Gaussian and Laplace priors as their Bayesian interpretations. **VIF** measures how much redundant predictors inflate coefficient uncertainty. **PCA** rotates features into variance-ranked directions; PCR drops some directions, while ridge shrinks them smoothly. **Logistic regression** carries the same estimation logic to binary outcomes using a Bernoulli likelihood.

## What you'll be able to do after this lesson

- Derive the OLS normal equations two ways (calculus and orthogonal projection) and state when a closed form exists.
- Show OLS is MLE under Gaussian noise, and state the Gauss–Markov theorem precisely (including what it does *not* require).
- Write the sampling distribution of $\hat\beta$ and use it for standard errors, $t$-tests, and confidence intervals.
- Derive ridge's closed form, explain lasso's sparsity, and identify both as MAP estimation under Gaussian/Laplace priors.
- Define VIF, explain variance inflation, and separate the *inference* damage of collinearity from its (often negligible) *prediction* damage.

## Why these two models, before the fancy ones

> **Goal — master the substrate every other model generalizes, and the statistical vocabulary interviews probe.** Trees, boosting, and nets are elaborations on "fit a function, define a loss, estimate parameters." Linear and logistic regression are the cases where you can do the estimation theory exactly — closed forms, sampling distributions, provable optimality — so they're where interviewers test whether you understand *estimation*, not just `model.fit`.

There is a version of this material you can skip if you only ever call a gradient booster. Quant-research interviews are not that version. They live here: factor models are OLS, "betas" are regression slopes, and the failure modes (collinear factors, overfit backtests, heteroskedastic returns) are the failure modes of regression. So we treat the statistics as first-class.

Before the derivations, five words that will otherwise slow you down. Skim them now and come back when one bites.

> **Vocabulary primer.** The "rusty staff engineer" assumption from c1.1 still holds.
>
> - **Design matrix** $X$ — the $n \times p$ matrix of features (a column of 1s gives the intercept).
> - **Estimator** — a function of the data that produces a parameter estimate $\hat\beta$. It's a *random variable* (it depends on the random sample), so it has a distribution.
> - **Unbiased / consistent / efficient / BLUE** — $\mathbb{E}[\hat\beta]=\beta$; $\hat\beta\to\beta$ as $n\to\infty$; minimum variance among a class; **B**est **L**inear **U**nbiased **E**stimator.
> - **Logit / odds** — $\text{logit}(p)=\log\frac{p}{1-p}$, the log-odds. Logistic regression makes the log-odds linear in $x$.
> - **MAP** — maximum a posteriori: the Bayesian point estimate that maximizes likelihood × prior.

## Part 1 — Ordinary least squares

> **Goal — understand the problem OLS solves before deriving it, then follow the chain from a fitted line to standard errors, $t$-statistics, $p$-values, and confidence intervals.**

### Start with the picture: signal plus noise

Forget matrices for a moment. Suppose we want to relate hours studied, $x$, to exam score, $y$. Students who study more tend to score higher, but not by a fixed amount, and not without exceptions. A linear model splits that observation in two: a clean part we intend to describe, and everything else. It says

$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i$$

Read that out loud: student $i$'s score equals a baseline $\beta_0$, plus $\beta_1$ points for every hour studied, plus a leftover $\varepsilon_i$ specific to that student. The line $\beta_0+\beta_1x$ is the systematic part. The error $\varepsilon_i$ collects everything the line does not explain: sleep, prior knowledge, luck, measurement error, and genuine randomness.

Now a distinction that costs people interview points every day. Three objects that look similar must stay separate:

- $\beta_1$ is the unknown **population parameter**: the slope in the data-generating model.
- $\hat\beta_1$ is an **estimate** computed from one observed sample.
- $b_1$ is sometimes used for the realized numerical value, such as $2.3$ points per hour.

The hat is not decoration. We never observe the true $\beta$. We observe data and use an **estimator**—a recipe—to produce $\hat\beta$. If we repeatedly sampled new students, the recipe would return a slightly different slope each time. Regression inference is about that repeated-sample variation. Hold on to that sentence; the next ten sections are all consequences of it.

> **Core mental model.** A coefficient is not just a number attached to a feature. It is a noisy measurement of an unknown relationship, conditional on the other features in the model.

### What “best-fitting line” means

"Best-fitting" is not self-defining. Pick a candidate line, and for each point you can measure how badly the line missed. The residual is

$$e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$$

In English: observed minus predicted. It is the vertical gap between the observed point and the line. Some residuals are positive, some negative.

So the obvious first idea is to add up the residuals and pick the line that makes the total smallest. That fails immediately. Adding raw residuals would let them cancel, so a wildly wrong line with a $+50$ miss and a $-50$ miss scores the same as a perfect one. You need a summary that treats a miss as a miss regardless of direction. OLS adds their squares:

$$\operatorname{RSS}(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)^2$$

Each term is one point's miss, squared, and the sum runs over every observation.

**Ordinary least squares chooses the line with the smallest residual sum of squares.** Squaring does three useful things: it prevents cancellation, penalizes large misses heavily, and produces a smooth convex objective with a closed-form optimum. It also makes OLS sensitive to outliers; that is the price of the choice. One point ten units away contributes as much as a hundred points one unit away.

Now solve it for the simplest case. For one centered predictor, the slope has an especially intuitive form:

$$\hat\beta_1=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)}$$

Read the fraction top to bottom. The numerator asks whether $x$ and $y$ move together. The denominator asks how much $x$ moved. If $x$ barely varies, the data cannot reveal how $y$ changes with $x$, so the slope becomes unstable. That is a fact about the world before it is a fact about algebra: if every student in your sample studied between 3.0 and 3.1 hours, no amount of careful grading tells you the effect of an extra hour. This “signal divided by available variation” intuition will return in standard errors and VIF.

The intercept is $\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x$, so the OLS line passes through $(\bar x,\bar y)$. The fitted line always goes through the middle of the data. Its literal interpretation—expected $y$ when $x=0$—may be meaningless if zero is outside the observed range. Nobody in your sample studied zero hours, so the intercept is an extrapolation dressed up as a coefficient. Centering $x$ makes the intercept the expected outcome at an ordinary, in-range value.

### From one line to many predictors

One predictor is where the intuition lives, but nothing real has one predictor. With $p$ coefficients, including an intercept if present, write

$$y=X\beta+\varepsilon$$

Each row of $X$ is one observation; each column is one predictor. Same equation as before, stacked. A coefficient $\beta_j$ means the expected change in $y$ for a one-unit change in $x_j$, **holding the other included predictors fixed**. That final clause is the source of both the usefulness and danger of multiple regression: adding a control can isolate a relationship, but a bad control can also change the question or introduce bias. "Holding fixed" is an instruction to the algebra, not something you actually did in the world.

The objective becomes

$$\operatorname{RSS}(\beta)=\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2$$

which says exactly what the sum of squares said: take the vector of residuals and measure its squared length.

### The normal equations, two ways

*Calculus.* Differentiate the total squared error and set its slope to zero:

$$\nabla_\beta \operatorname{RSS}=-2X^\top(y-X\beta)=0$$

The gradient is a vector of partial derivatives, one per coefficient, and at the bottom of a bowl every one of them is zero. Therefore

$$
X^\top X\hat\beta=X^\top y
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
\boxed{\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y}
$$

This formula exists when $X$ has full column rank: no column is an exact combination of the others. The Hessian is $2X^\top X\succeq0$, so RSS is convex and this stationary point is a global minimum. There is exactly one bottom to the bowl, which is why linear regression has no local-minimum story to tell. In numerical software, do not literally form the inverse; QR or SVD solvers are more stable. The inverse is valuable because it exposes the statistics.

*Projection (the geometric intuition).* Here is the same result with no derivatives. The columns of $X$ span every prediction vector the model can produce. Think of them as a flat sheet living inside $n$-dimensional space: everything the model could possibly output lies on that sheet. Usually $y$ is not exactly in that space. OLS chooses the reachable vector $\hat y=X\hat\beta$ closest to $y$: its orthogonal projection. Drop a perpendicular from $y$ to the sheet. At the closest point, the leftover residual $e=y-\hat y$ is perpendicular to every column of $X$:

$$X^\top e=0$$

Each entry of that vector equation is one predictor dotted with the residual, and every one of them is zero.

These are the same normal equations. They say the model has extracted every linear pattern available in its included columns; no predictor has any remaining *sample correlation* with the residual.

The fitted values are $\hat y=Hy$, where

$$H=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$$

is the **hat matrix** because it puts the “hat” on $y$. Feed it $y$, get $\hat y$. It is a projection: symmetric, idempotent ($H^2=H$), with $\operatorname{trace}(H)=p$. Idempotent is the algebra saying something obvious in geometry: once you are on the sheet, projecting again does nothing. The diagonal $h_{ii}$ measures **leverage**—how unusual observation $i$ is in feature space and how strongly it can pull its own fitted value.

### Fit is not truth

A fitted line is a summary of one sample under one set of choices. OLS always finds the best linear fit *within the chosen features and sample*. That does not imply:

- the relationship is causal;
- the conditional mean is truly linear;
- omitted variables are harmless;
- predictions will generalize;
- the coefficient is precisely estimated.

That last one is the one people forget, and it cuts both ways. A tiny coefficient can be estimated precisely; a large coefficient can be mostly noise. This is why regression tables report both estimates and uncertainty. A number without its error bar is half a result.

### OLS is MLE under Gaussian noise — the c1.2 throughline

Squared error looked like a convenient choice a few sections ago. It is also a forced one, once you commit to a particular story about the noise.

Assume $\varepsilon_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$. The log-likelihood is

$$\log L(\beta,\sigma^2) = -\tfrac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i - x_i^\top\beta)^2$$

Look at where $\beta$ appears. It appears in exactly one place: the sum on the right, which is RSS. For fixed $\sigma^2$, every term except RSS is constant in $\beta$. Maximizing likelihood is therefore identical to minimizing RSS. **OLS is MLE under independent Gaussian noise.** The probabilistic story explains the loss: Gaussian noise gives squared loss; Laplace noise gives absolute loss; heavy-tailed noise motivates robust alternatives. Every loss function is a claim about noise, whether or not you meant to make one.

Now the qualification, because interviewers reach for it. Gaussian errors are *not* needed to draw the OLS line. They make the usual finite-sample inference exact. That distinction matters constantly in interviews.

### Why the beta estimator is random

We keep saying $\hat\beta$ is a random variable. Here is the line of algebra that makes it undeniable. Substitute the data-generating model into the OLS formula:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat\beta
&=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top(X\beta+\varepsilon)\\
&=\beta+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line just swaps in $y=X\beta+\varepsilon$. The second line multiplies out, and the $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X$ collapses to the identity, leaving $\beta$ untouched plus a term built entirely from the noise.

This is the central identity of linear regression. It says:

$$\text{estimate}=\text{truth}+\text{weighted noise}$$

The true $\beta$ is fixed. The noise changes from sample to sample, so $\hat\beta$ changes too. Every standard error, $t$-statistic and confidence interval in this lesson is a statement about that second term. If $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$, then

$$\mathbb E[\hat\beta\mid X]=\beta$$

and OLS is unbiased. Unbiased does **not** mean this sample's estimate equals truth. It means the estimator is centered on truth across repeated samples. Your one estimate can still be badly off.

If $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I$, then

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta\mid X)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$$

Read the two factors separately. $\sigma^2$ is how noisy the world is. $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ is how well your design matrix pins down the coefficients, and it gets large exactly when $X^\top X$ is close to singular. Coefficient uncertainty is larger when outcomes are noisier ($\sigma^2$ is large), when the sample is smaller, when a predictor has little variation, or when predictors are redundant. This one covariance matrix anticipates standard errors, VIF, and regularization. Everything downstream is a reading of it.

### BLUE: a carefully limited promise

BLUE means **Best Linear Unbiased Estimator**. Four words, each of which is doing restrictive work:

- **Estimator:** a rule for turning data into an estimate.
- **Linear:** the estimator is a linear function of the observed outcomes $y$; it does not mean merely that the fitted relationship is linear.
- **Unbiased:** its repeated-sample mean is the true $\beta$.
- **Best:** it has the smallest variance among estimators satisfying the preceding two restrictions.

Under linear specification, full column rank, exogeneity $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$, and spherical errors $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I$, the **Gauss–Markov theorem** says OLS is BLUE.

The word “best” is fenced in, and the fence is the whole point. Gauss–Markov does not say OLS has the lowest prediction error among all models. It does not compare OLS with biased estimators such as ridge. It does not establish causality. And it does **not require Normal errors**. Normality is needed for exact small-sample $t$ and $F$ distributions, not for BLUE. If you take one sentence from this section into an interview, take that one.

The proof idea is intuitive: any other linear unbiased estimator equals OLS plus an extra linear piece that cannot add signal without introducing bias. Under spherical noise, that extra piece adds a positive-semidefinite amount of variance: $\sigma^2DD^\top\succeq0$. You can only add variance, never subtract it, so OLS wins inside the fence.

### Standard error: how much the estimate would move

Under Gaussian errors,

$$\hat\beta \sim \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\beta,\ \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}\bigr)$$

which reads: the estimator is Normally distributed, centered on the truth, with the covariance we just derived.

(and it is often approximately Normal in large samples without Gaussian errors). The **standard error** is the estimated standard deviation of this sampling distribution:

$$\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)=\sqrt{\hat\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}_{jj}}$$

Take the $j$-th diagonal entry of the covariance matrix, scale it by the estimated noise level, take the square root. That is the width of the cloud of slopes you would get from resampling.

We do not know the noise level, so we estimate the unknown noise variance using

$$\hat\sigma^2=\frac{\operatorname{RSS}}{n-p}$$

Why $n-p$? Fitting $p$ coefficients uses $p$ degrees of freedom. The residuals you observe are smaller than the true errors, because the fit spent those degrees of freedom bending toward the data. Dividing by $n$ would treat fitted residuals as if no parameters had been learned and would underestimate the noise variance. (The Gaussian MLE uses RSS/$n$ and is biased downward; RSS/$(n-p)$ is unbiased under the classical model.)

Say what a standard error is in one sentence, out loud. A standard error answers: **if we repeatedly collected comparable samples and refit the same model, roughly how much would this coefficient estimate vary?** It is not the standard deviation of the predictor, not the residual standard deviation, and not the typical prediction error. Those three are the usual wrong answers.

For simple regression, the slope SE is roughly

$$\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}}$$

Read the fraction. On top, how noisy the outcome is. On the bottom, how spread out your $x$ values are. This makes the levers visible: less outcome noise, more observations, and a wider spread of $x$ all produce a more precise slope. It is the arithmetic version of something you already believe, which is that a line is easier to pin down when you have points far out at both ends to pin it with.

### The $t$-statistic: signal measured in noise units

> **Slower lane.** This subsection and the two after it assume less background than the rest of the lesson: if "sampling distribution" and "null hypothesis" are words you have nodded along to without ever pinning down, start here.

**The question a $t$-statistic answers is "is this number big?" — and the whole trick is that "big" is meaningless until you say *big compared to what*.**

Suppose you fit sales against ad spend across $n=12$ stores and get a slope of $\hat\beta_1 = 4.2$: each extra 1k USD of ads is associated with 4.2k USD more sales. Is that real, or did the fit just chase noise?

You cannot answer from $4.2$ alone. Run the same experiment in a parallel universe — 12 *different* stores drawn from the same population — and you would get a different number: maybe $3.6$, maybe $5.1$. The slope is a random quantity, because the sample is random. The spread of those hypothetical re-runs is exactly what the standard error estimates. So:

- If $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = 1.5$, re-runs would routinely land anywhere from about $1$ to $7$. A slope of $4.2$ is a meaningful distance from zero.
- If $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = 6.0$, re-runs would scatter from $-8$ to $+16$. The same $4.2$ is now indistinguishable from a fluke.

Notice you just did the reasoning without any vocabulary. The $t$-statistic just makes that comparison explicit. It measures the estimate in standard-error units, converting "4.2k USD per 1k USD" into "how many noise-widths from zero":

$$t_j=\frac{\hat\beta_j-\beta_{j,0}}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)}$$

Estimate minus null value, divided by the noise width. Here $\beta_{j,0}$ is the value the **null hypothesis** proposes — the boring claim you are trying to rule out, almost always "this predictor does nothing," $\beta_{j,0}=0$. With that null, the formula is just estimate ÷ SE:

$$t_1 = \frac{4.2}{1.5} = 2.8 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad t_1 = \frac{4.2}{6.0} = 0.7$$

Same slope, same business story, opposite verdicts. A $t$ of $2.8$ says the estimate sits nearly three noise-widths above zero, which random sampling rarely manages by accident. A $t$ of $0.7$ says it sits well inside the range noise produces all the time.

Two things a $t$-statistic is *not*. It is not a measure of effect size — a huge $t$ can accompany an economically trivial slope, if $n$ is large enough to pin down a tiny number precisely. And it is not evidence of causality; it is a statement about one coefficient inside one assumed model.

#### Why the reference curve is Student's $t$, not a Normal

Under the classical model the standardised estimate follows

$$t_j\sim t_{n-p}$$

meaning: compare your $t$ against a Student's $t$ curve with $n-p$ degrees of freedom, not against a bell curve. The reason it is a $t$ rather than a standard Normal is worth understanding, because it is a stock interview question.

If we somehow *knew* the true noise level $\sigma$, then $(\hat\beta_j-\beta_j)/\operatorname{SE}$ would be exactly standard Normal — a known constant in the denominator just rescales a Normal. But we do not know $\sigma$. We estimate it from the same small sample, as $\hat\sigma^2=\operatorname{RSS}/(n-p)$, and that estimate is itself noisy.

So the denominator wobbles too. That is the entire story: you are dividing one random thing by another random thing. Formally the statistic has the shape

$$t = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/\nu}},\qquad Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\quad V\sim\chi^2_\nu \ \text{independent of } Z$$

A standard Normal on top, and on the bottom the square root of a chi-squared divided by its degrees of freedom, with the two independent of each other.

Dividing a Normal by a random, sometimes-too-small denominator produces occasional large values that a fixed denominator never would. Every so often you underestimate the noise and the ratio blows up. Those extra large values are precisely the $t$ distribution's heavier tails — it is the price of not knowing $\sigma$.

Watch the two curves near the middle first: they are nearly the same height and the same shape. Now follow them out to the edges, where the $t$ refuses to hug the axis as fast. That gap in the tails is the entire difference, and it is what changes your cutoffs.

**Properties worth carrying into an interview.**

- **Symmetric and centred at zero**, like the Normal, and bell-shaped — the difference is entirely in the tails, not the location.
- **Heavier tails, lower peak.** More probability sits far from zero, so the same cutoff captures less area.
- **One parameter: degrees of freedom $\nu = n-p$** — the sample size minus the coefficients you spent estimating the fit.
- **Variance is $\nu/(\nu-2)$ for $\nu>2$** — always greater than the Normal's $1$, and it *blows up* as $\nu\to2$. For $\nu\le2$ the variance is infinite, and at $\nu=1$ the $t$ is the Cauchy distribution, which has no mean at all.
- **It converges to the standard Normal as $\nu\to\infty$.** By $\nu\approx30$ the two are close enough that the distinction rarely changes a decision.

That convergence is what licenses the familiar "$|t|$ above $2$ means significant" shortcut — and it also shows when the shortcut lies:

<TCriticalVsDf />

Read this one from right to left. At large degrees of freedom the critical value settles near $2$, which is where the folk rule comes from. Now walk left, toward small samples, and watch how fast the bar rises.

With $\nu=10$, a $t$ of exactly $2.0$ gives a two-sided $p$ of $0.073$: *not* significant at 5%, despite clearing the folk threshold. You would need $|t|>2.23$. At $\nu=1$ you would need $|t|>12.7$. The rule of thumb is a large-sample convenience, not a law — which is why the ad-spend example above, with $\nu=12-2=10$ and $t=2.8$, is genuinely significant ($p=0.019$) while a $t$ of $2.0$ from the same study would not have been.

### The $p$-value: a probability under the null, not of the null

You now have a $t$. Turning it into a probability is one more step. For a two-sided test, the $p$-value is

$$p=\Pr\left(|T_{n-p}|\ge |t_{\text{observed}}|\mid H_0\right)$$

The bar means "given that", so the whole expression is a probability computed inside a world where the null is true.

It asks: **if the true coefficient were the null value and the model assumptions held, how often would random sampling produce a $t$-statistic at least this extreme?**

Concretely: draw the curve of $t$-values the null would generate, mark where your $t$ landed, and shade everything at least that far out. The shaded area *is* the $p$-value. Not a proxy for it, not a transformation of it. The area.

<PValueAsArea />

Read the picture in the order the logic runs. You start by *assuming* the null — the curve is what the world looks like if $\beta$ really is zero. Your observed $t$ is one draw. If it lands in the fat middle, the null explains your data comfortably. If it lands out in a thin tail, then either the null is true and you got unlucky, or the null is wrong. The $p$-value only quantifies the first branch: how unlucky you would have had to be. It never tells you the probability of the second.

That asymmetry is the source of nearly every misreading below.

It is not:

- the probability that $H_0$ is true;
- the probability the result occurred “by chance”;
- the size or business importance of the effect;
- evidence of causality;
- the probability the result will replicate.

A small $p$-value is evidence against the null *within the assumed model*. Both halves of that sentence carry weight. With huge $n$, trivial effects can have tiny $p$-values. With small or noisy data, important effects can miss a conventional threshold. Always report the estimate and uncertainty, not a significance star alone.

### Confidence intervals: plausible parameter values on the same scale

A test gives you a verdict. An interval gives you the same information in the units of the problem, which is usually what somebody actually wants. A $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval is

$$\hat\beta_j\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-p}\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)$$

Estimate, plus and minus a critical value times the noise width.

A 95% procedure means that, over repeated samples, 95% of intervals constructed this way cover the fixed true parameter. The 95% is a property of the recipe, not of the interval sitting in front of you. After observing one interval, frequentist language does not assign a 95% probability to the fixed $\beta$ being inside it. Keep hold of that distinction even though it sounds like pedantry, because it is the same move as the $p$-value being a statement about your procedure. Practically, the interval is more informative than a binary test: it displays the effect sizes compatible with the data and assumptions.

For a two-sided test at level $\alpha$, “the interval excludes zero” and “$p<\alpha$” are the same decision. Two presentations, one arithmetic.

### One worked regression-table reading

Put all of it together on a single row of output. Imagine a factor loading estimate of $0.30$ with SE $0.10$:

$$t=0.30/0.10=3.0$$

The estimate is three SEs from zero, producing a small two-sided $p$-value and an approximate 95% interval $0.30\pm1.96(0.10)=[0.10,0.50]$ in a large sample.

Now say what that means to somebody who does not do statistics. The conclusion is not “the factor definitely matters.” It is: conditional on the model, this sample is inconsistent with a zero loading, and values around $0.10$ to $0.50$ remain compatible with the data. A loading of $0.12$ and a loading of $0.48$ are both still on the table, and they imply very different portfolios.

Now imagine adding a nearly duplicate factor. The point estimate might remain $0.30$, but its SE could jump to $0.40$. Then $t=0.75$ and the interval crosses zero. Nothing about the world changed between those two runs. The observed association did not disappear; our ability to assign it uniquely to this factor did. That is multicollinearity, developed below.

### Six perturbations: what actually moves in the regression table

Interviewers love this family of questions because each one isolates a different part of the machinery, and because the intuitive answer is wrong about half the time. The pattern to internalise: **manipulations that add no new information leave the point estimates alone and corrupt the uncertainty.**

| Manipulation | Slopes $\hat\beta$ | Intercept | $R^2$ | Standard errors | $t$-stats | Unbiased / BLUE? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Add a pure-noise predictor | ~unchanged | ~unchanged | weakly **↑** (never ↓) | **↑** | **↓** | Yes, but less efficient |
| Duplicate a predictor column | **not identified** | unchanged | unchanged | **∞ / undefined** | undefined | Sum is estimable; individuals are not |
| Duplicate every row | **exactly unchanged** | unchanged | **exactly unchanged** | **× ≈ 1/√2** | **× ≈ √2** | Unbiased, but inference invalid |
| Duplicate one observation | **changes** | changes | changes | change | change | Unbiased; SE understated |
| Regress $x$ on $y$ instead | $\rho\,\sigma_x/\sigma_y$ (not $1/\hat\beta$) | changes | **same $\rho^2$** | change | same $t$ for $H_0$ of no relation | Answers a different question |
| Orthogonal (total) least squares | between the two OLS lines | changes | n/a | n/a | n/a | Not scale-invariant |

Work down the rows one at a time.

**1. Adding a pure-noise predictor.** $R^2$ can only go up, never down — the old fit is still available to the optimiser, so RSS cannot increase. Setting the new coefficient to zero reproduces the old model exactly, and the optimiser will not choose something worse than an option it already has. That makes plain $R^2$ useless for model selection. Adjusted $R^2$ has a crisp rule that is worth memorising: **it increases if and only if the new variable's $|t|$ exceeds $1$** — a much lower bar than significance. The other coefficients stay unbiased (including an irrelevant regressor does not violate exogeneity), but they lose precision: you spend a degree of freedom and, unless the noise column happens to be exactly orthogonal to the rest in-sample, you inflate their variance. Estimates honest, $t$-stats quieter.

**2. Duplicating a predictor column.** Now $X$ has two identical columns, $X^\top X$ is singular, and there is no unique $\hat\beta$: if $(\beta_1,\beta_2)$ fits, so does $(\beta_1+c,\ \beta_2-c)$ for any $c$. Shift credit from one twin to the other and the predictions do not move at all. What *is* pinned down is the column space, so the fitted values, residuals, RSS and $R^2$ are all completely unchanged. Only the attribution breaks. What software returns depends on the solver: a pseudoinverse (`numpy.linalg.lstsq`, scikit-learn) returns the minimum-norm solution, which splits the coefficient **equally** between the twins; R's `lm()` drops one and reports `NA`. Ridge also splits it equally — with an $L^2$ penalty, $(\beta/2,\beta/2)$ costs strictly less than $(\beta,0)$. Lasso, whose penalty is indifferent between those splits, has a genuinely non-unique solution and typically picks one arbitrarily. The estimable quantity throughout is the **sum** $\beta_1+\beta_2$; that combination is unbiased, and only it has a finite standard error.

**3. Duplicating every row.** Stack the dataset on top of itself. Every sum in the normal equations runs over twice as many terms, each pair identical, so both $X^\top X$ and $X^\top y$ exactly double, and the factor of two cancels: $\hat\beta$ is **identical**, to the last digit. TSS and RSS both double, so $R^2$ is identical too. But the variance formula sees $2n$ observations: $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta)$ picks up $(2X^\top X)^{-1}$, and

$$\frac{\operatorname{SE}_{\text{doubled}}}{\operatorname{SE}_{\text{original}}}=\sqrt{\frac{n-p}{2n-p}}\ \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\ \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$

so every standard error shrinks by about $30\%$ and every $t$-statistic inflates by about $\sqrt2$. Nothing was learned; the model merely counted the same evidence twice. The estimates stay unbiased, but the inference is fraudulent, because the errors of duplicated rows are perfectly correlated — the independence assumption behind the SE formula is gone. **This is the cleanest illustration of the whole section: point estimates survive fake data, uncertainty does not.** (It is also why clustered standard errors exist: repeated measurements on the same entity are a softer version of the same problem.)

**4. Duplicating a single observation.** Different beast. Copying one row is exactly **weighted least squares with weight 2 on that point**, so it *does* move the fit — toward that observation. How far depends on leverage: duplicating a point sitting at the centre of the $x$ cloud barely moves the line, while duplicating a high-leverage point can visibly tilt it. Everything in the table changes. The estimate remains unbiased (any fixed set of weights preserves unbiasedness given exogeneity), but if the row is a *copy* rather than a genuinely new draw, the standard errors are again understated.

> **Stop and think.** Duplicating *all* rows leaves $\hat\beta$ untouched, yet duplicating *one* row changes it. Reconcile these. *Hint: uniform reweighting rescales the normal equations by a constant that cancels; non-uniform reweighting changes their relative weighting, which is a different optimisation problem.*

**5. Regressing $x$ on $y$ instead of $y$ on $x$.** Most people's first instinct is that you should get the reciprocal slope. You do not. The two slopes are

$$\hat\beta_{y\sim x}=\rho\,\frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_x},\qquad \hat\beta_{x\sim y}=\rho\,\frac{\sigma_x}{\sigma_y},\qquad\text{so}\qquad \hat\beta_{y\sim x}\cdot\hat\beta_{x\sim y}=\rho^2$$

Multiply the two slopes together and the correlation squared falls out, not $1$. The second slope is **not** the reciprocal of the first — it equals the reciprocal only when $\rho^2=1$, i.e. a perfect fit. Since $\rho^2\le1$, each line is *flatter* than inverting the other would suggest. That gap is regression to the mean, and it has a mechanical cause: OLS minimises vertical distances, so swapping the roles of the axes minimises horizontal distances instead — a different objective, hence a different line. Both lines do pass through $(\bar x,\bar y)$, and both report the same $R^2=\rho^2$. The lesson for practice: "which variable goes on the left" is a modelling decision about what you want to predict, not a symmetric description of association.

**6. Orthogonal regression, and why PCA is a third answer.** If neither variable is naturally the response — both are noisy measurements of something — you can minimise *perpendicular* distance instead. That is **total least squares**, and its solution is the **first principal component** of the joint $(x,y)$ cloud. Its slope lies between the $y\sim x$ line and the inverted $x\sim y$ line. It is consistent under symmetric errors-in-variables — the regime where OLS instead suffers **attenuation bias**, shrinking its slope toward zero by the reliability ratio $\sigma_x^2/(\sigma_x^2+\sigma_u^2)$ because noise in $x$ inflates the denominator of the slope without touching its numerator ([derived in the quiz](/c/c1/c1.3/quiz)). The catch is that perpendicular distance mixes the two axes' units, so **TLS is not scale-invariant**: rescale $x$ from dollars to cents and the fitted line changes, whereas OLS slopes simply rescale in the obvious way. Standardise first, or do not use it.

Do not confuse that with **principal component regression**, which is a different procedure with a different purpose: project $X$ onto its top-$k$ principal components and regress $y$ on those. PCR is a variance-reduction method — biased, but stabilising when predictors are collinear, since it discards exactly the low-variance directions that inflate $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta)$. Its known weakness is that the components are chosen using $X$ alone, so a low-variance direction that happens to carry all the signal about $y$ gets thrown away; partial least squares exists to fix precisely that.

### When the usual standard error is wrong

Every SE formula so far assumed the noise was well behaved. Real data rarely obliges, and the interesting part is that the damage is narrower than people expect.

> **Stop and think.** Your residuals are heteroskedastic (e.g. volatility clustering in returns). Is $\hat\beta$ still unbiased? What breaks? *Hint: $\hat\beta$ stays unbiased — exogeneity, not homoskedasticity, controls bias — but $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ is the wrong covariance, so your $t$-stats are wrong. Fix with **White/HC** robust standard errors (HC3 for small samples), or Newey–West (HAC) for autocorrelation.*

This distinction is central: coefficient assumptions and inference assumptions can fail separately. Sort the failures by which one they break.

- **Heteroskedasticity:** OLS may stay unbiased, but classical SEs are wrong. Use heteroskedasticity-consistent HC standard errors.
- **Autocorrelation:** time-series errors move together, so IID SEs are usually too optimistic. Use HAC/Newey–West or an explicit time-series model.
- **Clustering:** observations within a firm, user, school, or day share shocks. Cluster the SEs at the level where dependence occurs.
- **Endogeneity:** $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]\ne0$ due to omitted variables, simultaneity, selection, or measurement error. Robust SEs do not fix coefficient bias.

Notice the last bullet is the odd one out: it is the only failure that moves the estimate itself. Robust SEs change the estimated covariance, $t$-statistics, $p$-values, and intervals. They do not change the OLS point estimates.

### $R^2$ vs adjusted $R^2$

$R^2 = 1 - \text{RSS}/\text{TSS}$ is **monotonically non-decreasing** in the number of predictors: adding *any* column (even pure noise) can only shrink the min-RSS, because you're minimizing over a larger column space. So $R^2$ rises when you add junk features. **Adjusted $R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{RSS}/(n-p)}{\text{TSS}/(n-1)}$** penalizes $p$ and *can* fall. The adjusted version divides each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom first, so spending a parameter has to earn its keep. Reporting plain $R^2$ to argue model quality is a classic tell.

Say what the number means before trusting it. $R^2$ measures the fraction of *in-sample outcome variation* accounted for by the fitted linear model. It is not accuracy, causality, calibration, or out-of-sample performance. A low $R^2$ can coexist with a precisely estimated scientific effect; a high $R^2$ can coexist with biased coefficients or terrible extrapolation.

## Part 3 — Regularization (and its Bayesian meaning)

> **Goal — feel why unstable models need shrinkage before learning ridge and lasso as formulas, geometry, and priors.**

### The problem: many coefficients can explain the same sample

OLS is committed to unbiasedness. That sounds ideal until the data provide weak information about individual coefficients. With many features, little data, or correlated columns, a small change in the sample can cause a large change in $\hat\beta$. Training fit may look excellent while coefficients and future predictions swing wildly.

Make it concrete. Imagine two almost identical predictors, $x_1$ and $x_2$. The data may strongly support the *combined* effect $\beta_1+\beta_2$, but barely distinguish how the total should be split. Solutions such as $(5,-4)$, $(2,-1)$, and $(0.6,0.4)$ can produce similar fitted values. All three sum to about the same thing, and the fit only really sees the sum. OLS has no preference among large offsetting coefficients if they shave RSS. Regularization adds one: among similarly fitting solutions, prefer the simpler, smaller one.

The general objective is

$$\text{data-fit loss}+\lambda\times\text{coefficient complexity}$$

In words: pay for your misses, and also pay rent on the size of your coefficients. $\lambda$ is the strength of skepticism. Turn the knob and watch: at $\lambda=0$ we recover OLS; as $\lambda$ grows, coefficients are pulled harder toward zero. Choose $\lambda$ using validation or cross-validation—not the training RSS and not the test set.

> **Why shrinkage can win.** Prediction error depends on bias and variance. OLS has zero bias under its assumptions but can have enormous variance. Accepting a small systematic pull toward zero can reduce random sample-to-sample movement enough to lower total MSE.

### Ridge: share credit and stabilize

**Ridge (L2)** solves

$$\min_\beta\ \frac12\lVert y-X\beta\rVert^2+\frac\lambda2\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2$$

Squared error, plus $\lambda$ times the squared length of the coefficient vector.

and has the closed form

$$\hat\beta_{\text{ridge}}=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$$

which is the OLS formula with $\lambda$ added down the diagonal.

That small change is the whole mechanism. The $+\lambda I$ lifts small eigenvalues away from zero. The inverse therefore cannot explode along weakly identified directions, even when $p>n$ or $X$ is rank-deficient. Correlated features tend to **share** credit rather than receive unstable offsetting weights. Ridge usually keeps every coefficient nonzero.

Geometrically, ridge asks for the lowest-RSS contour that touches a round L2 ball. The smooth boundary rarely touches an axis, so exact zeros are rare.

### Lasso: spend a limited coefficient budget

**Lasso (L1)** solves

$$\min_\beta\ \frac12\lVert y-X\beta\rVert^2+\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_1$$

Same squared error, but the penalty is now the sum of absolute values rather than the sum of squares. Think of it as a budget: you have a fixed allowance of total coefficient magnitude to spend, and every feature you fund takes some of it.

The L1 constraint has diamond-shaped corners on the coordinate axes. An expanding RSS contour often touches a corner, making one or more coefficients exactly zero. Lasso therefore performs estimation and feature selection together.

There is no general closed form because $|\beta_j|$ is nondifferentiable at zero; coordinate descent and LARS are standard solvers. The kink that creates the sparsity is the same kink that destroys the algebra.

> **Why orthonormal, and why doesn't anyone talk about the general case?** The reason we specialize to $X^\top X = I$ is not a modelling assumption — it's an algebraic trick to *see* the mechanism. Under a general design, RSS is a coupled quadratic in $\beta$: the elliptical RSS contours are stretched and tilted (as in the figure above), and moving $\beta_j$ changes what the optimal $\beta_k$ should be. Orthonormality diagonalizes $X^\top X$, so RSS decouples into $p$ independent scalar problems in $(\beta_j - \hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}})^2$, and each 1D subproblem has a closed form. The sparsity picture (corner-touching) is fully general — but the tidy $\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}/(1+\lambda)$ and soft-thresholding formulas only survive in the decoupled case. The general case *is* handled by the KKT subgradient condition below; it just doesn't reduce to a per-coordinate formula because the coefficients are coupled through the residual. This is exactly why textbook derivations start orthonormal: they're the shortest path to the mechanism, not the story about your actual data.

With orthonormal columns, the contrast is transparent:

$$\hat\beta_j^{\text{ridge}}=\frac{\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}}{1+\lambda}$$

Take the OLS answer and divide it by a constant bigger than one.

$$\hat\beta_j^{\text{lasso}}=\operatorname{sign}(\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}})\max\left(|\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}|-\lambda,0\right)$$

Take the OLS answer, move it $\lambda$ closer to zero, and stop if you get there.

Ridge proportionally shrinks; lasso **soft-thresholds**, subtracting a fixed amount and setting weak coefficients exactly equal to zero. Here “exactly zero” means $\hat\beta_j=0$, so feature $j$ contributes $x_j\hat\beta_j=0$ to every prediction—the fitted model has excluded that feature.

<ShrinkageCurves lambda={0.6} caption="Estimated coefficient as a function of its OLS value, under an orthonormal design and λ = 0.6. Follow each curve from left to right and compare it against the dashed gray OLS identity line. Blue: ridge shrinks every coordinate proportionally — the line tilts down but never touches zero for a nonzero input. Red: lasso subtracts λ, then clips to zero — every OLS coefficient in the shaded [−λ, λ] band gets snapped exactly to 0. That flat interval is the 'dead zone' the L1 kink creates; ridge has no analogue." />

**Orthonormality is only what makes the closed form simple.** Lasso can produce exact-zero coefficients for a general design matrix too. Here is the argument that does not need the trick. Let $r=y-X\hat\beta$ be the residual at the lasso optimum. Its KKT/subgradient condition for feature $j$ is

$$
x_j^\top r=
\begin{cases}
\lambda, & \hat\beta_j>0,\\
-\lambda, & \hat\beta_j<0,\\
\text{some value in }[-\lambda,\lambda], & \hat\beta_j=0.
\end{cases}
$$

Read the three cases as one statement about how much a feature still correlates with what the model got wrong. An active feature must correlate with the residual by exactly $\lambda$, sign matching its coefficient; a zeroed feature only has to stay inside the band.

Thus a feature whose remaining correlation with the residual is too weak to overcome the penalty, $|x_j^\top r|\le\lambda$, can optimally receive coefficient zero. Unlike the orthonormal case, the coefficients are coupled: changing one coefficient changes $r$ and therefore changes the conditions for all the others. There is no general rule that independently thresholds each OLS coefficient.

Ridge, by contrast, has a smooth penalty and **no mechanism designed to create sparsity**. Its solution is generally dense. Be precise about the claim, though. A ridge coefficient can coincidentally equal zero because of symmetry or cancellation, so “ridge never produces zeros” is too absolute; the accurate claim is that ridge does not map a whole region of weak signals to zero and is not a feature-selection method.

One caution before you reach for lasso as a variable selector. With a group of highly correlated features, lasso may choose one somewhat arbitrarily and drop the rest. That can be useful for a compact predictor but dangerous for scientific interpretation: “selected” does not mean uniquely important. **Elastic net** combines L1 and L2, encouraging sparsity while making correlated features more likely to enter together.

### Scaling and the intercept

Penalties act on coefficient size, so feature scale changes the effective punishment. A coefficient for a feature measured in dollars will have a different numerical scale from one measured in millions of dollars. The penalty cannot tell the difference between a large coefficient and a small unit. **Standardize predictors before ridge or lasso**, typically inside a pipeline fit only on training data. The intercept is normally not penalized; centering the data separates the baseline level from shrinkage of slopes.

**The Bayesian view (ties straight back to c1.2).** MAP estimation maximizes $\log p(y\mid\beta) + \log p(\beta)$ — the negative log-prior *is* the penalty, exactly as the negative log-likelihood is the data loss. Gaussian likelihood + **Gaussian prior** $\beta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2 I)$ → **ridge** (with $\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2$). Gaussian likelihood + **Laplace prior** → **lasso**. The Laplace prior's heavier tail and sharp peak at 0 are *why* L1 induces sparsity while L2 doesn't. So: L2 penalty ⟺ Gaussian prior, L1 penalty ⟺ Laplace prior — the same "every loss is a noise model" lens, applied to the parameters.

The prior view supplies intuition for $\lambda$: larger $\lambda$ means a tighter prior around zero—stronger prior belief that large coefficients are unlikely. Notice that $\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2$ says the same thing in symbols, since a small prior width $\tau$ makes $\lambda$ large. MAP is still a point estimate; a full Bayesian analysis keeps the posterior distribution and propagates parameter uncertainty.

**Bias for variance — and why unbiased isn't sacred.** For a single coefficient, the decomposition is

$$
\mathbb E\left[(\hat\beta_j-\beta_j)^2\right]
=\left(\mathbb E[\hat\beta_j]-\beta_j\right)^2
+\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j)
$$

In words: coefficient MSE equals squared bias plus variance. How far off you are on average, split into how far off you are systematically and how much you bounce around. For the full coefficient vector, the corresponding total squared-error decomposition is

$$
\mathbb E\left[\lVert\hat\beta-\beta\rVert_2^2\right]
=\left\lVert\mathbb E[\hat\beta]-\beta\right\rVert_2^2
+\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta)\right)
$$

The trace appears because $\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta)$ is a matrix; its diagonal entries are the component variances, and their sum is the estimator's total variance. Ridge is biased, but its reduction in total variance can outweigh its squared bias and produce lower total MSE than OLS. Look at the two terms and the trade is visible: you accept a nonzero first term to make the second term much smaller. OLS minimizes variance only *among linear unbiased estimators*, an often-overlooked restriction. That shrinkage can beat the unbiased estimate on *total* MSE is a real effect, not a numerical hack. (The classic formal result is James–Stein (1961) on shrinkage dominance; for the precise inadmissibility statement and proof, ask an AI assistant like Claude or ChatGPT — an interviewer won't.) Ridge applies that bias–variance logic to regression.

One thing you give up in the trade. Regularized coefficients do not inherit ordinary OLS $p$-values. The penalty changes their bias and sampling distribution, while data-driven tuning adds selection uncertainty. Use regularization primarily for prediction and stability; use carefully designed post-selection or Bayesian methods when inference is the goal.

## Part 4 — Multicollinearity and VIF

> **Goal — understand collinearity as an information problem, derive what VIF measures, and separate unstable explanations from potentially stable predictions.**

### The intuition: the model cannot assign credit

Suppose salary is predicted from age and years of experience. These predictors carry overlapping information. Older employees usually have more experience. If they move almost together, the model may know that their *combined direction* predicts salary while being unable to determine which variable deserves the credit.

This is **multicollinearity**: one predictor is well approximated by a linear combination of the others. Notice what it is not a claim about. It does not mean the predictor is unrelated to $y$. It means the data contain little independent movement in that predictor after controlling for the others.

Return to the simple-regression slope intuition: denominator variation makes a coefficient estimable. In multiple regression, what matters for $\beta_j$ is the variation in $x_j$ left after removing the part predictable from all other columns. If almost nothing unique remains, a small amount of outcome noise can cause a large swing in $\hat\beta_j$.

There are two severities. Perfect collinearity—for example, including temperature in Celsius and Fahrenheit—makes $X^\top X$ singular. There are infinitely many coefficient vectors with identical predictions, so individual coefficients are not identified. Near-collinearity is less obvious: an answer exists, but it is unstable. The software returns a number and gives you no reason to distrust it, which is why we need a diagnostic.

### VIF: compare actual uncertainty with an orthogonal world

The **variance inflation factor** for predictor $j$ is

$$\text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}$$

where $R_j^2$ is the $R^2$ from regressing $x_j$ on *all the other predictors*. So you run a side regression that has nothing to do with $y$ at all: you try to predict one feature from the rest. It multiplies the coefficient's variance: $\text{Var}(\hat\beta_j) \propto \text{VIF}_j$, so $\text{SE}(\hat\beta_j)$ scales as $\sqrt{\text{VIF}_j}$. Rules of thumb: $\text{VIF}>5$ moderate, $>10$ serious ($R_j^2>0.9$); $\text{VIF}=1$ means orthogonal.

That auxiliary regression asks: **how replaceable is $x_j$ using the other features?** Put numbers on it.

- If $R_j^2=0$, none of $x_j$ is linearly predictable from the others. VIF $=1$: no inflation.
- If $R_j^2=0.8$, VIF $=5$. The coefficient variance is five times its orthogonal-design baseline and its SE is $\sqrt5\approx2.24$ times larger.
- If $R_j^2=0.99$, VIF $=100$. Only 1% of the predictor's variance is unique; its SE is inflated tenfold.

An interval ten times wider is a different conclusion, not a slightly worse one.

The useful identity is

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j\mid X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{\operatorname{SST}_j(1-R_j^2)}$$

where $\operatorname{SST}_j=\sum_i(x_{ij}-\bar x_j)^2$. Read the denominator as two separate gates the data has to pass. Uncertainty grows because of noisy outcomes ($\sigma^2$), little raw variation in the predictor (small SST), or little *unique* variation (large $R_j^2$). VIF isolates the third cause.

Thresholds such as 5 and 10 are triage rules, not laws. A high VIF is evidence about the design matrix, not an automatic command to delete a variable. Context decides whether individual coefficient attribution matters.

**Inference vs prediction.** Collinearity inflates coefficient variances → **unstable, sign-flipping coefficients and wide confidence intervals** → coefficient *inference* is wrecked. But the model's *predictions* on similar $X$ can be perfectly fine — collinearity introduces no bias, it just makes the individual coefficients un-identifiable. A tree model doesn't care for prediction at all (it just splits on one of the redundant features). This is the precise resolution to "is multicollinearity bad?": **bad for interpretation, often harmless for prediction.** (This connects directly to c1.1: redundant/collinear features are exactly what defeats the drop-column leakage test and split tree-importance.)

Why can predictions survive? If two features move together in both train and test data, many unstable coefficient splits yield nearly the same combined prediction. The $(5,-4)$ and $(0.6,0.4)$ solutions from Part 3 agree on every point where the two features stay in lockstep. Prediction becomes fragile when the future breaks that correlation or when we extrapolate in a direction the training data never explored.

### What to do about high VIF

There is no single fix, because the diagnosis is "your data lacks information" and different goals tolerate that differently. The response follows the goal:

- **Inference on a specific coefficient:** collect data with more independent variation, remove a genuinely redundant measurement, combine variables using domain knowledge, or redesign the study. No algebra can create missing information.
- **Prediction:** validate out of sample. Ridge often stabilizes predictions without discarding useful signal.
- **A group-level question:** jointly test related coefficients with an $F$-test rather than demanding that each be individually significant.
- **Dimensionality reduction:** PCA/PCR can replace correlated features with orthogonal components, at the cost of original-feature interpretation.

And one thing not to do. Do not mechanically drop the feature with the largest VIF until all VIFs fall below a threshold. Removing a confounder merely to reduce VIF can introduce omitted-variable bias. You will have traded a wide interval for a wrong center, which is the worse deal. Centering helps with nonessential collinearity in polynomial or interaction terms, but it does not remove genuine redundancy between distinct variables.

## The stats / quant-research lens (read this before a quant interview)

- **Estimator ladder.** *Unbiased* ($\mathbb{E}[\hat\beta]=\beta$), *consistent* ($\hat\beta\to^p\beta$), *efficient* (attains the Cramér–Rao bound; MLE is asymptotically efficient), *BLUE* (min-variance among linear unbiased — Gauss–Markov). The gaps matter: unbiased $\not\Rightarrow$ consistent and vice-versa; BLUE is "best" only within the linear class — a biased nonlinear estimator (ridge) can beat it in MSE.
- **Factor models are OLS.** CAPM / Fama–French / Carhart: $r_i - r_f = \alpha + \beta\cdot(\text{factors}) + \varepsilon$. The "betas" are regression slopes; $\alpha$ (the intercept) is tested for $\ne 0$ via its $t$-stat. Value/quality/size factors are correlated → high VIF → **unstable, sign-flipping factor loadings** even when portfolio *prediction* is fine.
- **Heteroskedastic, autocorrelated returns** → must use White (HC) or Newey–West (HAC) standard errors, or you "discover" spurious alpha.
- **Overfit backtests.** More factors mechanically raise in-sample $R^2$ → inflated backtest Sharpe. This is the multiple-testing / data-snooping problem — cross-reference c1.1 leakage, adjusted $R^2$, and CV.

## Tradeoff matrix

| Method | Estimates | Closed form? | Handles collinearity | Sparse? | Bayesian prior |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| OLS | Conditional mean (Gaussian MLE) | Yes | No (singular if rank-deficient) | No | None (flat) |
| Ridge | Shrunk mean | Yes: $(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$ | Yes (always invertible) | No | Gaussian |
| Lasso | Sparse mean | No (coordinate descent) | Partially | Yes | Laplace |
| Logistic | $P(y=1\mid x)$ (Bernoulli MLE) | No (IRLS) | via penalty | via L1 | via penalty |
| PCR | Mean on top-$m$ PCs | Yes (per $m$) | Yes (drops low-variance) | No (in PC space) | — |

## Worked decision walk-throughs

**Scenario A: you need interpretable coefficients with $p$-values (which factors matter?).** Use **statsmodels OLS** (or Logit) — it reports SEs, $t$-stats, $p$-values, CIs. Check VIFs first; if factors are collinear, the individual coefficients are untrustworthy even if the fit is good.
**Scenario B: $p > n$ or heavy collinearity, you care about prediction.** **Ridge.** It's always invertible and shrinks the unstable low-variance directions. If you also want feature selection, **lasso** or **elastic net**.

## Production failure modes

1. **Wanting $p$-values from sklearn.** sklearn gives a penalized point estimate and **no inference** (no SEs/$p$-values). Use statsmodels for inference; remember to **`add_constant`** (statsmodels does *not* add an intercept, unlike sklearn).
2. **VIF computed without an intercept.** `statsmodels`' `variance_inflation_factor` needs a constant column added first, or the VIFs are wrong (the implicit regressions are forced through the origin).
3. **Ridge vs Lasso $\alpha$ aren't on the same scale.** sklearn `Ridge` puts `alpha` on the raw RSS; `Lasso`/`ElasticNet` carry a $1/(2n)$ factor. Don't copy an $\alpha$ between them.
4. **Reporting in-sample $R^2$ as model quality.** It only goes up with more features; use adjusted $R^2$ or out-of-sample/CV.

## Interview gotchas

- **"Is OLS the minimum-variance unbiased estimator, and does it need Gaussian errors?"** — It's BLUE (best *linear* unbiased) by Gauss–Markov; normality is **not** required for BLUE, only for finite-sample $t$/$F$ inference. A nonlinear or biased estimator can beat it.
- **"When is a biased estimator better than OLS?"** — bias-variance: ridge trades bias for a larger variance cut → lower MSE. (The formal shrinkage-dominance result is James–Stein; ask an AI for the precise statement — an interviewer won't.)
- **"Ridge vs lasso — and what priors are they?"** — L2 shrinks / Gaussian prior; L1 zeros / Laplace prior; show soft-thresholding.
- **"Two predictors have VIF 50 — what's affected?"** — coefficient SEs ($\times\sqrt{50}$), sign stability, interpretation — but not necessarily prediction.
