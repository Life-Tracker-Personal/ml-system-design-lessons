<Quiz id="1">

**Q1.** Derive that the SVM margin width is $2/\|w\|$, starting from the canonical margin hyperplanes. Why does maximizing the margin equal minimizing $\tfrac12\|w\|^2$, and why must it be the *geometric* margin, not the functional margin?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Setup.** Decision function $f(x)=w^\top x + b$; fix the scale so the closest points satisfy $y_i(w^\top x_i+b)=1$. The margin hyperplanes are $w^\top x + b = \pm 1$.

**Derivation.** Take $x_+$ on the $+1$ plane and $x_-$ on the $-1$ plane: $w^\top x_+ + b = +1$, $w^\top x_- + b = -1$. Subtract: $w^\top(x_+ - x_-) = 2$. The margin width is the component of $x_+ - x_-$ along the unit normal $w/\|w\|$:

$$\text{margin} = \frac{w^\top (x_+ - x_-)}{\|w\|} = \frac{2}{\|w\|}$$

**Max margin ⇔ min $\tfrac12\|w\|^2$.** Maximizing $2/\|w\|$ is minimizing $\|w\|$, equivalently $\tfrac12\|w\|^2$ (monotone, and the square makes it a smooth convex quadratic with a clean gradient).

**Geometric vs functional.** The functional margin $y_i(w^\top x_i + b)$ can be made arbitrarily large by rescaling $(w,b)\to(cw,cb)$ — it's not scale-invariant, so maximizing it is meaningless. Dividing by $\|w\|$ gives the geometric margin (actual Euclidean distance to the boundary), which is invariant to that rescaling. The canonical constraint $y_i f(x_i)\ge1$ removes the scale degree of freedom.

**Rubric:**
- Subtract the two canonical plane equations → $w^\top(x_+-x_-)=2$
- Project onto $w/\|w\|$ → $2/\|w\|$
- Max margin ⇔ min $\tfrac12\|w\|^2$
- Functional margin is scale-dependent; geometric (÷‖w‖) is the real distance

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q2.** A student says "we can kernelize the primal SVM directly — just replace $x$ with $\phi(x)$." Explain precisely why the **dual** is what makes kernelization possible, and what $w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i x_i$ implies when $\phi$ is infinite-dimensional.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Why the dual.** The dual objective and the decision function depend on the data *only through inner products* $\langle x_i, x_j\rangle$:

$$\max_\alpha \sum_i\alpha_i - \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j y_iy_j\langle x_i,x_j\rangle, \qquad f(x)=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\langle x_i,x\rangle + b$$

Every place the data appears is an inner product, so you can substitute $K(x_i,x_j)=\langle\phi(x_i),\phi(x_j)\rangle$ **without ever computing $\phi$**. The primal, by contrast, contains $w$ explicitly and $w^\top\phi(x)$ — to kernelize it you'd need to represent $w$ in feature space.

**The infinite-dimensional point.** The optimal weight is $w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\phi(x_i)$ (representer theorem). For an RBF kernel $\phi$ is infinite-dimensional, so $w$ is an infinite-dimensional object — you *cannot* store it as a coordinate vector. But you never need to: predictions are $f(x)=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i K(x_i,x)+b$, a finite sum over support vectors. The dual reduces an infinite-dimensional optimization to one over $n$ coefficients $\alpha_i$.

**Rubric:**
- Dual touches data only via inner products → can swap in $K$
- Primal has $w$/$w^\top\phi(x)$ explicitly → can't kernelize without representing $w$
- $w=\sum\alpha_i y_i\phi(x_i)$ is infinite-dim for RBF → can't materialize
- Predictions are a finite sum over SVs; dual = $n$ coefficients

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q3.** For a soft-margin SVM, classify a training point by its dual coefficient: (a) $\alpha_i = 0$, (b) $0 < \alpha_i < C$, (c) $\alpha_i = C$. Give each point's geometric position and which points are used to solve for $b$.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

By the KKT conditions (complementary slackness) of the soft-margin SVM:

- **(a) $\alpha_i = 0$** → $y_i f(x_i) \ge 1$: the point is **outside or on the correct side of the margin**, correctly classified with room to spare. It is **not a support vector** and has zero influence on $w$.
- **(b) $0 < \alpha_i < C$** → $y_i f(x_i) = 1$ and $\xi_i = 0$: the point lies **exactly on the margin boundary**. This is a **free (margin) support vector**. These are the points used to **solve for $b$** (from $y_i f(x_i) = 1$).
- **(c) $\alpha_i = C$** → $y_i f(x_i) \le 1$, $\xi_i \ge 0$: the point is **inside the margin or misclassified**. This is a **bounded support vector**.

Only (b) and (c) — the support vectors — appear in $w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i x_i$, which is why the solution is sparse.

**Rubric:**
- $\alpha=0$: outside margin, correct, not an SV
- $0<\alpha<C$: on the margin (free SV), used for $b$
- $\alpha=C$: inside margin / misclassified (bounded SV)
- Only SVs contribute to $w$ → sparsity

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q4.** Hinge loss and logistic loss are both convex, calibrated classification surrogates. Explain precisely why minimizing hinge loss produces a *sparse* set of support vectors while logistic regression uses every training point.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

Write both as functions of the margin $m = y f(x)$:

- **Hinge:** $\ell(m) = \max(0,\, 1 - m)$. For $m \ge 1$ (correctly classified beyond the margin), $\ell(m) = 0$ and $\ell'(m) = 0$.
- **Logistic:** $\ell(m) = \log(1 + e^{-m})$. This is *strictly positive and strictly decreasing for all finite $m$*; its derivative is $\ell'(m) = -\sigma(-m) < 0$ everywhere, where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid, so the slope never reaches zero.

**Consequence.** In a (sub)gradient method, a point's contribution to the gradient of $w$ is proportional to $\ell'(m_i)\, y_i x_i$. Under hinge loss, every point with $m_i > 1$ has $\ell'(m_i) = 0$ — it contributes **nothing**, so it is not a support vector and could be deleted without changing the solution. Only points with $m_i \le 1$ (on or inside the margin, or misclassified) have nonzero gradient. Under logistic loss, $\ell'(m_i) \ne 0$ for *every* point, so all points contribute (those far on the correct side contribute a tiny but nonzero amount) — hence no sparsity, but you get smooth, probabilistic outputs in exchange.

**The trade-off.** Hinge's flat zero region buys sparsity (memory/speed: store only SVs) at the cost of probabilities; logistic's everywhere-positive loss buys calibrated probabilities at the cost of density.

**Rubric:**
- Hinge is exactly 0 (and gradient 0) for $m\ge1$
- Logistic is strictly positive / nonzero-gradient for all finite $m$
- Zero gradient ⇒ point drops out of $w$ ⇒ not an SV ⇒ sparsity
- Trade-off: sparsity vs probabilities

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q5.** State the bias-variance direction of increasing $C$ and increasing the RBF $\gamma$ *separately*. Then: a model has ~100% training accuracy, near-chance test accuracy, and almost every training point is a support vector. Which knob(s), and which direction, do you turn?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**$C$.** $C$ penalizes margin violations. **Large $C$** → the model refuses to tolerate slack → harder margin, fits training data tightly → *less* regularization → **lower bias, higher variance** (overfit). Small $C$ → wider soft margin, more violations allowed → more regularization → higher bias, lower variance.

**$\gamma$ (RBF).** $K(x,x')=\exp(-\gamma\|x-x'\|^2)$. **Large $\gamma$** → small kernel width → each point's influence is very local → a wiggly boundary that can wrap individual points → **high variance** (overfit); in the limit each support vector only matches itself. Small $\gamma$ → wide, smooth bumps → can underfit.

**Diagnosis.** Perfect train accuracy + chance test accuracy + nearly all points as SVs is the classic **overfitting** signature for RBF SVM — the boundary has become extremely local. **Decrease $\gamma$** (smooth the kernel) and/or **decrease $C$** (allow a softer margin). Re-tune jointly by cross-validation; $\gamma$ is usually the dominant culprit when "almost every point is an SV."

**Rubric:**
- Large $C$ = less regularization, higher variance (overfit)
- Large $\gamma$ = more local/wiggly, higher variance (overfit)
- Diagnose the symptoms as overfitting
- Fix: lower $\gamma$ and/or $C$; re-tune by CV

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q6.** Prove that the Gaussian (RBF) kernel corresponds to an infinite-dimensional feature map. What goes wrong if someone tries to "just compute $\phi(x)$" and run a plain linear model on it?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Proof.** Write $K(x,x') = \exp(-\gamma\|x-x'\|^2) = \exp(-\gamma\|x\|^2)\exp(-\gamma\|x'\|^2)\exp(2\gamma\, x^\top x')$. Expand the cross term as a power series:

$$\exp(2\gamma\, x^\top x') = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(2\gamma)^k (x^\top x')^k}{k!}$$

Each term $(x^\top x')^k$ is itself an inner product of the degree-$k$ monomial features of $x$ and $x'$ (the $k$-fold tensor power). Summing over all $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ means the feature map $\phi$ contains monomials of *every* degree — infinitely many coordinates:

$$\phi_k(x) \propto e^{-\gamma\|x\|^2}\,\frac{(2\gamma)^{k/2}}{\sqrt{k!}}\, x^{\otimes k}, \quad k = 0,1,2,\dots$$

Here $x^{\otimes k}$ is the $k$-fold tensor power of $x$: the block of coordinates holding every degree-$k$ monomial in the entries of $x$. Stacking one such block for every $k$ gives a vector with no last entry, so $\phi$ is infinite-dimensional.

**Why you can't materialize it.** There is no finite feature vector $\phi(x)$ to compute or store — it has infinitely many entries. The kernel trick's value is exactly that you evaluate the scalar $K(x,x')$ in $O(d)$ time and *never* touch $\phi$. A "plain linear model on $\phi(x)$" is impossible to even instantiate. The practical price for staying in the dual is the $n\times n$ Gram matrix and $O(n^2)$–$O(n^3)$ training — which is why kernel SVMs don't scale to large $n$. (If you want an *approximate* finite $\phi$, that's what Nyström / random Fourier features provide.)

**Rubric:**
- Factor $K$ and power-series-expand $\exp(2\gamma x^\top x')$
- Identify monomials of all degrees → infinitely many feature coordinates
- Conclude no finite $\phi$ exists → can't materialize
- Note the dual's cost instead ($n\times n$ Gram, $O(n^2)$–$O(n^3)$); RFF/Nyström as the approximate escape

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q7.** State Mercer's condition for a valid kernel. Is $K(x,x') = (x^\top x' + 1)^3$ a valid kernel? Is $K(x,x') = \tanh(x^\top x')$? Justify.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Mercer's condition.** $K$ is a valid (Mercer) kernel iff it is **symmetric** and its **Gram matrix** $G_{ij} = K(x_i, x_j)$ is **positive semidefinite (PSD)** for every finite set of points. Equivalently, $K$ then corresponds to an inner product $\langle\phi(x),\phi(x')\rangle$ in some Hilbert space.

**$(x^\top x' + 1)^3$ — valid.** It's the polynomial kernel (degree 3, with bias). Build it from closure properties: $x^\top x'$ is a valid kernel (linear); adding the constant $1$ (a valid kernel) preserves validity; and **products and positive integer powers of kernels are kernels** (the Schur product theorem: the elementwise product of PSD matrices is PSD). So $(x^\top x'+1)^3$ is PSD — valid. Its feature map is the finite set of all monomials up to degree 3.

**$\tanh(x^\top x')$ — not valid in general.** The sigmoid/tanh "kernel" is **not PSD for all parameter values / all data** — one can construct point sets whose Gram matrix has a negative eigenvalue. It's used heuristically in some SVM packages (it "works" sometimes), but it does *not* satisfy Mercer's condition in general, so there's no guaranteed underlying inner-product space and the QP can become non-convex/ill-posed.

**Rubric:**
- Mercer: symmetric + PSD Gram matrix for all finite samples ⇔ inner product in a Hilbert space
- Poly kernel valid via closure (sum/product/power of kernels are kernels; Schur product)
- tanh kernel not PSD in general → not a valid Mercer kernel (heuristic only)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q8.** State the Cover–Hart bound for asymptotic 1-nearest-neighbor error in terms of the Bayes error $R^\star$ (binary case). Interpret "$R \le 2R^\star$." How does using $k \to \infty$ with $k/n \to 0$ change the conclusion?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Cover–Hart (1967).** As $n \to \infty$, the binary 1-NN error is squeezed between the Bayes error and twice it:

$$R^\star \le R \le 2R^\star(1 - R^\star) \le 2R^\star$$

(The general $M$-class form carries an extra $M/(M-1)$ factor; for the exact statement, ask an AI assistant — an interviewer wants the $2R^\star$ takeaway, not the constant.)

**Interpretation of $R \le 2R^\star$.** Even the crudest possible classifier — "copy the label of the single closest point" — has asymptotic error no worse than **twice** the best achievable error of *any* classifier (the Bayes classifier, which knows the true $P(y\mid x)$). Put differently, at least **half the achievable information about the label is contained in the single nearest neighbor**. That's a remarkable guarantee for a method that does no training at all.

**$k \to \infty,\ k/n \to 0$.** With $k$ growing (so the vote averages over many neighbors → variance $\to 0$) but slowly enough that the neighborhood still shrinks to the query point ($k/n \to 0$ → bias $\to 0$), kNN becomes **Bayes-consistent**: $R \to R^\star$. So 1-NN pays a factor-of-2 price for using only one neighbor; letting $k$ grow appropriately recovers the optimal error in the limit.

**Rubric:**
- State the bound (general $M$ and binary $R\le 2R^\star(1-R^\star)\le 2R^\star$)
- Interpret: 1-NN ≤ 2× Bayes; half the info is in the nearest neighbor
- $k\to\infty$, $k/n\to0$ ⇒ variance→0 and bias→0 ⇒ Bayes-consistent ($R\to R^\star$)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q9.** Explain the curse of dimensionality for kNN as a *probability statement*, not a vibe. State the Beyer et al. (1999) condition and conclusion, give the $1/\sqrt{d}$ argument for why distances concentrate, and explain why this drives modern retrieval toward learned embeddings + approximate NN.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The probability statement (Beyer et al. 1999).** Let $\text{Dmin}_d$ and $\text{Dmax}_d$ be the nearest and farthest distances from a query to $n$ points in dimension $d$. **If** the relative variance of distance vanishes,

$$\lim_{d\to\infty}\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{D_d}{\mathbb{E}[D_d]}\right) = 0 \qquad (D_d = \text{query-to-point distance in dim } d)$$

(true for i.i.d. features and much more broadly), **then** for every $\varepsilon > 0$,

$$\Pr\bigl[\text{Dmax}_d \le (1+\varepsilon)\,\text{Dmin}_d\bigr] \longrightarrow 1.$$

The nearest and farthest points become indistinguishable — the "relative contrast" $(\text{Dmax}-\text{Dmin})/\text{Dmin} \to 0$ in probability.

**Why $1/\sqrt{d}$.** For independent coordinates, $\|x - x'\|^2 = \sum_{j=1}^d (x_j - x_j')^2$ is a sum of $d$ i.i.d. terms. By the law of large numbers its mean grows $\propto d$ while its standard deviation grows $\propto \sqrt{d}$, so the **coefficient of variation** (std/mean) shrinks like $\sqrt{d}/d = 1/\sqrt{d} \to 0$. All pairwise distances bunch around the same value.

**Why retrieval moved to embeddings + ANN.** Raw high-dimensional kNN is meaningless (every point is ~equidistant) and exact search is $O(nd)$ with tree indices collapsing to $O(n)$ past ~20 dimensions. So modern systems (1) **learn low-dimensional embeddings** where semantic similarity gives genuine distance contrast (low *intrinsic* dimension), and (2) use **approximate nearest-neighbor** indices (HNSW, FAISS, ScaNN) for sub-linear, high-recall search. That's the backbone of semantic search and RAG.

**Rubric:**
- State Beyer condition (relative variance → 0) and conclusion ($\Pr[\text{Dmax}\le(1+\varepsilon)\text{Dmin}]\to1$)
- $1/\sqrt d$ coefficient-of-variation argument (mean $\propto d$, std $\propto\sqrt d$)
- Consequence: nearest ≈ farthest, distance meaningless
- Drives learned embeddings (low intrinsic dim) + ANN (HNSW/FAISS) for retrieval/RAG

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q10.** A kNN model uses two features: annual income (range 0 to 500,000 USD) and a normalized risk score (range 0–1), with raw Euclidean distance. Explain why the model effectively ignores the risk score, and give two distinct fixes naming the metric each implies.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The problem.** Euclidean distance is $\sqrt{(\Delta\text{income})^2 + (\Delta\text{score})^2}$. Income differences are on the order of $10^4$–$10^5$; score differences are at most $1$. So $(\Delta\text{income})^2$ dominates the sum by ~$10^8$–$10^{10}$, and $(\Delta\text{score})^2$ is numerically negligible. The "nearest neighbors" are determined almost entirely by income; the risk score has essentially zero influence on the distance, so the model ignores it regardless of how predictive it is.

**Fix 1 — standardize, then Euclidean.** Z-score each feature ($x \mapsto (x-\mu)/\sigma$) or min-max scale to $[0,1]$, so both features have comparable spread. Distance is then Euclidean **on the standardized features**, and the score contributes on equal footing.

**Fix 2 — Mahalanobis distance.** Use $d(x,x') = \sqrt{(x-x')^\top \Sigma^{-1}(x-x')}$. The $\Sigma^{-1}$ rescales each direction by its variance (and rotates out correlations), so it bakes the standardization (and decorrelation) directly into the metric — no separate scaling step. (For magnitude-invariant data like text/embeddings, **cosine distance** is a third option.)

**Rubric:**
- Identify scale dominance: $(\Delta\text{income})^2 \gg (\Delta\text{score})^2$ → distance ≈ income only
- Fix 1: standardize/min-max → Euclidean on scaled features
- Fix 2: Mahalanobis ($\Sigma^{-1}$) bakes in scaling/decorrelation
- (Bonus) cosine for magnitude-invariant data

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q11.** Express kNN regression as a local-averaging estimator and relate it to Nadaraya–Watson kernel regression. What plays the role of "bandwidth" in kNN, and how does $k$ trade bias against variance in the regression risk?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**kNN as local averaging.** kNN regression predicts $\hat f(x) = \frac1k \sum_{i\in N_k(x)} y_i$, the average target over the $k$ nearest neighbors $N_k(x)$. This is a weighted average of all training targets with weights that are $1/k$ on the $k$ nearest and $0$ elsewhere.

**Relation to Nadaraya–Watson.** N–W is $\hat f(x) = \frac{\sum_i K_h(x,x_i)\,y_i}{\sum_i K_h(x,x_i)}$, a kernel-weighted average with a *fixed* bandwidth $h$. kNN is the special case using a **uniform kernel over the $k$-NN ball** with an **adaptive bandwidth**: instead of a fixed radius $h$, the radius is whatever distance contains exactly $k$ neighbors. So both estimate $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x]$ by averaging nearby points; kNN's bandwidth adapts to local density (wider in sparse regions, narrower in dense ones), N–W's is fixed. kNN's estimate is a step function; a Gaussian-kernel N–W estimate is smooth.

**$k$ as bandwidth and the bias-variance trade.** $k$ is the (inverse) bandwidth knob. The regression risk decomposes as irreducible noise + bias$^2$ + variance. The variance of the average of $k$ neighbors is $\approx \sigma^2/k$ — **decreasing in $k$**. The bias **increases with $k$**: a larger neighborhood reaches farther from $x$, averaging in points whose true mean differs, so $\hat f$ is smoothed away from $f(x)$. Small $k$ → low bias, high variance ($k=1$ interpolates the data); large $k$ → high bias, low variance ($k=n$ predicts the global mean). Optimal $k$ balances the two and grows with $n$.

**Rubric:**
- kNN regression = $\frac1k\sum_{i\in N_k}y_i$, a local average
- Relate to Nadaraya–Watson: kNN = uniform kernel + *adaptive* bandwidth vs N–W fixed $h$
- Bandwidth role played by $k$ (radius to $k$-th neighbor)
- Variance $\propto \sigma^2/k$ (↓ with $k$), bias ↑ with $k$; optimal $k$ balances, grows with $n$

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q12.** A quant has 5 million rows of high-dimensional, sparse features. Explain precisely why `SVC(kernel='rbf')` is the wrong choice (cite the computational reason), and name scalable alternatives that preserve a margin/linear-SVM flavor — including one kernel-approximation route. Bonus: what does sklearn's `gamma='scale'` compute, and why does `LinearSVC` differ from `SVC(kernel='linear')`?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Why not `SVC(kernel='rbf')`.** Kernel SVM training (libsvm) scales **$O(n^2)$ to $O(n^3)$** in the number of samples — it forms/operates on the $n \times n$ Gram matrix. sklearn's own docs warn the fit time "scales at least quadratically … impractical beyond tens of thousands of samples." At $n = 5\text{M}$ this is completely infeasible (memory for the Gram matrix alone is $\sim n^2$). The model also stores all support vectors, which can be a large fraction of 5M.

**Scalable alternatives.**
- **`LinearSVC`** (liblinear) or **`SGDClassifier(loss='hinge')`** — solve the *linear* SVM (hinge + L2) directly in the input space, scaling roughly linearly in $n$. Ideal here because high-dimensional sparse data (e.g. text-like features) is usually linearly separable enough that a linear margin is competitive.
- **Kernel approximation:** if you genuinely need RBF-like nonlinearity, map features through **Nyström** or **random Fourier features** (`sklearn.kernel_approximation.RBFSampler`) to an explicit *finite* approximate feature space, then fit a linear SVM / SGD on those — recovering most of the RBF benefit at linear cost.

**Bonus — `gamma='scale'`.** It sets $\gamma = \dfrac{1}{n_{\text{features}} \cdot \operatorname{Var}(X)}$ — concretely `1 / (n_features * X.var())`, where `X.var()` is the variance over *all* entries of the training matrix (so it implicitly assumes roughly comparable feature scales — standardize anyway). It's the default since sklearn 0.22; the older `gamma='auto'` is $1/n_{\text{features}}$. `'scale'` adapts to feature spread, making it more robust to unscaled data (though you should still standardize).

**Bonus — `LinearSVC` vs `SVC(kernel='linear')`.** They optimize *different* objectives: `LinearSVC` (liblinear) defaults to **squared hinge** loss and regularizes the intercept differently, while `SVC(kernel='linear')` (libsvm) uses plain hinge via the dual. `LinearSVC` scales far better to large $n$; `SVC` returns exact support vectors and supports `predict_proba` only if `probability=True` (off by default; Platt scaling fit via internal cross-validation, which can disagree with `decision_function`). So they give different coefficients on the same data.

**Rubric:**
- Kernel SVM is $O(n^2)$–$O(n^3)$ (Gram matrix); infeasible at 5M (cite the scaling)
- `LinearSVC` / `SGDClassifier(hinge)` for the linear margin at scale
- Kernel approximation (Nyström / random Fourier features) for approximate RBF
- Bonus: `gamma='scale'` $= 1/(n_\text{features}\cdot\operatorname{Var}(X))$ vs `'auto'` $=1/n_\text{features}$
- Bonus: `LinearSVC` (squared hinge, liblinear) ≠ `SVC(linear)` (hinge, libsvm)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q1.** Prove that Bernoulli Naive Bayes has a linear decision boundary. Derive the weight $w_j$ and bias $b$ explicitly.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

For binary $x_j\in\{0,1\}$ let $\theta_{cj}=p(x_j{=}1\mid y{=}c)$. The per-feature log-likelihood is

$$\log p(x_j\mid c)=x_j\log\theta_{cj}+(1-x_j)\log(1-\theta_{cj})=x_j\log\tfrac{\theta_{cj}}{1-\theta_{cj}}+\log(1-\theta_{cj})$$

— **affine in $x_j$**. The two-class log-odds is the sum over $j$ of the $c{=}1$ minus $c{=}0$ versions, plus the prior log-odds:

$$\log\frac{p(1\mid x)}{p(0\mid x)} = \underbrace{\log\frac{p(1)}{p(0)} + \sum_j \log\frac{1-\theta_{1j}}{1-\theta_{0j}}}_{b} + \sum_j x_j \underbrace{\log\frac{\theta_{1j}(1-\theta_{0j})}{\theta_{0j}(1-\theta_{1j})}}_{w_j} = w^\top x + b$$

Since the log-odds is affine in $x$, the boundary $\{x: w^\top x + b = 0\}$ is a **hyperplane** — NB is a linear classifier, of the same log-linear form as logistic regression. The difference is only in how $w,b$ are obtained: NB plugs in $\theta_{cj}$ from counts; logistic fits $w,b$ by maximizing the conditional likelihood.

**Rubric:**
- $\log p(x_j\mid c)$ is affine in $x_j$ (show the slope/intercept split)
- Sum over $j$, subtract classes → $w^\top x + b$
- State $w_j=\log\frac{\theta_{1j}(1-\theta_{0j})}{\theta_{0j}(1-\theta_{1j})}$ and the prior+correction bias $b$
- Conclude linear boundary; note same form as logistic, different fit

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q2.** Show that Gaussian Naive Bayes with variance shared across classes reduces to logistic regression in functional form. What happens to the boundary if the per-class variances are *not* shared?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Shared variance.** With $p(x_j\mid c)=\mathcal{N}(x_j;\mu_{cj},\sigma_j^2)$ (note $\sigma_j^2$ does *not* depend on $c$), the log-odds is

$$\log\frac{p(1\mid x)}{p(0\mid x)} = \log\frac{p(1)}{p(0)} + \sum_j\left[-\frac{(x_j-\mu_{1j})^2}{2\sigma_j^2} + \frac{(x_j-\mu_{0j})^2}{2\sigma_j^2}\right]$$

Expand the squares: the $x_j^2$ terms are $-\frac{x_j^2}{2\sigma_j^2}+\frac{x_j^2}{2\sigma_j^2}=0$ — **they cancel because the variance is shared.** What remains is linear in $x_j$:

$$= b + \sum_j \frac{\mu_{1j}-\mu_{0j}}{\sigma_j^2}\,x_j = w^\top x + b \;\Rightarrow\; p(1\mid x) = \sigma(w^\top x + b)$$

— exactly the logistic/sigmoid form, with $w_j=(\mu_{1j}-\mu_{0j})/\sigma_j^2$. So GNB (equal-variance) and logistic regression assume the **same** $p(y\mid x)$ family; they differ only in fitting (GNB plugs in class moments; logistic maximizes conditional likelihood).

**Unshared variance.** If $\sigma_{1j}^2\neq\sigma_{0j}^2$, the $x_j^2$ terms **do not cancel**, leaving a quadratic-in-$x$ log-odds → a **quadratic decision boundary**. That's **QDA** (quadratic discriminant analysis), no longer logistic-equivalent.

**Rubric:**
- Write both log-Gaussians; expand squares
- Show $x_j^2$ terms cancel *only* under shared variance → linear → sigmoid
- $w_j=(\mu_{1j}-\mu_{0j})/\sigma_j^2$
- Unshared variance → quadratic terms survive → QDA

</details>

**Q3.** Show that Laplace/Lidstone smoothing is MAP estimation of a multinomial under a Dirichlet prior. Derive the smoothed estimate as the posterior mode.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Setup.** A multinomial over $d$ outcomes with counts $N_1,\dots,N_d$ ($N=\sum_j N_j$) has likelihood $\propto \prod_j \theta_j^{N_j}$. Put a symmetric **Dirichlet$(\beta,\dots,\beta)$** prior, $p(\theta)\propto\prod_j \theta_j^{\beta-1}$, where the single concentration parameter $\beta>0$ is shared by all $d$ outcomes.

**Posterior.** Conjugacy gives $p(\theta\mid N)\propto \prod_j \theta_j^{N_j+\beta-1}$ — a **Dirichlet$(N_j+\beta)$**.

**MAP = posterior mode.** Maximize $\sum_j (N_j+\beta-1)\log\theta_j$ subject to $\sum_j\theta_j=1$ (Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$): $\frac{N_j+\beta-1}{\theta_j}=\lambda \Rightarrow \theta_j\propto N_j+\beta-1$. Normalizing,

$$\hat\theta_j^{\text{MAP}} = \frac{N_j+\beta-1}{N+d(\beta-1)}$$

Set $\alpha=\beta-1$: $\hat\theta_j=\frac{N_j+\alpha}{N+\alpha d}$ — exactly Laplace ($\alpha=1$, i.e. a Dirichlet$(2)$ prior) / Lidstone ($0<\alpha<1$) smoothing. (Plain **MLE** is the $\alpha=0$ / flat-prior case, the raw frequencies.)

**The point.** Smoothing isn't a hack — it's the **MAP estimate under a Dirichlet prior**, the same loss=−log-likelihood, penalty=−log-prior structure as ridge/lasso in c1.3 (Gaussian/Laplace priors on coefficients). It shrinks empirical frequencies toward uniform — variance reduction on rare events (James–Stein logic).

**Rubric:**
- Multinomial likelihood × Dirichlet prior → Dirichlet posterior (conjugacy)
- Maximize log-posterior with the simplex constraint (Lagrange) → mode $\propto N_j+\beta-1$
- Identify $\alpha=\beta-1$ → recovers $\frac{N_j+\alpha}{N+\alpha d}$
- Connect: MLE = raw counts; MAP = smoothed; the c1.3 prior/shrinkage thread

</details>

**Q4.** A spam classifier sees a legitimate email containing one word never seen in the "ham" training set. Show that *unsmoothed* Naive Bayes assigns $p(\text{ham}\mid x)=0$ regardless of how ham-like every other word is. Explain in log-space and show how $\alpha=1$ fixes it.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The veto.** Unsmoothed MLE sets $\hat\theta_{\text{ham},j}=N_{\text{ham},j}/N_{\text{ham}}=0$ for the unseen word $j$. The class score is a product:

$$p(\text{ham})\prod_k p(x_k\mid\text{ham}) = p(\text{ham})\cdot \underbrace{0}_{\text{word }j}\cdot \prod_{k\neq j}p(x_k\mid\text{ham}) = 0$$

A single zero factor annihilates the entire product, no matter how strongly the other 500 words point to ham. After normalization $p(\text{ham}\mid x)=0$, so the email is classified spam on the strength of one unseen word.

**In log-space** (where NB is actually computed): $\log p(x_j\mid\text{ham})=\log 0=-\infty$, and $\sum_k \log p(x_k\mid\text{ham}) = -\infty$ — an **absorbing state** no finite evidence escapes.

**The fix.** Laplace smoothing $\hat\theta_{cj}=\frac{N_{cj}+1}{N_c+d}$ (or Lidstone with $0<\alpha<1$) ensures every probability is strictly positive, so $\log p(x_j\mid\text{ham})$ is finite and the unseen word contributes a small (not infinite) penalty. The class decision is then driven by the *accumulated* evidence rather than vetoed by one zero.

**Rubric:**
- Unsmoothed MLE gives $\hat\theta=0$ for the unseen feature
- One zero factor → whole product 0 → $p(\text{ham}\mid x)=0$
- Log-space: $\log 0=-\infty$, absorbing
- $\alpha>0$ smoothing makes every term finite/positive → no veto

</details>

**Q5.** You accidentally duplicate a feature ($x_2 := x_1$). Show what happens to NB's log-odds contribution from that feature, to the true evidence, and to the predicted probability. Does the classification change? Connect to calibration (ECE/Brier).

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Log-odds doubles.** NB treats $x_1$ and $x_2$ as conditionally independent, so each contributes its own term $w_1 x_1$ and $w_2 x_2 = w_1 x_1$ (identical, since $x_2=x_1$). The feature's contribution to the log-odds is therefore **counted twice** — $2 w_1 x_1$ instead of $w_1 x_1$.

**True evidence is unchanged.** A duplicated feature carries no new information; the correct log-likelihood-ratio contribution is $w_1 x_1$ (once). NB's independence assumption is violated (the two features are perfectly dependent), and the error is exactly this double-counting.

**Probability is pushed toward 0/1.** Doubling the log-odds pushes the posterior $\sigma(\text{log-odds})$ further from $\tfrac12$ — toward 0 or 1. The model becomes **overconfident**. Concretely, if the true posterior is $0.7$ (log-odds $\approx 0.847$), double-counting reports $\sigma(1.69)\approx 0.84$.

**Classification usually unchanged.** The argmax depends only on the *sign* of the log-odds, and doubling a nonzero number preserves its sign. So accuracy / AUC are unaffected — the prediction is still "class 1." Only the *probability* is wrong.

**Calibration link.** This is Domingos–Pazzani in miniature: correlated features degrade the *probabilities* (worse ECE / Brier — c1.2), not the *ranking/argmax* (fine AUC). It's why NB has good AUC and bad calibration, and why you must calibrate (Platt/isotonic) before using NB probabilities for expected-value decisions.

**Rubric:**
- Duplicated feature → its log-odds term counted twice (independence assumption violated)
- True evidence unchanged (no new information)
- Posterior pushed toward 0/1 → overconfident
- Argmax (sign) preserved → accuracy/AUC unaffected
- Tie to Domingos–Pazzani / calibration (ECE/Brier degrade, ranking doesn't)

</details>

**Q6.** State the Ng–Jordan result on Naive Bayes vs. logistic regression: their sample complexities in the number of features $n$, which has the lower asymptotic error, and the two-regime consequence. Why does each scaling hold?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Sample complexity.** Write $m$ for the number of training examples and fix any tolerance $\varepsilon>0$. To reach within $\varepsilon$ of its own asymptotic (infinite-data) error over $n$ features:
- **Naive Bayes (generative):** needs $m = O(\log n)$ examples. Its parameters are $O(n)$ *independent univariate* estimates ($p(x_j\mid y)$ per feature), each converging at $O(1/\sqrt m)$; a union bound over $n$ of them gives error $\lesssim \sqrt{(\log n)/m}$.
- **Logistic regression (discriminative):** needs $m = O(n)$ examples — it fits the joint weight vector by conditional likelihood, a problem of VC-dimension $\Theta(n)$, so uniform convergence requires $m=\Omega(n)$.

**Asymptotic error.** Logistic regression has the **lower** asymptotic error: it optimizes $p(y\mid x)$ directly, so it isn't biased by NB's (usually false) conditional-independence assumption. NB's independence assumption biases its asymptote upward.

**Two regimes (the consequence).** With **few** examples, NB reaches its (worse) plateau quickly and **wins**; with **many** examples, logistic regression converges and **overtakes**, ending lower. The crossover is real (Ng–Jordan, ~15 UCI datasets). Takeaway: generative models aren't obsolete — they're the right choice when labels are scarce relative to dimension (the high-bias/low-variance estimator wins in the small-data regime). This is bias-variance at the *estimator-class* level.

**Rubric:**
- NB $O(\log n)$ vs logistic $O(n)$ sample complexity
- Why: NB = $n$ independent 1-D estimates (union bound); logistic = $\Theta(n)$ VC-dim joint fit
- Logistic has lower asymptotic error (fits $p(y\mid x)$ directly; NB biased by independence)
- Two regimes: NB wins small-data, logistic wins large-data; crossover

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q7.** Construct (a) a distribution where Naive Bayes classifies with 0% error but its estimated posteriors are off by an arbitrary amount, and (b) a distribution where the independence violation actually flips the argmax and NB misclassifies.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**(a) Perfect classification, arbitrarily wrong probabilities.** Take two strongly correlated (even identical) features that both genuinely predict the class. Class 1: $x_1=x_2=1$ w.p. ~1; Class 0: $x_1=x_2=0$ w.p. ~1. NB double-counts the (identical) evidence, so for a class-1 point it might report $p(1\mid x)=0.999$ when the true posterior is, say, $0.7$. But the *argmax* is correct on every point → **0% classification error with grossly miscalibrated probabilities.** By adding more duplicated features you push the reported probability arbitrarily close to 1 while the truth stays at 0.7 — the error is unbounded. (This is the constructive version of Domingos–Pazzani: the 0-1-optimality region is vastly larger than the probability-optimality region.)

**(b) Independence violation flips the argmax.** Make the features informative *jointly* but misleading *marginally*, e.g. an XOR-like structure: class 1 iff $x_1 \neq x_2$. Marginally, each $x_j$ is independent of $y$ ($p(x_j{=}1\mid y)=0.5$ for both classes), so NB learns $w_j\approx 0$ and predicts by the prior alone — **it cannot represent XOR** (a linear classifier can't), so it misclassifies ~half the points even though the classes are perfectly separable jointly. More generally, when correlated features' double-counting pushes the (wrong) class's biased posterior across $\tfrac12$, the argmax flips.

**Rubric:**
- (a) correlated/duplicated predictive features → double-counting → arbitrarily overconfident but correct argmax → 0% error, terrible probabilities
- Note this is the Domingos–Pazzani optimality-region gap
- (b) XOR / jointly-informative-marginally-uninformative → NB (linear) can't represent it → misclassifies
- Distinguish: (a) ranking fine/probabilities wrong vs (b) argmax actually flips

</details>

**Q8.** Logistic regression and equal-variance Gaussian NB have the same $p(y\mid x)$ functional form, yet they fit different $(w,b)$ on the same finite data. Explain exactly why, what each optimizes, and predict which wins with (a) 20 examples, (b) 20,000 examples.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Same form, different objective.** Both assume $p(1\mid x)=\sigma(w^\top x+b)$ (shown in Q2 of this Naive Bayes set). But:
- **GNB maximizes the joint likelihood** $\sum_i \log p(x_i,y_i)$ — equivalently it estimates class priors and per-feature class-conditional moments ($\mu_{cj},\sigma_j^2$) and *plugs them in* to get $w,b$. It spends modeling effort on $p(x)$.
- **Logistic regression maximizes the conditional likelihood** $\sum_i \log p(y_i\mid x_i)$ directly — it fits $w,b$ to the boundary and never models $p(x)$.

On finite data these give different $(w,b)$ because GNB's plug-in estimates are only optimal if the Gaussian/equal-variance/independence assumptions hold exactly; when they don't (the usual case), the direct conditional fit is better at the actual task. (If the generative assumptions were exactly true, both would converge to the same answer.)

**Predictions (Ng–Jordan).**
- **(a) 20 examples:** **GNB wins.** Its $O(\log n)$ sample complexity means it reaches its plateau fast; logistic hasn't had enough data to fit its $O(n)$ parameters and is high-variance.
- **(b) 20,000 examples:** **logistic wins.** It has converged to its lower asymptotic error; GNB has plateaued at its higher (independence-biased) asymptote.

**Rubric:**
- Same $p(y\mid x)$ family (from Q2 of this Naive Bayes set), different *objective* (joint vs conditional likelihood)
- GNB plugs in moments (models $p(x)$); logistic fits the boundary directly
- Finite-data divergence because generative assumptions don't hold exactly
- 20 → GNB (fast convergence); 20,000 → logistic (lower asymptote)

</details>

**Q9.** Derive the decision rules for Bernoulli NB and Multinomial NB on a bag-of-words document, and show that Bernoulli NB *penalizes word absence* while Multinomial NB ignores absent words. Give a concrete document where they predict differently.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Bernoulli NB** ($x_j\in\{0,1\}$ = presence of word $j$ in the vocabulary): the likelihood explicitly includes *both* presence and absence,

$$\log p(x\mid c)=\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\bigl[x_j\log\theta_{cj}+(1-x_j)\log(1-\theta_{cj})\bigr]$$

The $(1-x_j)\log(1-\theta_{cj})$ term means **every word in the vocabulary that is *absent* from the document contributes evidence** (the $\log(1-\theta_{cj})$ term).

**Multinomial NB** ($x_j$ = count of word $j$): the score sums only over words that *appear*,

$$\log p(x\mid c)=\log p(c)+\sum_{j}x_j\log\theta_{cj}$$

Absent words have $x_j=0$, contributing $0$ — **Multinomial NB never penalizes absence**, and it weights present words by their counts.

**Where they disagree.** Consider a *short* document containing only the word "free" (count 1) over a vocabulary that also contains, say, "meeting" (a strong ham indicator). Multinomial NB sees only "free" → leans spam. Bernoulli NB additionally adds, for "meeting," the absence term $\log(1-\theta_{\text{ham},\text{meeting}})$ vs $\log(1-\theta_{\text{spam},\text{meeting}})$ — the *absence* of a strong ham word is itself spam-evidence, and the absence of many common ham words can tip Bernoulli NB more strongly toward spam (or, symmetrically, the absence of spammy words toward ham). On documents where absence patterns matter, they predict differently — Bernoulli NB tends to do better on short texts/binary presence, Multinomial on longer count-rich texts.

**Rubric:**
- Bernoulli: likelihood includes $(1-x_j)\log(1-\theta_{cj})$ → absence contributes
- Multinomial: $\sum_j x_j\log\theta_{cj}$ → absent words ($x_j=0$) contribute nothing; counts matter
- Construct a short doc where the absence terms flip the prediction
- Note the use-case split (Bernoulli short/binary, Multinomial long/counts)

</details>

**Q10.** In scikit-learn ≥1.4 you set `MultinomialNB(alpha=0)` expecting unsmoothed MLE, but smoothing still seems applied or you get a warning. Explain the `force_alpha` default and exactly how to get truly-unsmoothed NB — and why you usually shouldn't.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The `force_alpha` change.** Older scikit-learn silently floored any `alpha < 1e-10` up to `1e-10` to avoid numerical issues — so `alpha=0` was quietly turned into `1e-10` (effectively still smoothed). In **scikit-learn 1.4**, `force_alpha` defaulted to **`True`**, which means your `alpha` is used **as given**. So:
- `MultinomialNB(alpha=0, force_alpha=False)` → clips to `1e-10` (a tiny smoothing).
- `MultinomialNB(alpha=0, force_alpha=True)` → **truly unsmoothed** MLE (the default `force_alpha=True` in ≥1.4 means just `alpha=0` now gives unsmoothed).

**Why you usually shouldn't.** Unsmoothed NB hits the zero-count catastrophe (Q4 of this Naive Bayes set): any feature with a zero count in a class makes $\log\theta=-\infty$, vetoing that class on any test document containing that feature. On real vocabularies that's almost guaranteed. Keep `alpha>0` (Laplace `alpha=1` or tune Lidstone $0<\alpha<1$ by CV).

**Rubric:**
- Old behavior: `alpha<1e-10` silently floored to `1e-10`
- `force_alpha=True` (default since 1.4) uses `alpha` as-is
- Truly unsmoothed = `alpha=0` with `force_alpha=True`
- Warn: unsmoothed → zero-count $-\infty$ veto; keep `alpha>0`

</details>

**Q11.** Why is standard Multinomial NB biased on imbalanced text data, and how does Complement NB (Rennie 2003) fix it? What does scikit-learn's `norm=False` default change relative to the paper?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The bias.** Multinomial NB estimates each class's word weights $\log\theta_{cj}$ from *that class's own* documents. When one class has far more training data than another, its parameter estimates are sharper and the decision systematically favors the majority class — and with skewed priors the $\log p(c)$ term compounds it. Minority classes with few documents get noisy, under-weighted parameters.

**Complement NB's fix.** Instead of estimating word weights from class $c$'s documents, CNB estimates them from the **complement** of $c$ (all documents *not* in $c$):

$$\tilde\theta_{cj}=\frac{\alpha+\sum_{i:y_i\neq c}x_{ij}}{\alpha d+\sum_{i:y_i\neq c}\sum_k x_{ik}}$$

Here $x_{ij}$ is the count of word $j$ in training document $i$, $d$ is the vocabulary size, $k$ ranges over that vocabulary, $\alpha$ is the smoothing pseudo-count, and the tilde marks an estimate built from the complement rather than from the class itself. The classifier then picks by the **smallest** complement match: $\hat y=\arg\min_c\sum_j x_j\log\tilde\theta_{cj}$ ("this document looks *least* like everything that isn't $c$"). Because the complement of a minority class is large and balanced, its weight estimates are far more stable — CNB is much more robust on imbalanced text and competitive with linear SVMs.

**`norm=False`.** scikit-learn's `ComplementNB` defaults to `norm=False`, which **omits** the paper's second correction — the L2-normalization of the per-class weight vectors that addresses the magnitude bias from documents of differing lengths. So sklearn's default CNB matches the Weka/Mahout variant, not the full Rennie algorithm; set `norm=True` for the complete weight-normalized version.

**Rubric:**
- MNB estimates weights from each class's own (possibly tiny) data → majority-class bias
- CNB estimates from the *complement* (large, stable) and picks the smallest complement match
- Complement of a minority class is big → robust estimates → better on imbalanced text
- `norm=False` default omits the paper's weight-vector normalization (Weka-style, not full Rennie)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q12.** Your Naive Bayes fraud model has AUC 0.94, but you're told its "probabilities" are useless for an expected-loss threshold (probability times USD amount). Explain why high AUC and bad calibration coexist *for NB specifically*, prove the mechanism, and give the fix.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Why both are true.** AUC measures only the *ranking* of scores — whether positives tend to score above negatives (c1.9: AUC = concordance probability). Calibration measures whether a "0.9" *happens* 90% of the time. They're orthogonal: any monotonic transform of the scores leaves AUC unchanged but can wreck calibration. NB can rank fraud cases well (high AUC) while reporting probabilities that are systematically too extreme.

**The mechanism (prove it).** Real fraud features are correlated (e.g. "amount", "amount relative to history", "merchant risk" all move together). NB assumes conditional independence, so it **multiplies their likelihood ratios as if independent — double-counting (here, multiply-counting) the shared evidence.** Each redundant feature adds its (correlated) term to the log-odds, so the log-odds is inflated in magnitude, and $\sigma(\text{inflated log-odds})$ is pushed toward 0 or 1. The posteriors are therefore **overconfident** — pushed to the extremes — even though their *order* (hence AUC) is fine. This is Domingos–Pazzani: violated independence damages probabilities, not the argmax/ranking. (Unlike the deep-net overconfidence of Guo 2017 in c1.2, this one is *structural* — it comes from the independence assumption itself.)

**The fix.** Don't use NB's `predict_proba` as a probability. **Calibrate** with `CalibratedClassifierCV` on a held-out set: **isotonic** regression (nonparametric monotone map — flexible, needs more data) or **Platt/sigmoid** scaling (two-parameter sigmoid — safer on small calibration sets). Both are *monotone*, so they fix log-loss/Brier/ECE **without changing the AUC** (the cleanest demonstration that ranking and calibration are independent — c1.2). Verify with a reliability diagram before trusting a probability-times-dollars threshold.

**Rubric:**
- AUC = ranking only; calibration = probability accuracy; orthogonal (monotone transforms preserve AUC)
- Mechanism: correlated features → NB multiply-counts evidence → log-odds inflated → posteriors pushed to 0/1 (overconfident)
- Domingos–Pazzani: hurts probabilities, not ranking; structural (vs Guo 2017 deep-net case)
- Fix: `CalibratedClassifierCV` isotonic (more data) vs sigmoid (less); monotone → AUC unchanged; verify with reliability diagram

</details>
