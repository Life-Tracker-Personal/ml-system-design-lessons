## TL;DR

Imbalance is **not a problem in itself** — it's only a problem when it degrades the metric you actually care about, and the cleanest fix is almost never to touch the data: **train normally, then move the decision threshold and recalibrate.** The key result: **every resampling/reweighting scheme is mathematically equivalent to an implicit shift of the decision threshold on the base rate** — so if you're going to set a threshold anyway (you are), the resampling was at best a detour and at worst it destroyed your calibration. This unifies the imbalance threads already in the course: c1.3's loss levers (focal/class-balanced, decoupling), c1.1's gradient-share and 95%-accuracy trap, and c1.10's PR-AUC-over-ROC-AUC.

## What you'll be able to do after this lesson

- Explain precisely when imbalance hurts (and when it doesn't) in terms of the target metric.
- Write SMOTE's interpolation, state its convexity assumption, and say where it breaks (high-dim, categoricals).
- Spot and fix the SMOTE-then-split leakage trap with a resampling-inside-CV pipeline.
- Derive the cost-optimal threshold and show that resampling is an implicit, less-calibrated version of it.
- Apply prior correction to recover true-prevalence probabilities after training on resampled data.
- Decide between resampling, reweighting, threshold-moving, and anomaly-detection for a given imbalance ratio.

## Why imbalance gets its own (consolidating) lesson

> **Goal — turn the scattered imbalance folklore into one decision framework, and replace "always oversample" with "calibrate + threshold, and only resample if it provably helps your metric."** The high-value interview move here is contrarian and evidence-backed: the reflex to SMOTE is usually unnecessary, often harmful to calibration, and almost always reproducible by a threshold move you should make explicitly anyway.

> **Vocabulary primer.** The "rusty staff engineer" assumption from c1.1 still holds.
>
> - **Imbalance ratio (IR)** — $N_{\text{maj}}/N_{\text{min}}$; "mild" ≤ 4:1, "extreme" ≥ 100:1; fraud/CTR routinely $1000{:}1$+.
> - **Over/under-sampling** — duplicate minority (ROS) / drop majority (RUS) rows.
> - **SMOTE** — synthesize minority points by interpolating between minority neighbors.
> - **Class weight / cost-sensitive** — scale each class's loss; act on a cost matrix.
> - **Threshold moving / prior correction** — adjust the decision cutoff / recalibrate predicted probabilities for the deployment base rate.

## Part 1 — When is imbalance actually a problem?

> **Goal — stop treating imbalance as automatically bad; locate the real harm in the metric.**

Accuracy is *prevalence-weighted*, so "always predict negative" scores 95% at 5% positives (c1.1's 95%-accuracy trap) — useless. Under plain cross-entropy, the $q-y$ gradient (c1.3) is ~99% sourced from negatives at 1% prevalence, so the minority barely moves the weights. **But** — and this is the framing — **ROC-AUC is invariant to prevalence** (c1.10), so imbalance doesn't hurt *ranking*; it hurts the *operating point* (precision collapses) and the *gradient signal*. The honest statement: **imbalance is a problem only insofar as it degrades the metric you care about.** A well-calibrated model evaluated by PR-AUC and deployed at a cost-chosen threshold often needs **no** resampling at all.

## Part 2 — Resampling (and its leakage trap)

> **Goal — synthesize/drop rows to rebalance, and know the assumption and the leakage failure that come with it.**

**Random oversampling** duplicates minority rows (adds no information → overfits the duplicates); **random undersampling** drops majority rows (discards data, especially hard negatives near the boundary). **SMOTE** (Chawla et al. 2002) synthesizes minority points by interpolating between a minority point and one of its $k$ minority neighbors:

$$x_{\text{new}} = x_i + u\,(x_i^{(\text{nn})} - x_i),\qquad u\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)$$

so new points lie on segments of the minority $k$-NN graph — **assuming the minority class is locally convex/linear** in feature space. Variants concentrate synthesis on the boundary: **Borderline-SMOTE** (only "in-danger" points) and **ADASYN** (density-adaptive: more synthesis where the minority is harder). For mixed types, **SMOTENC** takes the neighborhood *mode* of categoricals (plain SMOTE on one-hots yields meaningless fractions).

<SmoteInterpolation caption="SMOTE, in one picture. Pick a minority point (red, ringed), find its k=5 minority neighbors, and place synthetic points (open red circles) on the line segments to each. Great when the minority manifold is locally linear/convex — bad when it curves. The inset shows the failure: a banana-shaped minority distribution has synthesized points landing off the true manifold entirely. That's why Borderline-SMOTE and ADASYN exist, and why 'SMOTE on high-dim categoricals' is nonsense unless you use SMOTENC." />

**The critical leakage trap.** Resampling is a *fit*-time transform that uses labels. Doing it **before** the split (or before CV folding) lets a synthetic/duplicated point derived from a row land in a *different* fold than that row → the validation label leaks into training (c1.1's information-availability rule), the exact structural bug as c1.5's non-OOF stacking. **Resample inside each fold, on the training partition only.** Use `imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline`, whose samplers run only on `fit`/`fit_resample` and are **bypassed on `transform`/`predict`** — so the held-out fold is scored at the true prevalence. (`sklearn.pipeline.Pipeline` can't host a resampler, which is *why* imbalanced-learn ships its own.)

<SmoteLeakageSplit caption="Wrong (left) vs right (right). Left: SMOTE runs on the full dataset, then you split — every synthetic point is an interpolation of two real points, and if one of those real points ends up in val, its synthesized neighbors are already in train. Val is contaminated. Right: split first, then run SMOTE only on the training fold; val is scored at the true prevalence, on rows the model has never seen or interpolated from. The `imblearn.pipeline` API exists specifically to make the right flow the default." />

## Part 3 — Reweighting and cost-sensitive learning

> **Goal — re-weight the loss instead of the data, and know when that differs from resampling.**

Class weighting scales each class's loss by a constant; sklearn's `class_weight='balanced'` uses $w_c=\frac{N}{K\,n_c}$ (n_samples / (n_classes × class count)) — e.g. 100 pos / 900 neg gives $w_{\text{pos}}=1000/(2\cdot100)=5$, $w_{\text{neg}}\approx0.56$. **Reweighting ≡ resampling in expectation** over a full epoch (both rescale the minority's total gradient contribution), but they **diverge under minibatch SGD**: oversampling changes the *empirical distribution* (more steps on duplicated rows → overfitting), reweighting changes per-example gradient *magnitude* (noisier/larger steps, may need LR retuning). This is c1.3's Stop-and-think made precise — and neither is free, because both shift the base rate (Part 4).

*Concretely — where they diverge:* oversampling puts more *gradient steps* on duplicated rare rows (they appear twice each epoch), so the model sees them more often. Class weighting takes fewer steps but each step's rare-class contribution is bigger. Identical under full-batch GD; genuinely different under minibatch SGD, where step count and step magnitude both affect the trajectory.

## Part 4 — Threshold moving and prior correction (the central insight)

> **Goal — see that the right lever is a calibrated probability plus a cost-derived threshold, and that resampling is just an implicit, worse-calibrated version of it.**

**Cost-optimal threshold.** With per-error costs, predict positive iff the calibrated $p\ge t^\star=\dfrac{c_{\text{FP}}}{c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}}}$ (c1.10). Imbalance enters only through how it affects your ability to *estimate* $p$ — not through any need to rebalance.

**Resampling = an implicit threshold shift.** Train on a balanced base rate $\bar y$ instead of the true $\tau$. By Bayes, balancing **multiplies the predicted odds** by $(1-\tau)/\tau$ relative to the truth:

$$\frac{p_{\text{bal}}}{1-p_{\text{bal}}}=\frac{p_{\text{true}}}{1-p_{\text{true}}}\cdot\frac{1-\tau}{\tau}$$

So (i) the predicted probabilities are now systematically **over-estimated** for the minority — *miscalibrated* (exactly what van den Goorbergh et al. 2022 measured: resampling leaves ROC-AUC ~unchanged but wrecks calibration); and (ii) the *decision* is identical to leaving probabilities alone and moving the threshold by the corresponding amount. **"Resampling improved my classifier" almost always means "resampling silently moved my threshold."** Correct the prior — **King & Zeng's** intercept correction $\beta_0^{\text{corr}}=\hat\beta_0-\ln\!\bigl[\frac{1-\tau}{\tau}\frac{\bar y}{1-\bar y}\bigr]$ — and you recover the original model. (The multiclass version is **logit adjustment**, $f_y+\log\pi_y$, Menon et al. 2021 — from c1.3; and Kang 2020's decoupling shows the *classifier head*, not the representation, is the bottleneck.)

*What the King–Zeng correction is doing:* if you trained on 50/50 but will deploy at 1/99, every log-odds output is offset by the same constant $\ln[(1-\tau)/\tau \cdot \bar y/(1-\bar y)]$. Subtract that constant from every logit and the probabilities snap back to the true prevalence — no retraining needed.

> **Stop and think.** A colleague reports a jump from 0.78 to 0.95 PR-AUC after applying SMOTE to the whole dataset and then doing 5-fold CV. Why is that number almost certainly fake? *Hint: SMOTE-before-split is leakage — synthetic minority points are interpolations of real points, so a validation point's near-duplicate (or its very neighbors) sit in the training folds; the model is scored on rows it effectively trained on (c1.1 / c1.5 OOF). Resample inside each fold via `imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline` and the inflated score collapses back toward 0.78.*

## Part 5 — Extreme imbalance: reframe as anomaly detection

> **Goal — recognize when the "positive class" is too sparse to model as classification.**

At ~0.01% positives, there aren't enough positives to define a decision boundary; model the **majority (normal) distribution** and flag outliers. **One-Class SVM** (Schölkopf 2001) learns a boundary enclosing a $\nu$-fraction of normal data — best when training data is *clean* (novelty detection). **Isolation Forest** (Liu et al. 2008): anomalies are isolated in *fewer* random splits → shorter average path length; robust to contaminated/multimodal data, with `contamination` setting the flagged fraction. This is the same "is it really imbalance, or a different problem?" move as c1.1's click-data-is-causal-inference framing.

**Evaluate under imbalance with PR-AUC, not ROC-AUC** (c1.10), use per-class metrics, and **stratify your splits** (c1.1) — at 1% positives a 1000-row test set has ~Binomial(1000, 0.01) positives (SD ≈ 3.15), so recall estimates are wildly noisy without stratification.

## The stats / quant-research lens

- **Prior correction is the headline result** — resampling changes the effective base rate, so predicted probabilities are biased by a known log-odds offset; the King–Zeng intercept correction recovers the true posterior. This is the same machinery as **case-control sampling** in epidemiology and **importance weighting** (c1.1: oversampling the minority = a proposal $q$; weights $p/q$ restore unbiased gradients).
- **SMOTE's manifold assumption, as statistics** — interpolation assumes local convexity. In high dimensions it provably fails: Blagus & Lusa (2013) show SMOTE **leaves class means ~unchanged while shrinking within-class variance and inducing inter-sample correlation**, so it doesn't reduce majority bias for most classifiers; on categoricals it produces nonsense (→ SMOTENC).
- **Calibration cost** — all corrections leave ROC-AUC ~unchanged (discrimination is a monotone-invariant rank statistic, c1.10) but RUS/ROS/SMOTE systematically **over-estimate minority probability** (van den Goorbergh 2022) — a reliability/Brier failure (c1.3's Murphy decomposition). The sensitivity "gain" is reproducible by threshold-shifting alone.
- **Threshold-vs-resample as expected-cost minimization** — estimate calibrated $p$, set $t^\star=c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}})$; resampling is then exposed as a prior shift that implicitly re-derives a threshold you should set explicitly.

## Tradeoff matrix

| Lever | What it does | Cost | Reach for it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Threshold move + calibrate** | sets the operating point on a calibrated $p$ | ~free, honest | **default** — almost always first |
| Class weights / `pos_weight` | reweights the loss | cheap; shifts base rate | gradient signal too weak; recalibrate after |
| Random over/under-sample | rebalances data | overfit (ROS) / data loss (RUS) | RUS to *scale* at extreme IR |
| SMOTE / ADASYN | synthesizes minority | leakage risk; high-dim/categorical failure | low-dim, continuous, *and* it beats threshold on your metric |
| Anomaly detection | models normal, flags outliers | different paradigm | extreme IR (~0.01%) |

## Worked decision walk-throughs

**Scenario A: 1:50 imbalance, you're using XGBoost.** **Don't SMOTE.** Train normally, **calibrate**, and **move the threshold** to the cost-optimal point (Elor 2022: for strong probabilistic learners with a tuned threshold, SMOTE gives no benefit). Evaluate with PR-AUC.

**Scenario B: you've decided SMOTE genuinely helps (weak learner, low-dim, validated).** Put it **inside an `imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline`** so it resamples only the training fold; never SMOTE-then-split.

**Scenario C: 1:10,000 fraud.** Reframe as **anomaly detection** (Isolation Forest / One-Class SVM) — too few positives to learn a boundary; or aggressively undersample negatives + ensemble.

**Scenario D: you must report honest performance.** **PR-AUC** (not ROC-AUC), **stratified** splits, calibrated probabilities, and the operating point's precision/recall at the cost-chosen threshold.

## Production failure modes

1. **SMOTE before the split.** Leakage → inflated CV scores that collapse in production. Resample inside the pipeline/fold.
2. **Resampling breaks calibration.** Predicted probabilities over-estimate the minority; apply prior correction or recalibrate before using $p$ for decisions.
3. **SMOTE on categorical features.** Interpolating one-hots is meaningless; use SMOTENC.
4. **SMOTE in high dimensions.** Doesn't move class means, shrinks variance (Blagus–Lusa); often no benefit.
5. **ROC-AUC for imbalanced evaluation.** Hides the precision collapse; use PR-AUC (c1.10).
6. **`class_weight` + the mean-reduction gotcha** (c1.3): weighted losses can change the effective learning rate; tune accordingly.

## Interview gotchas

- **"How do you handle class imbalance?"** First: does it actually hurt the *target metric*? If so, **calibrate + move the threshold** before resampling. Cite Elor 2022 (SMOTE's benefit vanishes for strong learners once you tune the threshold).
- **"Is SMOTE-then-train fine?"** Only inside a fold — SMOTE-then-split is leakage.
- **"Does resampling improve the model?"** It shifts the base rate → an implicit threshold move + worse calibration; correct the prior and you recover the original model.
- **"ROC-AUC or PR-AUC for imbalanced data?"** PR-AUC (c1.10) — ROC-AUC is prevalence-invariant and hides the operating-point collapse.
- **"Extreme imbalance (1:10⁴)?"** Reframe as anomaly detection; or undersample + ensemble.
