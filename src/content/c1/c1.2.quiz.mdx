**Q1.** Derive from first principles that minimizing mean squared error (MSE) is equivalent to maximum-likelihood estimation under additive Gaussian noise. Show every step. Why does the variance $\sigma^2$ not affect the optimizing parameters?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Setup.** Assume the data-generating process is

$$y_i = \hat{f}_\theta(x_i) + \epsilon_i, \qquad \epsilon_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$$

Equivalently, $y_i \mid x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\hat{f}_\theta(x_i), \sigma^2)$.

**One-example likelihood.**

$$p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i))^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

**Joint log-likelihood for $n$ iid examples.**

$$\log L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i))^2$$

**Maximize over $\theta$.** The first term doesn't depend on $\theta$, drop it:

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta\, -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_i (y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i))^2 = \arg\min_\theta \sum_i (y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i))^2$$

The factor $1/(2\sigma^2) > 0$ is a positive constant w.r.t. $\theta$, so it can be dropped without changing the argmin. The result is the MSE objective (up to a constant $1/n$ if you average rather than sum). **QED.**

**Why $\sigma^2$ doesn't matter for $\hat{\theta}$.** Because $\sigma^2$ appears only as a *positive multiplicative constant* on the residual-sum-of-squares term. Multiplying an objective by a positive constant doesn't change its minimizer. $\sigma^2$ affects the *value* of the likelihood at the optimum (it's needed to actually compute predictive intervals or to estimate it jointly via MLE) but not the *location* of the optimum.

**Subtle bonus.** Joint MLE for $(\theta, \sigma^2)$ does give an estimate of $\sigma^2$ — it's the residual mean square, $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i \hat{r}_i^2$. So you can recover the noise scale after fitting; you just don't need it during fitting.

**Rubric — full credit:**
- Write the Gaussian likelihood
- Take log, drop constants in $\theta$
- Recognize that maximizing log-likelihood ↔ minimizing the negative
- Drop the $1/(2\sigma^2)$ factor with justification
- Land on $\arg\min \sum r_i^2$ (MSE)
- Articulate that $\sigma^2$ is a positive constant w.r.t. $\theta$ → doesn't affect argmin

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q2.** Repeat the derivation for MAE: show that minimizing MAE is MLE under additive Laplace noise. State the implication for what kind of conditional summary statistic the MAE-optimal model is computing.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Setup.** Assume $y_i = \hat{f}_\theta(x_i) + \epsilon_i$ with $\epsilon_i$ iid Laplace$(0, b)$, density

$$p(\epsilon) = \frac{1}{2b} \exp\!\left(-\frac{|\epsilon|}{b}\right)$$

**One-example log-likelihood.**

$$\log p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta) = -\log(2b) - \frac{|y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i)|}{b}$$

**Joint log-likelihood and MLE.**

$$\log L(\theta) = -n\log(2b) - \frac{1}{b} \sum_i |y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i)|$$

Maximizing over $\theta$ — drop constants in $\theta$, drop the positive constant $1/b$:

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\min_\theta \sum_i |y_i - \hat{f}_\theta(x_i)|$$

which is the MAE objective.

**Conditional summary statistic.** Recall that for a random variable $Y$ with distribution $p(Y)$:

- $E[Y]$ minimizes $E[(Y - c)^2]$ — the mean is the optimal squared-error predictor.
- $\text{median}(Y)$ minimizes $E[|Y - c|]$ — the median is the optimal absolute-error predictor.

Therefore an MAE-optimal model is computing the **conditional median** $\text{median}(Y \mid X = x)$ at each input. The MSE-optimal model is computing the **conditional mean**. These are different functions whenever the conditional distribution is skewed.

**Practical consequence.** For skewed targets like house prices, claim sizes, or latency, MAE-trained models systematically under-predict (because the median is below the mean for right-skewed distributions). That's not a bug — it's correct under the Laplace-noise assumption. The right framing for an interview is: *the loss decides which summary statistic the model is targeting, and you choose accordingly.*

**Rubric:**
- Laplace density written correctly
- Drop additive and positive multiplicative constants
- Land on $\arg\min \sum |r_i|$
- Identify that MAE-optimal = conditional median (with the parallel: MSE-optimal = conditional mean)
- Bonus: discuss bias under skewed targets

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q3.** You have 999 training examples with residuals roughly $\pm 1$ and 1 outlier with residual $+100$. Compute the *exact* contribution of the outlier to the gradient under MSE vs MAE, as a fraction of total gradient magnitude. What does this tell you, quantitatively, about robustness?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

Let the per-example loss have gradient $g_i = \partial \mathcal{L}_i / \partial \hat{y}_i$. The total gradient on the prediction has magnitude $|\sum_i g_i|$ but more relevant for influence is the absolute contribution $|g_i|$.

**Under MSE** ($\mathcal{L} = (y - \hat{y})^2$): $g_i = -2(y_i - \hat{y}_i) = -2 r_i$.

- Bulk contribution: 999 examples × $2 \times 1 = 1998$ units of gradient magnitude.
- Outlier contribution: $1 \times 2 \times 100 = 200$ units.
- **Outlier fraction:** $200 / (1998 + 200) = 200/2198 \approx 9.1\%$.

The single anomalous example accounts for ~9% of the total optimization signal at this snapshot — it has substantial control over how the model moves.

**Under MAE** ($\mathcal{L} = |y - \hat{y}|$): $g_i = -\text{sign}(y_i - \hat{y}_i) \in \{-1, +1\}$.

- Bulk: $999 \times 1 = 999$ units.
- Outlier: $1 \times 1 = 1$ unit.
- **Outlier fraction:** $1 / 1000 = 0.1\%$.

The outlier contributes the same as any other example — its larger residual gets it no extra influence because the gradient is *saturated*.

**Quantitative interpretation.** The outlier has ~91× more influence under MSE than under MAE in this setup. More generally, an outlier with residual $r_o$ relative to bulk residual $r_b$ has influence ratio $\approx |r_o|/|r_b|$ under MSE vs $\approx 1$ under MAE.

**The deeper point.** Robustness isn't a property of the loss in isolation; it's a property of how the loss's gradient magnitude scales with the residual. *Bounded-gradient losses* (MAE, Huber's tail, log-cosh's tail) prevent any single example from dominating. *Unbounded-gradient losses* (MSE) let large residuals seize the gradient.

**Rubric:**
- Compute MSE bulk + outlier contributions
- Compute MAE bulk + outlier contributions
- Get fractions $\sim 9\%$ vs $\sim 0.1\%$ correct
- Generalize: outlier influence ratio scales with residual under MSE, constant under MAE
- State the general principle: bounded-gradient ↔ robust

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q4.** MAE is not differentiable at $r = 0$. Define the subgradient there and explain (a) why this isn't fatal for SGD-based optimizers in practice, and (b) what *is* fatal for second-order optimizers like XGBoost.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Subgradient at zero.** For a convex function $f$, the **subgradient** at a point $x_0$ is the set of slopes $g$ such that $f(x) \ge f(x_0) + g(x - x_0)$ for all $x$. At $r = 0$ for $f(r) = |r|$, any slope between $-1$ and $+1$ satisfies this inequality (it's a "supporting line"). So the subgradient set at zero is the closed interval

$$\partial |r|\bigl|_{r=0} = [-1, +1]$$

A common convention is to just pick $0$ (or `sign(0) = 0` numerically) as the subgradient value at zero. Any choice in $[-1, +1]$ is mathematically valid.

**(a) Why this isn't fatal for SGD.** SGD steps are $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta g$ where $g$ is a (sub)gradient estimate. Three reasons the kink doesn't break things:

1. **Vanishing probability of exactly hitting zero.** Continuous residuals are almost never exactly zero in floating-point arithmetic. The set $\{r = 0\}$ has measure zero. SGD just doesn't visit the kink.
2. **Any choice in $[-1, +1]$ is a valid descent direction.** Even if you hit zero exactly, picking $\text{sign}(0) = 0$ as the subgradient is a fine choice — it just means "no update from this example this step."
3. **Mini-batches average over many examples.** The aggregate gradient over a mini-batch is smooth in expectation even when individual examples sit at kinks.

So in practice, MAE works fine with first-order optimizers like SGD, Adam, etc.

**(b) Why second-order optimizers like XGBoost choke.** Second-order optimizers use the **Hessian** $H = \partial^2 \mathcal{L} / \partial \hat{y}^2$ for Newton-like updates: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta H^{-1} g$. For MAE:

$$\frac{\partial^2 |r|}{\partial r^2} = 0 \quad \text{wherever differentiable}$$

The Hessian is zero almost everywhere. Newton's method divides by zero (or by tiny numbers), which gives infinite or wildly-varying updates. XGBoost specifically computes a per-leaf optimal step using the Hessian; with Hessian = 0, the optimal step is undefined. The library either refuses to optimize ("zero gradient" — confusingly named, it's really "zero Hessian") or defaults to constant updates.

**Fix.** Use **pseudo-Huber loss**:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{pHuber}}(r) = \delta^2 \bigl(\sqrt{1 + (r/\delta)^2} - 1\bigr)$$

It's a smooth function that behaves like MSE near zero (quadratic) and like MAE for $|r| \gg \delta$ (linear). It has a well-defined, nonzero Hessian everywhere. XGBoost exposes this as `objective="reg:pseudohubererror"`.

**Rubric:**
- Define subgradient set as $[-1, +1]$ at $r = 0$
- Three reasons SGD copes with the kink (measure zero, valid sub-direction, mini-batch smoothing)
- Identify the Hessian as the problem for second-order methods
- Show that $\partial^2 |r|/\partial r^2 = 0$ a.e.
- Propose pseudo-Huber (or equivalent smooth approximation) as the fix

</details>

**Q5.** Show that Huber loss has a continuous derivative everywhere — including at the threshold $\pm \delta$. Why does this matter operationally?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

Recall the definition:

$$\mathcal{L}_\delta(r) = \begin{cases} \tfrac{1}{2} r^2 & |r| \le \delta \\ \delta\bigl(|r| - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta\bigr) & |r| > \delta \end{cases}$$

**Derivative on each branch.**

- $|r| \le \delta$: $\dfrac{d}{dr}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2} r^2\bigr) = r$.
- $r > \delta$: $\dfrac{d}{dr}(\delta r - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2) = \delta$.
- $r < -\delta$: $\dfrac{d}{dr}(-\delta r - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2) = -\delta$.

**Check continuity at the join $r = \delta$.**

- Approaching from below ($r \to \delta^-$): derivative $\to \delta$.
- Approaching from above ($r \to \delta^+$): derivative $= \delta$.

Equal. The derivative is continuous at $r = \delta$. Symmetric argument at $r = -\delta$.

**Continuity of the loss itself at $r = \delta$.**

- Quadratic branch evaluated at $\delta$: $\tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2$.
- Linear branch evaluated at $\delta$: $\delta(\delta - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta) = \tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2$.

Equal. So the function joins smoothly *and* has matching derivative — it's a $C^1$ function (continuously differentiable everywhere).

**Why this matters operationally.**

1. **No subgradient pathology at the threshold.** Unlike MAE (which has a kink at $r = 0$), Huber has no point where the gradient is multi-valued. First-order optimizers can use the ordinary gradient without special cases.
2. **Stable second-order optimization.** The Hessian is $1$ inside the quadratic region and $0$ in the linear region. While the Hessian jumps at $\pm\delta$, it's not infinite — second-order methods like Newton or XGBoost can use it (with the caveat that the zero-Hessian region behaves like MAE there). In practice, **pseudo-Huber** is preferred for libraries that need a strictly positive Hessian; classical Huber's piecewise constant Hessian is acceptable for line-search methods.
3. **Predictable behavior across regimes.** The loss transitions smoothly between "MSE-like" and "MAE-like" — there's no discontinuity that would cause the optimizer to thrash.

**Rubric:**
- Derive derivative on each branch
- Evaluate both branches at $r = \delta$, both give $\delta$ → continuous
- Note loss values also match: $\tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2$ on both sides
- State this makes Huber $C^1$
- Operational consequence: no subgradient pathology, gradient-based optimization is straightforward

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q6.** You're training a regression model on a dataset of 1 million examples. Roughly 1% of the labels appear to be corrupted (gross errors). Design a protocol to decide between MSE, MAE, and Huber — and to pick $\delta$ for Huber. What evidence would you gather?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Step 1: characterize the noise.**

Fit a quick model with MSE and look at the residual distribution.

- *Histogram of residuals.* Is the bulk approximately Gaussian (bell-shaped, symmetric)? If yes, MSE's assumption holds for the bulk.
- *Tail heaviness.* Compare the empirical residual quantiles to a Gaussian fit. If the 99th-percentile residual is much larger than a Gaussian would predict (say, $> 4\sigma$), the tail is heavy.
- *Robust scale.* Estimate the noise scale via median absolute deviation: $\hat{\sigma}_{\text{MAD}} = 1.4826 \cdot \text{median}(|r_i - \text{median}(r)|)$. MAD is robust to outliers; ordinary std is not. If $\hat{\sigma}_{\text{std}} \gg \hat{\sigma}_{\text{MAD}}$, the std is being inflated by outliers — heavy tail confirmed.

**Step 2: classify outliers as real or corruptions.**

- *Domain check.* Are the outliers real signal (e.g., promotional spikes in revenue, exceptional patients) or corruptions (sensor failures, manual data-entry errors, mislabels)?
- *If real signal* → consider MSE (you want the model to fit them).
- *If corruptions* → MAE or Huber (you want the model to ignore them).

This question can't be answered statistically alone; talk to a domain expert.

**Step 3: choose the loss.**

- *~0% gross outliers + approx Gaussian noise* → MSE.
- *Many gross outliers but bulk is heavy-tailed too* → MAE.
- *Mostly Gaussian with a heavy tail of corruptions* → Huber (this is the standard "1% gross corruptions" case).

For the 1% gross outlier scenario described in the question, **Huber is the typical answer.**

**Step 4: pick $\delta$ for Huber.**

The classical rule from Huber's 1964 paper: $\delta = 1.345 \cdot \hat{\sigma}_{\text{MAD}}$. This value achieves 95% asymptotic efficiency under pure Gaussian noise — i.e., on clean data, Huber is only 5% less efficient than MSE.

In practice: try $\delta \in \{0.5, 1.0, 1.345, 2.0, 3.0\} \cdot \hat{\sigma}_{\text{MAD}}$ on a held-out validation set, evaluated by a metric that doesn't reward over-fitting to outliers (e.g., 95th-percentile-trimmed RMSE, or RMSE on a labeled-as-clean subset).

**Step 5: validate on a held-out set with known-good labels.** If possible, separate a small validation set whose labels you've manually verified as clean. Comparing MSE, MAE, and Huber on this gold set — rather than the noisy training distribution — gives you the cleanest signal of which choice generalizes.

**Step 6: monitor in production.** Track the residual distribution over time. If the corruption rate changes (say, a new label source comes online), $\delta$ may need re-tuning.

**Rubric:**
- Examine residual histogram and tails after a quick MSE fit
- Use median absolute deviation for robust scale estimation
- Distinguish real outliers from corruptions (domain-expert step)
- For the 1% gross-corruptions case, choose Huber
- Initialize $\delta \approx 1.345 \hat{\sigma}_{\text{MAD}}$ from Huber's classical rule
- Validate on a clean held-out set if possible

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q7.** The pinball (quantile) loss for quantile $q \in (0, 1)$ is

$$\rho_q(r) = \begin{cases} q \cdot r & r \ge 0 \\ (q - 1) \cdot r & r < 0 \end{cases}$$

Show that minimizing $\rho_q$ in expectation makes the optimal prediction the $q$-th conditional quantile. As a corollary, show that $q = 0.5$ recovers MAE up to a constant. What's the practical use case for $q = 0.95$?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Expected loss derivation.** For a fixed prediction $\hat{y}$ and random target $Y$ with CDF $F$ and density $f$:

$$\mathbb{E}_Y[\rho_q(Y - \hat{y})] = q \int_{\hat{y}}^\infty (y - \hat{y}) f(y)\, dy + (q-1) \int_{-\infty}^{\hat{y}} (y - \hat{y}) f(y)\, dy$$

Take the derivative with respect to $\hat{y}$ using Leibniz's rule. The boundary terms at $y = \hat{y}$ in each integral are zero (the integrand vanishes there), so we only need the partials inside:

$$\frac{d}{d\hat{y}} \mathbb{E}[\rho_q] = -q \int_{\hat{y}}^\infty f(y)\, dy - (q - 1) \int_{-\infty}^{\hat{y}} f(y)\, dy$$
$$= -q(1 - F(\hat{y})) - (q - 1) F(\hat{y})$$
$$= -q + q F(\hat{y}) - (q - 1) F(\hat{y})$$
$$= -q + F(\hat{y})$$

Setting this to zero: $F(\hat{y}^*) = q$, i.e., $\hat{y}^*$ is the $q$-th quantile of $Y$. So the minimizer of expected pinball loss is the $q$-th quantile.

**For a regression model**, the same argument applied conditionally on $X = x$ gives: $\hat{f}^*(x) = q\text{-th quantile of } Y \mid X = x$. The pinball-minimizing model is computing the conditional $q$-quantile.

**Corollary: $q = 0.5$ recovers MAE.** Plugging $q = 0.5$ into the definition:

$$\rho_{0.5}(r) = \begin{cases} 0.5 r & r \ge 0 \\ -0.5 r & r < 0 \end{cases} = 0.5 \cdot |r|$$

So $\rho_{0.5}(r) = \tfrac{1}{2}|r|$ — half of the MAE per-example loss. The constant factor doesn't affect the minimizer, so minimizing pinball at $q = 0.5$ is equivalent to minimizing MAE. The minimizer is the conditional median, which agrees with the general result at $q = 0.5$.

**Practical use case for $q = 0.95$.** You're predicting a quantity and want an **upper-bound estimate** that holds 95% of the time — e.g., "we want a latency target such that 95% of requests come in under it." Training a model with pinball loss at $q = 0.95$ gives you that conditional 95th percentile per input.

Common production usage: fit two models, one at $q = 0.05$ and one at $q = 0.95$, to produce a 90% prediction interval per input. Or fit at $q = 0.99$ to predict p99 latency. Or at $q = 0.5$ for a robust point estimate. The same model architecture trained with three different $q$'s gives you a calibrated distribution per input — much richer than a point prediction.

**Bonus: under-prediction and over-prediction asymmetry.** Notice $\rho_q$ is asymmetric: for $q > 0.5$, under-predicting costs more than over-predicting; for $q < 0.5$, the reverse. This makes pinball loss the natural choice when downstream error costs are asymmetric (e.g., under-stocking inventory costs more than over-stocking).

**Rubric:**
- Set up expected pinball loss as a split integral
- Differentiate and use $F$ to land on $F(\hat{y}^*) = q$
- Conclude: minimizer is the $q$-th quantile
- Show $\rho_{0.5}(r) = 0.5 |r|$, hence equivalence to MAE
- Practical use case: prediction intervals, p99 latency, asymmetric costs

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q8.** Linear regression with MSE has a closed-form solution $\theta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$. Linear regression with MAE does *not*. Why? Be specific about what's different in the optimization problem.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**MSE: why closed form exists.** The MSE objective for linear models is

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{n} \|y - X\theta\|_2^2 = \frac{1}{n}(y - X\theta)^\top(y - X\theta)$$

This is a **convex quadratic function** of $\theta$. Its gradient is *linear* in $\theta$:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = -\frac{2}{n} X^\top (y - X\theta)$$

Setting $\nabla = 0$ yields the **linear system** (the normal equations):

$$X^\top X \theta = X^\top y$$

A linear system in $\theta$ has a closed-form solution (when $X^\top X$ is invertible): $\theta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$. The closed form exists because *squaring made the optimality condition linear*.

**MAE: why no closed form.** The MAE objective is

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{n} \|y - X\theta\|_1 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i |y_i - x_i^\top \theta|$$

This is a piecewise linear function (its graph is made of flat regions joined at the points where some $y_i - x_i^\top \theta = 0$). The gradient is

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{n} \sum_i \text{sign}(y_i - x_i^\top \theta) \cdot x_i$$

Setting this to zero does *not* give a linear system in $\theta$ because `sign` is a piecewise-constant, non-linear function of $\theta$. The optimality condition reads: "find $\theta$ such that the signed sum of $x_i$ over examples with positive residual equals the signed sum over examples with negative residual" — a combinatorial condition that depends on which residuals are positive at the optimum.

**Equivalent formulations.** MAE minimization can be written as a **linear program**:

$$\min_{\theta, u} \sum_i u_i \quad \text{subject to} \quad -u_i \le y_i - x_i^\top \theta \le u_i$$

with $2n$ inequality constraints and $n + p$ variables. LPs don't have closed-form solutions in general (no inverse-style formula), but they're solvable in polynomial time by simplex or interior-point methods.

**The general principle.** Closed-form solutions for $\arg\min$ problems exist when the optimality condition is a *linear* equation in the parameters. That requires the objective's gradient to be linear, which essentially means the objective is *quadratic* in $\theta$. Anything more exotic (absolute values, sign functions, indicator functions) gives a piecewise structure that doesn't reduce to a single matrix inverse.

**Side note: numerical implications.** For large datasets, even MSE's closed form is rarely used directly — $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ is a $p \times p$ inverse that costs $O(p^3)$ to compute and is numerically unstable when $X^\top X$ is ill-conditioned. Production typically uses iterative methods (conjugate gradient, SGD) for both MSE and MAE. The "MSE has closed form" point is a theoretical convenience that translates to practical convenience only for small linear problems.

**Rubric:**
- MSE objective is quadratic in $\theta$
- Its gradient is linear in $\theta$
- Setting gradient to zero gives normal equations: a linear system
- Linear system → closed-form inverse
- MAE objective is piecewise linear
- Its gradient involves `sign`, which is non-linear in $\theta$
- Therefore no linear system, no closed form
- LP reformulation exists (bonus)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q9.** Standardizing features (subtract mean, divide by std) before fitting an MSE regression model gives a model whose predictions, after un-standardizing, are equivalent to fitting on the original scale. Is the same true for Huber loss? Explain.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**MSE is scale-equivariant.** Let $\tilde{x} = (x - \mu)/\sigma$ be the standardized feature and consider the linear model $\hat{y} = \theta_0 + \theta_1 \tilde{x}$ vs the original-scale model $\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$. They predict the same value when:

$$\beta_1 = \theta_1 / \sigma, \quad \beta_0 = \theta_0 - \theta_1 \mu / \sigma$$

Under MSE, the optimal $\theta$ minimizes $\sum (y_i - \theta_0 - \theta_1 \tilde{x}_i)^2$. Substituting $\tilde{x}_i = (x_i - \mu)/\sigma$ and grouping terms shows the optimum corresponds exactly to the optimal $\beta$ on the original scale. **The relationship is bijective: fitting on standardized features and un-standardizing is equivalent to fitting on original features.** MSE-optimal predictions are invariant to this affine transformation of features.

This is what we mean by *scale-equivariant*: rescale features → rescale parameters → same predictions.

**Why MSE has this property.** The MSE objective depends on features *only through the predictions* $\hat{y}_i = x_i^\top \theta$. The squaring is on the residual $y_i - \hat{y}_i$, which only sees feature-space through $\hat{y}$. So any feature transformation that can be absorbed into $\theta$ (linear transformations, including standardization) doesn't change the optimum's predictions.

**Huber loss inherits this property.** Inspect the Huber loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_\delta(r_i) = \begin{cases} \tfrac{1}{2} r_i^2 & |r_i| \le \delta \\ \delta(|r_i| - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta) & |r_i| > \delta \end{cases}$$

The Huber loss is a function of the **residual** only. Like MSE and MAE, it doesn't see the features directly — only through $\hat{y}$. Since standardization is a linear feature transformation absorbed by $\theta$, the same $\hat{y}$-values (and hence the same residuals) are achievable on either scale.

**However, there's a subtlety with $\delta$.** The Huber threshold $\delta$ is in the same units as the residual $r_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$, which is in the units of the *target* $y$. Feature standardization doesn't affect the target's units, so $\delta$ doesn't need to change. **But if you *also* standardize the target, $\delta$ would have to be rescaled by the target's std.** This is a common source of confusion.

**General rule.** Any loss that depends on features only through $\hat{y}$ is feature-scale-equivariant for linear models. This includes MSE, MAE, Huber, log-cosh, and pinball. Losses that have an additional hyperparameter in the units of $y$ (like Huber's $\delta$ or ε-insensitive's $\epsilon$) need that hyperparameter rescaled if you also rescale the target.

**Rubric:**
- MSE-optimal $\theta$ on standardized features ↔ MSE-optimal $\beta$ on original features, with bijection
- The deeper reason: MSE depends on features only through $\hat{y}$
- Huber inherits the property because it too depends only on the residual
- Subtlety: if you rescale the *target*, $\delta$ also rescales
- General rule: any residual-only loss is feature-scale-equivariant

</details>

**Q10.** You hand XGBoost a *custom* MAE objective that returns `grad = sign(r)` and `hess = 0`. Training produces a model whose leaf values look like garbage (or it warns about "zero gradient" and does nothing useful). Explain precisely what's wrong — it isn't the gradient — and propose two distinct fixes. Bonus: how does XGBoost's *built-in* `reg:absoluteerror` make MAE work natively?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The gradient is fine; the Hessian is the problem.** The MAE *gradient* is $\partial |r|/\partial r = \text{sign}(r) \in \{-1, +1\}$ — definitely **not** zero. What XGBoost actually needs (and your custom objective reports as zero) is the **Hessian**:

$$\frac{\partial^2 |r|}{\partial r^2} = 0 \quad \text{(wherever defined)}$$

XGBoost's tree-fitting algorithm (and the gradient-boosting framework generally) uses a Newton-style approximation. For each candidate leaf, the optimal leaf value $v^*$ is chosen as:

$$v^* = -\frac{\sum_{i \in \text{leaf}} g_i}{\sum_{i \in \text{leaf}} h_i + \lambda}$$

where $g_i$ are gradients and $h_i$ are Hessians, and $\lambda$ is a regularization term. For MAE, $h_i = 0$ everywhere, so the denominator collapses to $\lambda$ (or 0 if no regularization), and the algorithm either thrashes or refuses.

The "zero gradient" wording is colloquial — it really means "the second-order information needed for tree fitting is missing."

**Bonus — how the built-in `reg:absoluteerror` makes MAE work natively (XGBoost $\ge$ 1.7).** It decouples *tree structure* from *leaf values*. The tree is grown using a placeholder/approximate Hessian to decide splits, and then each leaf's output is overwritten with the **median of the residuals** falling into that leaf — which is exactly the MAE-optimal constant for that leaf (the median minimizes $\sum|r|$). This is Friedman's classic LAD-TreeBoost trick ("adaptive leaf values"): you never actually need a nonzero Hessian because the optimal leaf value has a closed form (the median) that doesn't go through the Newton step. So the modern library does *not* refuse MAE — a *naive custom* objective with `hess=0` does.

**Fix 1: pseudo-Huber loss.**

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{pHuber}}(r) = \delta^2 \bigl(\sqrt{1 + (r/\delta)^2} - 1\bigr)$$

This is a smooth approximation to Huber. Its Hessian is

$$\frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{L}_{\text{pHuber}}}{\partial r^2} = \frac{1}{(1 + (r/\delta)^2)^{3/2}}$$

which is strictly positive everywhere and never zero. XGBoost happily uses it as `objective="reg:pseudohubererror"` with a tunable `huber_slope` parameter (their name for $\delta$). Behaves like MSE for $|r| \ll \delta$ and like MAE for $|r| \gg \delta$.

**Fix 2: quantile loss at $q = 0.5$.**

XGBoost supports quantile loss via `objective="reg:quantileerror"`. The pinball loss at $q = 0.5$ is equivalent to MAE up to a constant, *but* XGBoost's quantile implementation uses a different leaf-selection rule — it computes the exact quantile of residuals in each leaf (a sorting operation, not a Hessian-based step). So this avoids the Hessian-zero problem by sidestepping the algorithm that needs it.

**Bonus fix 3: LightGBM has true MAE support.**

LightGBM has long supported `objective="regression_l1"` directly, using the same median-of-residuals leaf-value idea. The general point: both major GBDT libraries support exact MAE out of the box — the zero-Hessian trap is specific to rolling your own objective that reports `hess=0`.

**Rubric:**
- Identify that the library's "zero gradient" message is misleading; the actual problem is zero Hessian
- Show $\partial^2 |r|/\partial r^2 = 0$
- Explain how XGBoost uses the Hessian for leaf-value optimization
- Propose pseudo-Huber as fix 1, with the smooth-Hessian property
- Propose quantile-loss as fix 2, with the algorithm-not-using-Hessian explanation
- Bonus: built-in `reg:absoluteerror` uses adaptive leaf values (median of residuals per leaf, Friedman's LAD-TreeBoost) — so modern XGBoost supports MAE natively; only a naive `hess=0` custom objective fails
- Bonus: LightGBM's heuristic L1 implementation

**Q12.** Show that log-cosh loss is approximately quadratic near zero and approximately linear far from zero. Then argue: why might you prefer log-cosh over Huber in practice?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Definition.** $\mathcal{L}(r) = \log(\cosh(r))$ where $\cosh(r) = (e^r + e^{-r})/2$.

**Behavior near zero.** Taylor-expand $\cosh(r)$ around $r = 0$:

$$\cosh(r) = 1 + \frac{r^2}{2} + \frac{r^4}{24} + O(r^6)$$

So

$$\log(\cosh(r)) = \log\!\left(1 + \frac{r^2}{2} + \frac{r^4}{24} + \cdots\right)$$

Using the Taylor expansion of $\log(1 + u) = u - u^2/2 + O(u^3)$ with $u = r^2/2 + O(r^4)$:

$$\log(\cosh(r)) = \frac{r^2}{2} - \frac{r^4}{12} + O(r^6)$$

For small $|r|$, the leading term is $r^2/2$ — **the same as MSE/2**. So near zero, log-cosh behaves like a scaled MSE.

**Behavior for large $|r|$.** For large $|r|$, $\cosh(r) \approx e^{|r|}/2$ (one of the two exponentials dominates), so

$$\log(\cosh(r)) \approx \log\!\left(\frac{e^{|r|}}{2}\right) = |r| - \log 2$$

For large $|r|$, log-cosh is **linear** in $|r|$, like MAE (offset by a constant $-\log 2$ that doesn't affect optimization).

**Gradient and Hessian.**

$$\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{dr} = \tanh(r), \qquad \frac{d^2 \mathcal{L}}{dr^2} = 1 - \tanh^2(r) = \text{sech}^2(r)$$

The gradient is bounded in $(-1, +1)$ — same as MAE's saturation, but smooth. The Hessian is strictly positive everywhere ($> 0$), unlike Huber's Hessian which is exactly zero in the linear region. This is the key advantage.

**Why prefer log-cosh over Huber.**

1. **No hyperparameter.** Huber has $\delta$; log-cosh has nothing. The transition between quadratic and linear regimes happens at $|r| \approx 1$ for log-cosh, which means you should standardize your residuals (or your target) before using it. But once standardized, there's no tuning.
2. **Smooth Hessian everywhere.** $\text{sech}^2(r) > 0$ for all $r$, so second-order optimizers (Newton, XGBoost) have a non-degenerate Hessian everywhere. Huber's Hessian is exactly zero outside $|r| \le \delta$ — XGBoost can use Huber, but the leaf-value optimization in the linear region is then governed by regularization alone.
3. **Infinitely differentiable.** Useful for higher-order optimizers (rare but exists) or when you want to differentiate through the loss in a meta-learning setup.

**Why prefer Huber over log-cosh.**

1. **Computational simplicity.** Huber is a single comparison; log-cosh requires `cosh` and `log`, both more expensive operations.
2. **Tunable robustness.** $\delta$ in Huber gives explicit control over where the regime shift happens. Log-cosh's transition is fixed (around $|r| \approx 1$), so you have to set the scale through standardization.
3. **Long history and widespread analysis.** Huber's robust-statistics literature gives you rules of thumb (the 1.345 rule, 95% efficiency, breakdown analysis); log-cosh's literature is thinner.

**Practical recommendation.** Use Huber when you can choose and tune $\delta$ and need explicit outlier control. Use log-cosh when you want a no-tuning, smooth-Hessian alternative that's compatible with any second-order optimizer.

**Rubric:**
- Taylor expansion near zero: $\log(\cosh(r)) \approx r^2/2$
- Asymptotic behavior far from zero: $\log(\cosh(r)) \approx |r| - \log 2$
- Compute gradient $\tanh(r)$ and Hessian $\text{sech}^2(r)$
- Note that Hessian is strictly positive everywhere
- Compare to Huber: no hyperparameter, smooth Hessian, but more expensive to compute
- Bonus: discuss when each is preferred

<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Setup.** $q = \text{softmax}(z)$, one-hot label $y$, loss $\mathcal{L} = -\sum_i y_i \log q_i$.

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_j} = \sum_i \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial z_j} = \sum_i \left(-\frac{y_i}{q_i}\right) q_i(\delta_{ij} - q_j) = -\sum_i y_i(\delta_{ij} - q_j)$$

using $\sum_i y_i = 1$. So $\nabla_z \mathcal{L} = q - y$.

Pair cross-entropy with a different output nonlinearity, or squared error with softmax, and you reintroduce a multiplicative derivative term that can vanish.

- Algebraically split CE into $H(p) + \text{KL}$

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Argmax invariance.** $\text{softmax}(z/T)_k = e^{z_k/T}/\sum_j e^{z_j/T}$ is a strictly increasing function of $z_k$ (for $T>0$, dividing by $T$ is order-preserving, and softmax is monotonic in each logit). So $\arg\max_k \text{softmax}(z/T)_k = \arg\max_k z_k$ for every $T>0$. The predicted class never changes ⇒ **accuracy is identical for all $T>0$**. This is why temperature scaling is a "free" calibration fix.

**Confidence does change.** As $T \to \infty$, $z/T \to 0$ and $\text{softmax}\to$ uniform (entropy maximized, confidence → $1/K$). As $T \to 0^+$, the distribution concentrates on the argmax (entropy → 0, confidence → 1). So $T$ is a pure confidence/entropy knob: $T>1$ softens (fixes over-confidence), $T<1$ sharpens.

**When is optimal $T<1$?** $T$ is fit by minimizing validation NLL. If the model is *under*-confident — its correct predictions sit at modest probabilities (like Model A in Q7) — sharpening ($T<1$) lowers NLL. This is rarer for modern deep nets (which are over-confident, so usually $T>1$), but happens with heavily regularized models, strong label smoothing, or small ensembles averaged into mush.

**Rubric:**
- Argmax preserved because $z\mapsto z/T$ is order-preserving and softmax monotonic → accuracy unchanged
- Entropy/confidence varies: $T\to\infty$ uniform, $T\to0$ one-hot
- $T$ fit by validation NLL
- Optimal $T<1$ when the model is under-confident

</details>

</Quiz>

<Quiz id="22">

**Q10.** Evaluate $\log \text{softmax}([1000, 1001, 1002])$ both naively (softmax then log) and via the log-sum-exp trick, and show why the naive route fails. Which PyTorch loss should you use to avoid this, and what silent bug appears if you apply softmax yourself and then pass the result to `NLLLoss`?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Naive route fails.** Computing $\text{softmax}$ first requires $e^{1000}, e^{1001}, e^{1002}$ — all overflow to `inf` in float32/float64 (max representable $\approx 10^{308}$, and $e^{1000}\approx 10^{434}$). Then $\text{inf}/\text{inf} = \text{NaN}$. Even where it doesn't overflow, the subsequent $\log$ of an underflowed $0$ gives $-\text{inf}$.

**Log-sum-exp.** $\log\text{softmax}(z)_k = z_k - \operatorname{logsumexp}(z)$, with $\operatorname{logsumexp}(z) = m + \log\sum_j e^{z_j-m}$, $m=\max_j z_j = 1002$:

$$\operatorname{logsumexp} = 1002 + \log(e^{-2} + e^{-1} + e^{0}) = 1002 + \log(0.1353 + 0.3679 + 1) = 1002 + \log(1.5032) \approx 1002.4076$$

So $\log\text{softmax} \approx [\,-2.408,\ -1.408,\ -0.408\,]$, and $\text{softmax} \approx [0.0900, 0.2447, 0.6652]$. The max-subtraction keeps every exponent $\le 0$, so nothing overflows.

**Which loss.** Use `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` (it takes raw logits and is `LogSoftmax + NLLLoss` fused with the stable log-sum-exp), or `nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss` for the binary/multilabel case.

**The silent bug.** `nn.NLLLoss` expects **log-probabilities**. If you compute `softmax(z)` yourself and pass it to `NLLLoss`, you've passed probabilities, not log-probabilities — the loss takes a stray $\log$ where there should be none / none where there should be one, training on a wrong objective. No exception is raised; the model just trains poorly. (Symmetrically, passing already-softmaxed values to `CrossEntropyLoss` applies softmax twice.)

**Rubric:**
- Naive softmax overflows ($e^{1000}=$ inf) → NaN
- Log-sum-exp with max-subtraction; compute logsumexp ≈ 1002.41 and the log-probs
- Use `CrossEntropyLoss` (logits in; LogSoftmax+NLLLoss fused)
- `NLLLoss` expects log-probs; feeding softmax/logits is a silent correctness bug

</details>

</Quiz>

<Quiz id="23">

**Q11.** You train a binary classifier on a 1:99 positive:negative dataset with `BCEWithLogitsLoss`. Explain the role of `pos_weight`, what value you'd start with, and the qualitative effect of raising it on precision, recall, and the model's effective decision threshold. How does `pos_weight` differ from the `weight` argument?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**What `pos_weight` does.** `BCEWithLogitsLoss` with `pos_weight = w` computes, per example, $-w\,y\log\sigma(z) - (1-y)\log(1-\sigma(z))$. It multiplies **only the positive term**, so each positive example contributes $w\times$ the gradient of a negative. This counteracts the 99:1 dominance of negatives in the summed loss.

**Starting value.** The standard heuristic is $\text{pos\_weight} = \#\text{neg}/\#\text{pos} = 99$ — equalizing the total gradient mass of the two classes. Treat it as a starting point to tune, not gospel.

**Effect of raising it.** Larger `pos_weight` makes false negatives costlier relative to false positives, so the model is pushed to predict positive more readily: **recall ↑, precision ↓**. Equivalently, it shifts the *effective decision threshold* downward (the model's optimal operating point moves to flag more positives) and **inflates predicted probabilities** for the positive class (so you'll likely need to recalibrate before treating outputs as true probabilities).

**`pos_weight` vs `weight`.** `weight` scales the *entire* per-example (or per-element) loss — it reweights examples/elements regardless of their label. `pos_weight` scales *only the positive term within* the BCE expression — it reweights the positive class specifically. Use `pos_weight` for class imbalance in a binary/multilabel setting; use `weight` for per-example importance weighting.

**Rubric:**
- `pos_weight` multiplies the positive term of BCE → boosts positive-class gradient
- Start at #neg/#pos = 99
- Raising it: recall ↑, precision ↓, effective threshold drops, probabilities inflate (recalibrate)
- `weight` scales whole-example loss; `pos_weight` scales only the positive term

</details>

</Quiz>

<Quiz id="24">

**Q12.** Refute both halves of the claim: *"focal loss only helps with class imbalance, and cross-entropy gives you calibrated probabilities for free."* Cite named evidence. Then make the deeper point: for long-tailed classification, when is the loss function the *wrong* lever, and what works better?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Half 1 — "focal loss only helps imbalance" is false.** Mukhoti et al. (NeurIPS 2020, "Calibrating Deep Neural Networks using Focal Loss") show focal loss trains *better-calibrated* models than cross-entropy on **balanced** benchmarks. Mechanism: focal loss can be bounded below by a KL term minus an entropy-like term, so it implicitly **regularizes confidence** (penalizes overconfident predictions) — an effect independent of imbalance. So focal loss has a real, general second use: confidence regularization.

**Half 2 — "CE is calibrated for free" is false.** Guo et al. (ICML 2017, "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks") show modern deep nets are **systematically overconfident**: increasing depth/width and adding batch norm *worsen* calibration, and networks overfit NLL (test error keeps dropping while test NLL rises — confidence inflates). That's why temperature scaling exists. Nuance worth stating: Minderer et al. (NeurIPS 2021) find newer non-convolutional architectures (ViT, MLP-Mixer) are *better* calibrated out of the box — so even the "modern nets are overconfident" story is architecture-dependent and has evolved.

**Deeper point — the loss is often the wrong lever.** Kang et al. (ICLR 2020, "Decoupling Representation and Classifier") found that on long-tailed recognition, **class imbalance barely hurts representation learning — the bottleneck is the classifier**. Training the backbone normally (instance-balanced sampling) and then *retraining only the classifier head* (cRT) or rescaling its weight norms ($\tau$-normalization) beats elaborate reweighted/resampled losses on ImageNet-LT, Places-LT, and iNaturalist. And Menon et al. (ICLR 2021, "Logit Adjustment") give a **Bayes-consistent** alternative — add $\log \pi_y$ to the logits — that is theoretically grounded where focal/reweighting are heuristics. So the strong answer: focal loss ≈ a confidence regularizer + soft hard-mining; class-balanced ≈ a principled reweighting *magnitude*; but on real long-tail problems both are often dominated by *decoupling* or *logit adjustment*. Reach for the loss last, not first.

**Rubric:**
- Refute "focal only for imbalance": Mukhoti 2020, focal improves calibration on balanced data via confidence regularization
- Refute "CE auto-calibrated": Guo 2017 overconfidence (depth/width/BN, NLL overfitting); bonus Minderer 2021 architecture caveat
- Deeper point: Kang 2020 decoupling (classifier is the bottleneck, not the representation)
- Menon 2021 logit adjustment as the Bayes-consistent alternative
- Conclusion: the loss is frequently not the best lever for long-tail

</details>

</Quiz>
