**Q1. From “closest line” to the OLS formula.** OLS chooses the prediction vector $X\hat\beta$ closest to $y$. Show that statement algebraically in two ways: minimize RSS with calculus, then view $X\hat\beta$ as the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto the space spanned by $X$. Use the projection picture to decide whether the hat matrix is symmetric, idempotent, invertible, has trace $p$, and has eigenvalues in $\{0,1\}$. Finally, explain what must be true of the feature columns for the usual closed form to exist.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Calculus.** $\text{RSS}(\beta)=\|y-X\beta\|^2$; $\nabla_\beta\text{RSS}=-2X^\top(y-X\beta)=0 \Rightarrow X^\top X\hat\beta = X^\top y \Rightarrow \hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$. Hessian $2X^\top X\succeq 0$ ⇒ convex ⇒ global min.

**Projection.** Minimizing $\|y-X\beta\|$ means $X\hat\beta$ is the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto $\text{col}(X)$, so the residual $e=y-X\hat\beta \perp \text{col}(X)$: $X^\top e=0 \Rightarrow X^\top X\hat\beta=X^\top y$. Same equations, no calculus.

**Hat matrix** $H=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$, the projector onto $\text{col}(X)$:
- **Symmetric** ✓ ($H^\top=H$ by inspection).
- **Idempotent** ✓ ($H^2=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top=H$) — projecting twice = once.
- **Invertible** ✗ — $H$ is $n\times n$ but has rank $p<n$, so it's singular (it collapses the residual space to 0).
- **$\text{trace}=p$** ✓ ($\text{trace}(H)=\text{trace}((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X)=\text{trace}(I_p)=p$) — equals the effective degrees of freedom of the fit.
- **Eigenvalues in $\{0,1\}$** ✓ — a projector satisfies $H^2=H$, so each eigenvalue $\lambda$ obeys $\lambda^2=\lambda$.

**Existence.** The closed form needs $X^\top X$ invertible ⇔ $X$ has **full column rank** ($p$ linearly independent columns; requires $n\ge p$ and no perfect collinearity). Otherwise use the pseudoinverse or regularize (ridge).

**Rubric:**
- Both derivations land on $X^\top X\hat\beta=X^\top y$
- Symmetric ✓, idempotent ✓, invertible ✗ (rank $p<n$), trace $=p$ ✓, eigenvalues $\{0,1\}$ ✓ — with reasons
- Closed form needs full column rank / $X^\top X$ invertible

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q2. Why does squared error appear?** Begin with the assumption that outcomes equal a linear signal plus independent Gaussian noise. Write the likelihood and show why maximizing it chooses the same $\hat\beta$ as minimizing RSS. Then replace Gaussian noise with Laplace noise and show why absolute error appears instead. Finish by explaining, in words, how a noise model determines a loss and why the unbiased estimate of $\sigma^2$ divides RSS by $n-p$ rather than $n$.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**OLS = Gaussian MLE.** Assume $\varepsilon_i\overset{\text{iid}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$, so $y_i\mid x_i\sim\mathcal{N}(x_i^\top\beta,\sigma^2)$. The log-likelihood is

$$\log L = -\tfrac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-x_i^\top\beta)^2.$$

Only the second term depends on $\beta$, and the positive constant $1/(2\sigma^2)$ doesn't change the argmin, so maximizing over $\beta$ ⇔ minimizing $\sum_i(y_i-x_i^\top\beta)^2=\text{RSS}$ = OLS.

**Laplace → LAD.** If $\varepsilon_i\sim\text{Laplace}(0,b)$, the log-likelihood contributes $-\frac1b\sum_i|y_i-x_i^\top\beta|$, so MLE minimizes $\sum|r_i|$ — least-absolute-deviations (the conditional-median fit, exactly MAE from c1.2).

**General principle (c1.2).** The loss you minimize is the negative log-likelihood of an assumed noise model: Gaussian → squared loss (OLS, conditional mean); Laplace → absolute loss (LAD, conditional median); Student-$t$ → a robust, Huber-like loss. Choosing a loss is choosing a noise model.

**Unbiased $\sigma^2$.** The MLE is $\hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}}=\text{RSS}/n$ (biased downward). The unbiased estimator divides by the degrees of freedom: $\hat\sigma^2=\text{RSS}/(n-p)$.

**Rubric:**
- Write Gaussian log-likelihood; drop $\beta$-independent terms and the positive constant → minimize RSS
- Laplace noise → MLE = LAD (sum of absolute residuals)
- State the noise-model ↔ loss principle (Gaussian/squared, Laplace/absolute)
- Unbiased $\sigma^2 = \text{RSS}/(n-p)$ vs MLE $\text{RSS}/n$

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q3. Why can a useless feature improve $R^2$?** Add a pure-noise column, independent of $y$, to an OLS model. Reason from the enlarged set of possible fits—not from memorized rules—to determine what happens to training RSS and $R^2$. Then explain how adjusted $R^2$ charges for the added flexibility and what the new coefficient's $t$-statistic and $p$-value should look like across repeated samples.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

- **(a) Training RSS: can only decrease (or stay equal).** OLS now minimizes over a larger column space (the old space is a subspace), so the achievable minimum RSS cannot increase. In finite samples the noise feature picks up some spurious correlation, so RSS strictly decreases almost surely.
- **(b) $R^2 = 1-\text{RSS}/\text{TSS}$: can only increase.** Directly follows from (a) since TSS is fixed. **This is why $R^2$ is not a model-quality metric** — adding junk raises it.
- **(c) Adjusted $R^2 = 1-\frac{\text{RSS}/(n-p)}{\text{TSS}/(n-1)}$: usually decreases.** The denominator's $n-p$ shrinks as $p$ grows, penalizing the added parameter. Unless the noise feature reduces RSS by more than the degrees-of-freedom penalty (rare for true noise), adjusted $R^2$ falls.
- **(d) $t$-stat / $p$-value: usually non-significant.** For a feature truly independent of $y$, $\hat\beta_{\text{new}}$ is centered at 0 with its sampling SE, so $|t|$ is small and the $p$-value is large — by construction it'll exceed 0.05 about 95% of the time. (The ~5% false positives are the multiple-comparisons trap.)

**Asymmetry.** $R^2$ rewards in-sample fit only; adjusted $R^2$ charges a price per parameter, so it can fall — making it the honest summary when comparing models with different $p$.

**Rubric:**
- RSS ↓, $R^2$ ↑ (larger column space)
- Adjusted $R^2$ usually ↓ (dof penalty)
- New coefficient usually non-significant (centered at 0), ~5% spurious hits
- Articulate why $R^2$ is gameable and adjusted $R^2$ isn't

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q4. What does “best” in BLUE actually promise?** Evaluate this claim: *“OLS is the minimum-variance unbiased estimator, and this guarantee requires Gaussian errors.”* Unpack every qualifier in BLUE, identify the restricted class within which OLS is best, list the assumptions that buy the result, and separate the role of Gaussianity in estimation from its role in exact small-sample inference.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Both clauses are wrong as stated.**

**Clause 1 — "minimum-variance unbiased."** Gauss–Markov says OLS is **BLUE — best *linear* unbiased estimator**: minimum variance *within the class of linear unbiased estimators*. It is **not** necessarily the minimum-variance estimator among *all* unbiased estimators (that stronger property — MVUE / attaining the Cramér–Rao bound — does hold for OLS under added Gaussianity, but Gauss–Markov alone gives only the "linear" qualifier). And it's certainly not best among *all* estimators: biased estimators (ridge, James–Stein) can have lower MSE.

**Clause 2 — "requires Gaussian errors."** Gauss–Markov needs only: linearity, strict exogeneity $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$, homoskedasticity $\text{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I$, and no perfect collinearity. **Normality is not among them.** Normality is needed only for the *finite-sample* $t$/$F$ distributions and exact confidence intervals; without it, inference holds asymptotically via the CLT, but BLUE still stands.

**Correct statement:** "Under the Gauss–Markov assumptions (which do not include normality), OLS is the best *linear* unbiased estimator."

**Rubric:**
- "Minimum-variance unbiased" → should be "best *linear* unbiased" (BLUE)
- Note biased estimators can beat OLS in MSE
- Normality NOT required for BLUE; only for finite-sample inference
- List the actual Gauss–Markov assumptions

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q5.** Ridge regression is $\hat\beta=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$. (a) Why is $X^\top X+\lambda I$ invertible even when $p>n$ or $X$ is collinear? (b) Ridge is a *biased* estimator. State the bias–variance decomposition first for one coefficient and then for the full coefficient vector. Use the vector decomposition to explain why ridge can have lower total mean squared error than OLS, and name the classical result behind the argument.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**(a) Invertibility.** $X^\top X$ is symmetric positive-*semi*definite (eigenvalues $\ge 0$); adding $\lambda I$ with $\lambda>0$ shifts every eigenvalue up by $\lambda$, making them all $\ge\lambda>0$, so $X^\top X+\lambda I$ is positive-*definite* and hence invertible — **regardless of rank**, even when $p>n$ (where $X^\top X$ is singular) or columns are collinear (near-zero eigenvalues). This is ridge's structural advantage: it always has a unique solution.

**(b) Bias–variance.** For a single coefficient, MSE is a scalar and decomposes as

$$
\mathbb E\left[(\hat\beta_j-\beta_j)^2\right]
=\left(\mathbb E[\hat\beta_j]-\beta_j\right)^2
+\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j).
$$

For the full coefficient vector, $\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta)$ is a matrix, so it cannot be added directly to a scalar squared bias. Under squared Euclidean error, the correct total-MSE decomposition is

$$
\mathbb E\left[\lVert\hat\beta-\beta\rVert_2^2\right]
=\left\lVert\mathbb E[\hat\beta]-\beta\right\rVert_2^2
+\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta)\right).
$$

The trace sums the component variances. OLS is unbiased, but when $X^\top X$ has small eigenvalues, $\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta_{\text{OLS}})=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ can have a very large trace. Ridge shrinks coefficients toward zero, creating bias, while sharply reducing variance along those poorly identified directions. For an appropriate $\lambda>0$, the reduction in $\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta))$ can exceed the increase in squared bias, giving ridge lower total coefficient MSE. OLS minimizes variance only within the class of linear unbiased estimators.

**The intuition (skip the theorem).** With many noisy coefficients, shrinking them all toward zero gives up a little bias to buy a large cut in variance — so on *total* MSE the shrunk estimate can beat the unbiased one. That is the whole reason ridge is principled and not a hack. There is a celebrated formal result making this precise for a high-dimensional Gaussian mean, but no interviewer will ask you to reproduce it — if you want the exact statement and proof, ask an AI assistant (Claude or ChatGPT) to walk you through it.

**Rubric:**
- $X^\top X$ PSD; $+\lambda I$ shifts eigenvalues to $\ge\lambda>0$ → positive-definite → always invertible (even $p>n$)
- Scalar decomposition: coefficient MSE = squared bias + variance
- Vector decomposition: total MSE = squared bias norm + $\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta))$
- Explain why covariance itself cannot be added to scalar squared bias; ridge accepts bias to cut total variance
- Collinearity → huge OLS variance, so ridge wins for some $\lambda$
- Intuition: shrinkage trades a little bias for a big variance cut → can beat OLS on total MSE (no need to quote the formal theorem)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q6.** A coefficient equal to zero means the fitted model does not use that feature: its contribution $x_j\hat\beta_j$ is identically zero. Lasso can set some estimated coefficients **exactly equal to $0$**; ridge usually makes coefficients smaller without making them equal to $0$.

To see why, assume the feature columns are orthonormal, $X^\top X=I$, and compare these precisely scaled objectives:

$$
\text{ridge: }\frac12\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2+\frac\lambda2\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2,
\qquad
\text{lasso: }\frac12\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_1.
$$

Derive each solution from $\hat\beta^{\text{OLS}}=X^\top y$. Which OLS coefficient values does lasso map to exactly zero? Then answer three follow-ups: **(i) why do we start orthonormal at all — what breaks in the general case?** **(ii) does lasso's ability to produce zeros depend on orthonormality?** Give the general KKT/subgradient condition. **(iii)** Contrast with the precise—not absolute—claim we should make about ridge and feature selection.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

With $X^\top X=I$, the OLS solution is $\hat\beta^{\text{OLS}}=X^\top y$. Completing the square gives

$$
\frac12\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2
=\frac12\lVert\beta-\hat\beta^{\text{OLS}}\rVert_2^2+C,
$$

where $C$ does not depend on $\beta$. This is not a different definition of ridge or lasso. It is the original RSS term rewritten under the special condition $X^\top X=I$. Because the squared norm is a sum over $j$, each coefficient can now be minimized separately.

**Ridge.** For coordinate $j$, minimize

$$
\frac12(\beta_j-\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}})^2+\frac\lambda2\beta_j^2.
$$

Differentiate and set the derivative to zero:

$$
(\beta_j-\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}})+\lambda\beta_j=0.
$$

Therefore

$$\hat\beta_j^{\text{ridge}}=\frac{\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}}{1+\lambda}.$$

Ridge multiplies every OLS coefficient by the positive number $1/(1+\lambda)$. For example, if $\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}=0.02$, ridge might turn it into $0.01$ or $0.002$, but not exactly $0$. In this orthonormal case and for finite $\lambda$, the ridge estimate equals zero only if the OLS estimate was already zero.

**Lasso.** For coordinate $j$, minimize

$$
\frac12(\beta_j-\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}})^2+\lambda|\beta_j|.
$$

Consider three cases. If $\beta_j>0$, the derivative gives $\beta_j=\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}-\lambda$, which is valid only when $\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}>\lambda$. If $\beta_j<0$, it gives $\beta_j=\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}+\lambda$, valid only when $\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}<-\lambda$. If $|\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}|\le\lambda$, neither nonzero candidate has the required sign, and the minimum is at the kink $\beta_j=0$.

Combining the cases gives **soft-thresholding**:

$$\hat\beta_j^{\text{lasso}}=\text{sign}(\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}})\max\!\bigl(|\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}|-\lambda,\ 0\bigr).$$

Thus every OLS coefficient in the interval $[-\lambda,\lambda]$ is mapped to the number $0$—not merely to a small number. For example, with $\lambda=0.1$, OLS coefficients $0.06$ and $-0.08$ both become exactly $0$, while $0.35$ becomes $0.25$. A zero coefficient removes that feature from the fitted linear predictor, which is why lasso is described as performing feature selection.

**Why start orthonormal?** Not as a modelling assumption — as an algebraic device to expose the mechanism. Under a general $X$, the quadratic $\tfrac12\lVert y-X\beta\rVert^2$ has the Hessian $X^\top X$, which is a full (non-diagonal) matrix; the RSS contours in $\beta$-space are stretched, tilted ellipses (see the figure in the lesson), and the coefficients are *coupled* — changing $\beta_j$ changes the optimal $\beta_k$. Orthonormality (or any other assumption that makes $X^\top X$ a positive multiple of the identity) diagonalizes that quadratic, so the objective decouples into $p$ independent scalar problems in $(\beta_j - \hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}})^2$. Each 1D subproblem has a closed form, and it's from those closed forms — $\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}/(1+\lambda)$ and soft-thresholding — that the ridge/lasso contrast becomes visible on a page. In the general case the *geometric* story (corner-touching, dead zones) still holds, but no per-coordinate formula exists because the coordinates are coupled through the residual.

<RegularizationBalls caption="The geometry that survives without orthonormality. Ellipses (blue) are RSS contours around β̂ᴼᴸˢ — round only under orthonormal design, tilted otherwise. Orange: the L1 diamond has corners on the axes; a typical ellipse hits a corner, so β₂ = 0. The L2 disc is smooth; a typical ellipse touches at a generic interior direction, so both coefficients stay nonzero. **The zero-producing mechanism is general.** Orthonormality gives the simple soft-threshold formula; it is not required for sparsity. For a general $X$, let $r=y-X\hat\beta$ at the lasso solution. The KKT/subgradient condition is

$$
x_j^\top r=
\begin{cases}
\lambda, & \hat\beta_j>0,\\
-\lambda, & \hat\beta_j<0,\\
\text{some value in }[-\lambda,\lambda], & \hat\beta_j=0.
\end{cases}
$$

So if feature $j$'s remaining correlation with the residual is not strong enough to overcome the penalty, $|x_j^\top r|\le\lambda$, zero satisfies the optimality condition. With correlated columns the coefficients are coupled through $r$, so we cannot threshold each OLS coefficient independently; lasso may choose one correlated feature and zero another.

**Why ridge is different.** The L1 penalty has a constant-magnitude subgradient away from zero and a kink at zero, allowing an interval of data signals to share the same optimum $\hat\beta_j=0$. With the scaling used here, the L2 penalty's gradient is $\lambda\beta_j$ and vanishes smoothly as $\beta_j\to0$. Ridge therefore has no built-in sparsity or feature-selection mechanism and its solution is generally dense. A ridge coefficient can still be zero accidentally through symmetry or cancellation, so “ridge never produces zeros” is only true for a nonzero OLS coefficient in this orthonormal derivation—not as a universal statement.

**Rubric:**
- Explain “exact zero”: $\hat\beta_j=0$, so feature $j$ contributes nothing to predictions
- Show why RSS rewrites as distance from OLS only under $X^\top X=I$
- Ridge: $\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}/(1+\lambda)$ (proportional shrinkage; nonzero input stays nonzero)
- Lasso: soft-threshold $\text{sign}\cdot\max(|\cdot|-\lambda,0)$; the entire interval $[-\lambda,\lambda]$ maps to 0
- **Why orthonormal:** decouples $X^\top X$ so the quadratic separates into per-coordinate problems; the general case is coupled through the residual and has no per-coordinate closed form
- Orthonormality gives the closed form but is not required for lasso sparsity
- General KKT condition: zero is optimal when $|x_j^\top r|\le\lambda$, jointly with the other coefficients
- Precise ridge claim: smooth L2 has no sparsity mechanism and is generally dense, though accidental zeros are possible
- L1-ball corners vs round L2-ball geometry (see the figure)

</details>

**Q7. How does a penalty encode prior skepticism?** Starting from MAP estimation, show how a Gaussian prior on coefficients creates ridge and a Laplace prior creates lasso. Explain why the L1 kink—not positive probability mass at exactly zero—allows a MAP estimate to land at zero. Connect this to the broader rule: the data loss is a negative log-likelihood, while the regularization penalty is a negative log-prior.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**MAP setup.** With a Gaussian likelihood (squared-error data term), the MAP estimate maximizes $\log p(y\mid\beta)+\log p(\beta)$ — equivalently minimizes $\|y-X\beta\|^2 - 2\sigma^2\log p(\beta)$. The **negative log-prior is the penalty**.

- **Gaussian prior** $\beta_j\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2)$: $-\log p(\beta)\propto\sum_j\beta_j^2$ → **ridge** (L2), with $\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2$.
- **Laplace prior** $\beta_j\sim\text{Laplace}(0,b)$: $-\log p(\beta)\propto\sum_j|\beta_j|$ → **lasso** (L1).

**Why Laplace → sparsity.** The Laplace density $\propto e^{-|\beta|/b}$ has a **sharp, non-differentiable peak at 0** and heavier tails than the Gaussian. Because it is continuous, it does *not* place positive probability mass at the single point 0. The exact-zero MAP estimates instead come from the kink in its negative log-density, $|\beta|$: its nonzero slope persists all the way to zero, so a whole interval of weak data signals is optimized at the kink. The Gaussian negative log-density $\beta^2/(2\tau^2)$ is smooth with derivative 0 at the origin, so its pull fades continuously near zero and ordinarily shrinks without producing exact-zero modes.

**c1.2 tie-in.** This is the same lens, moved from the *data* to the *parameters*: in c1.2, the negative-log of the *noise* distribution was the data loss (Gaussian→squared, Laplace→absolute); here the negative-log of the *prior* on $\beta$ is the penalty (Gaussian→L2, Laplace→L1). Loss and penalty are both negative-log-densities; the whole objective is a negative-log-posterior.

**Rubric:**
- Ridge ↔ Gaussian prior; lasso ↔ Laplace prior
- Negative log-prior = penalty (MAP)
- Laplace: sharp peak / L1 kink at 0 → exact-zero MAP estimates (but no point mass at 0); Gaussian penalty is smooth → no exact zeros
- Connect to c1.2: loss = −log(noise), penalty = −log(prior)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q8. Why is logistic regression easy to optimize but impossible to solve in one algebraic step?** Derive the gradient and Hessian of the Bernoulli negative log-likelihood. Use the Hessian to show the objective is convex, then explain why the score equation is nevertheless nonlinear in $\beta$ and has no closed form. Contrast this with putting squared error around a sigmoid: what happens to convexity and saturated gradients, and why is cross-entropy the principled loss?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Convexity.** Log-likelihood $\ell(\beta)=\sum_i[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)]$ with $p_i=\sigma(x_i^\top\beta)$. Using $\partial p_i/\partial\beta = p_i(1-p_i)x_i$:

$$\nabla\ell = \sum_i (y_i-p_i)x_i = X^\top(y-p), \qquad \nabla^2\ell = -\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)x_ix_i^\top = -X^\top W X$$

with $W=\text{diag}(p_i(1-p_i))\succeq 0$. So $\nabla^2\ell = -X^\top W X \preceq 0$ — the log-likelihood is **concave**, hence the **NLL is convex**. No bad local minima; Newton/IRLS finds the global optimum.

**Sigmoid + MSE is non-convex.** With $\mathcal{L}=\sum_i(y_i-\sigma(x_i^\top\beta))^2$, the Hessian involves $\sigma''$, which changes sign, so $\mathcal{L}$ is not convex in $\beta$ — multiple local minima even in 1-D. Plus it suffers vanishing gradients on saturated, confidently-wrong examples (c1.3). **Use cross-entropy** (the Bernoulli NLL), which is convex and has the clean $X^\top(y-p)$ gradient.

**No closed form.** Setting $\nabla\ell=X^\top(y-p)=0$ gives an equation that is **nonlinear in $\beta$** because $p=\sigma(X\beta)$ — you cannot isolate $\beta$ algebraically. So you iterate (Newton–Raphson = IRLS, a sequence of weighted least-squares fits). Contrast OLS, where setting the gradient to zero gives the *linear* normal equations and thus a closed form.

**Rubric:**
- Gradient $X^\top(y-p)$, Hessian $-X^\top W X$, $W=\text{diag}(p_i(1-p_i))\succeq0$
- Conclude log-lik concave → NLL convex → global optimum
- Sigmoid+MSE non-convex (sign-changing $\sigma''$) + saturation; use cross-entropy
- No closed form: score equation nonlinear in $\beta$ (vs OLS's linear normal equations) → IRLS

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q9. Why can perfectly classified data break logistic regression?** Suppose one hyperplane separates every training example correctly. Scale that separating coefficient vector larger and larger, and track the predicted probabilities and likelihood. Use this thought experiment to explain why the unregularized MLE is not finite, why coefficients and standard errors explode, and why an optimizer may never converge. Give two fixes and explain what each changes.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**What happens.** If some hyperplane perfectly separates the two classes, take the separating direction $\beta^\*$ and scale it: $\beta=c\beta^\*$. As $c\to\infty$, every fitted probability $p_i=\sigma(x_i^\top\beta)\to$ its correct 0 or 1, so each likelihood term $\to 1$ and the log-likelihood $\to 0$ — its **supremum**, which is **never attained at any finite $\beta$**. Therefore:
- **Coefficients diverge to $\pm\infty$** (at least along the separating direction).
- **The likelihood has no maximizer** — the **MLE does not exist** (Albert & Anderson, *Biometrika* 1984; this is the "complete separation" case, with an analogous "quasi-complete" case).
- **Standard errors explode** — the Hessian $X^\top W X$ becomes near-singular as $p_i(1-p_i)\to0$ for all $i$, so $\text{Var}(\hat\beta)\to\infty$. Optimizers report non-convergence or absurd coefficients/SEs.

**Why it's counterintuitive.** It's *cleanly separable* data — the "easy" case — yet the unregularized fit blows up, precisely *because* it can keep improving the likelihood by becoming more confident without bound.

**Two fixes.** (1) **Regularization** — any penalty (ridge/L2 is standard; it's exactly why sklearn's penalized default doesn't diverge) caps the coefficient magnitude, restoring a finite optimum. (2) **Firth's penalized likelihood** (a Jeffreys-prior bias correction) guarantees finite estimates and is the standard remedy in statistics. (Also acceptable: drop/merge the separating feature, or use exact logistic regression.)

**Rubric:**
- Coefficients → ±∞; likelihood → its supremum but unattained → MLE does not exist
- SEs explode (Hessian → singular as $p(1-p)\to0$)
- Explain *why*: confidence can grow without bound to keep raising the likelihood
- Cite Albert–Anderson (complete/quasi separation)
- Two fixes: regularization (ridge) and Firth

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q10. The model can predict, but it cannot assign credit.** Two predictors each have $\operatorname{VIF}=50$. Translate that number into the fraction of each feature explained by the others and the corresponding standard-error multiplier. Then explain why individual coefficients may swing or flip sign while the combined prediction $X\hat\beta$ remains stable. End with the deployment condition under which collinearity can begin to hurt prediction too.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Definition.** $\text{VIF}_j=1/(1-R_j^2)$, where $R_j^2$ is the $R^2$ from regressing predictor $x_j$ on **all the other predictors**. $\text{VIF}=50 \Rightarrow R_j^2=1-1/50=0.98$ — the predictor is 98% explained by the others.

**(a) Standard errors.** $\text{Var}(\hat\beta_j)\propto\text{VIF}_j$, so $\text{SE}(\hat\beta_j)$ scales as $\sqrt{\text{VIF}_j}=\sqrt{50}\approx 7.1\times$ larger than under orthogonality. Confidence intervals balloon; $t$-stats shrink; truly-relevant predictors can look insignificant.

**(b) Stability and sign.** With nearly-redundant columns, the data barely constrains how the shared effect is *split* between them, so tiny perturbations (a few resampled rows, adding/removing a point) swing the coefficients wildly and can **flip their signs**. The individual coefficients are essentially un-identifiable.

**(c) Predictive accuracy.** Largely **unaffected**. Collinearity introduces **no bias**; the fitted values $\hat y=X\hat\beta$ and the model's predictions on $X$ drawn from the same distribution are fine — the instability is in how the (well-determined) total effect is *attributed* to the collinear features, not in the prediction itself.

**Why the split.** Prediction depends on the *combination* $X\hat\beta$, which is well-determined; inference depends on *individual* $\hat\beta_j$, whose variance is inflated by collinearity. So multicollinearity is a problem for **interpretation/inference, not (usually) prediction**. (A tree model doesn't even care — it just splits on one of the redundant features. Connects to c1.1: this redundancy is exactly what defeats drop-column leakage detection.)

**Rubric:**
- VIF $=1/(1-R_j^2)$; $R_j^2$ from regressing $x_j$ on the others; VIF 50 → $R_j^2=0.98$
- SEs scale $\sqrt{\text{VIF}}\approx7\times$
- Coefficients unstable / sign-flipping (effect-splitting un-identifiable)
- Prediction unaffected (no bias); $X\hat\beta$ well-determined
- Articulate inference-vs-prediction distinction

</details>

**Q11. Ridge through the geometry of the data cloud.** In the SVD $X=UDV^\top$, a small singular value marks a direction in feature space with little observed variation and therefore unstable estimation. Read the factor $d_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda)$ in that light: which directions does ridge trust, and which does it suppress? Compare this smooth weighting with PCR's keep-or-drop rule, then give one case where suppressing a low-variance direction helps and one where it destroys predictive signal.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Shrinkage factors.** Along principal direction $j$, OLS uses factor $1$; ridge uses $\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}\in(0,1)$. For **high-variance** directions ($d_j^2\gg\lambda$) the factor $\approx 1$ — barely shrunk. For **low-variance** directions ($d_j^2\ll\lambda$) the factor $\approx d_j^2/\lambda\approx 0$ — **shrunk hard**. So **ridge shrinks most along the low-variance principal directions** — which are exactly the ill-determined, collinear ones (small singular values ↔ near-dependencies). It leaves the well-determined high-variance directions almost untouched.

**PCR.** Principal-component regression keeps the top $m$ components *fully* (factor 1) and drops the rest *entirely* (factor 0). That's a **hard threshold** on the same principal directions.

**"Soft PCR."** Ridge replaces PCR's 0/1 cliff with the smooth ramp $d_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda)$ — a continuous down-weighting of low-variance directions rather than an abrupt cut. Same idea (suppress the unstable directions), softened.

**When it helps.** When the low-variance directions carry mostly *noise* (estimation variance) rather than signal — the usual case under collinearity — shrinking them trades a little bias for a large variance reduction and lowers MSE. It **hurts** if the target actually depends on a low-variance direction, since both ridge and (worse) PCR suppress exactly that direction — a manifestation of PCA/PCR being target-blind.

**Rubric:**
- Factor $d_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda)$: ≈1 for large $d_j$, ≈0 for small $d_j$ → shrinks low-variance directions most
- Low-variance ↔ collinear/ill-determined directions
- PCR = hard threshold (keep top $m$, drop rest)
- Ridge = soft version of the same
- Helps when low-variance dirs are noise; hurts if signal lives there (target-blind)

</details>

**Q12. Same model name, different statistical job.** You fit scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression()` and statsmodels' `Logit().fit()` on the same matrix and get different coefficients; only statsmodels reports standard errors and $p$-values. Explain the different defaults and goals behind the two libraries, including intercept handling and regularization. Then state the scikit-learn 1.8 API for an unregularized fit and for requesting L1 behavior.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Different coefficients.** scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression` is **L2-regularized by default** (`C=1.0`, where `C` is the *inverse* regularization strength) — it returns a penalized (shrunk) point estimate, *not* the textbook MLE. statsmodels' `Logit().fit()` is **unregularized maximum likelihood** by default. So the two optimize different objectives and land on different $\hat\beta$ (sklearn's are shrunk toward 0). This is the single most commonly-missed fact about sklearn logistic regression: out of the box it is not "plain" logistic regression.

**No $p$-values from sklearn.** sklearn is a *prediction* library — it returns a point estimate and **no inferential statistics** (no standard errors, $t$/$z$-stats, $p$-values, or confidence intervals). statsmodels is built for *inference* and reports the full table from the asymptotic sampling distribution of the MLE. (Also: statsmodels does **not** add an intercept — you must `sm.add_constant(X)` — whereas sklearn adds one via `fit_intercept=True`.)

**Reproduce unregularized in sklearn 1.8.** Set `C=np.inf` (effectively no penalty). In **scikit-learn 1.8**, the `penalty` parameter was **deprecated in favor of `l1_ratio`** (removal slated for 1.10): `penalty='l2'` → `l1_ratio=0` (the new default), `penalty='l1'` → **`l1_ratio=1`**, `penalty='elasticnet'` → `0<l1_ratio<1`, and `penalty=None` → `C=np.inf`. (On scikit-learn ≤1.7, `penalty='none'`/`penalty='l1'` is still the live API — so check the installed version.) For pure-L1 you also need a compatible solver (`saga`, or `liblinear`).

**Rubric:**
- sklearn L2-penalized by default (`C=1.0`, inverse strength) vs statsmodels unregularized MLE → different coefficients
- sklearn returns no inference (no SEs/$p$-values); statsmodels does; statsmodels needs `add_constant`
- Unregularized in sklearn: `C=np.inf`
- sklearn 1.8: `penalty` deprecated → `l1_ratio`; `penalty='l1'` → `l1_ratio=1`
- Bonus: note ≤1.7 still uses `penalty=`; L1 needs saga/liblinear

**Q13 · must-know. Noise in $y$ is harmless; noise in $x$ is not.** The true model is $y=\beta x+\epsilon$.

**(a)** You observe $y^{obs}=y+u$ rather than $y$, where $u$ is mean-zero with $\mathbb E[u\mid x]=0$ and uncorrelated with $\epsilon$. Say what happens to the OLS slope and, separately, to its standard error — and be explicit about which of those two assumptions does which job.

**(b)** Now the response is clean but the predictor is not: you observe $x^{obs}=x+u$, with $u$ mean-zero and independent of both $x$ and $\epsilon$. Derive $\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta$ from $\operatorname{Cov}(x^{obs},y)\big/\operatorname{Var}(x^{obs})$. Explain in one sentence of geometry why the answer comes out the way it does, and name precisely which OLS assumption (a) preserves and (b) destroys.

**(c)** In practice $x$ is latent and $\sigma_u^2$ is not handed to you. Design an estimator of the attenuation factor from observable data alone.

**(d)** Instantiate (c) for a trading signal $s^{obs}_{i,t}=s^{true}_{i,t}+u_{i,t}$ regressed on future returns, where the true signal is unobservable — concretely, a news-sentiment alpha.

**(e)** Does collecting more data fix any of this?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**(a) Noise in the response is absorbed by the error term.** Substitute $y^{obs}=y+u$ into the true model:

$$y^{obs}=\beta x+\underbrace{(\epsilon+u)}_{\textstyle \epsilon'}$$

The measurement noise merges into a new error term $\epsilon'$. Exogeneity survives: $\mathbb E[\epsilon'\mid x]=\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x]+\mathbb E[u\mid x]=0$. Nothing on the *right-hand side* moved, so the estimator is untouched in expectation — $\hat\beta$ stays **unbiased and consistent**.

Note which assumption bought which result: **$\mathbb E[u\mid x]=0$ alone delivers the unbiasedness** — it is all that exogeneity of $\epsilon'$ needs. The second assumption, $\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon,u)=0$, buys nothing for the slope; it only makes the variance decompose cleanly in the next step. A candidate who bundles them has not separated estimation from precision.

What you pay is precision. With the noises uncorrelated, $\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon')=\sigma_\epsilon^2+\sigma_u^2$, and since

$$\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta)=\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon'}}{\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2}}$$

the standard error **inflates**, $t$-statistics shrink, confidence intervals widen, and $R^2$ falls. The regression table gets quieter but stays honest — the same trade you get from adding an irrelevant regressor.

**(b) Noise in the predictor lands in the regressor *and* the error, and the shared $u$ couples them.** Compute the two pieces of the population slope. The numerator, using independence of $u$ from both $x$ and $\epsilon$:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(x^{obs},y)=\operatorname{Cov}(x+u,\ \beta x+\epsilon)=\beta\underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}(x,x)}_{\sigma_x^2}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}(x,\epsilon)}_{0}+\beta\underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}(u,x)}_{0}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}(u,\epsilon)}_{0}=\beta\sigma_x^2$$

The denominator, again by independence:

$$\operatorname{Var}(x^{obs})=\operatorname{Var}(x+u)=\sigma_x^2+\sigma_u^2$$

Hence

$$\boxed{\ \operatorname{plim}\hat\beta=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x^{obs},y)}{\operatorname{Var}(x^{obs})}=\beta\,\frac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_x^2+\sigma_u^2}\ }$$

**Why this is attenuation *toward zero*.** The multiplier $\lambda=\sigma_x^2/(\sigma_x^2+\sigma_u^2)$ lies in $(0,1]$, so $|\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta|<|\beta|$ while the **sign is preserved**. The limits are the sanity check: $\sigma_u^2\to0$ gives $\lambda\to1$ (recover $\beta$); $\sigma_u^2\to\infty$ gives $\lambda\to0$ — the estimate collapses to *zero specifically*, not to some other wrong number. Concretely, noise variance equal to signal variance ($\sigma_u^2=\sigma_x^2$) means $\lambda=\tfrac12$: you measure **half** the true effect, no matter how much data you have.

**The geometry, in one sentence:**

> Measurement noise increases horizontal variation in the observed predictor without increasing its covariance with $y$, so the fitted relationship gets diluted.

The numerator of the slope is untouched at $\beta\sigma_x^2$ while the denominator grows by $\sigma_u^2$ — you are spreading the same rise over a wider run, and the line flattens.

**Which assumption breaks.** Rewrite the *feasible* regression in terms of what you actually run. Since $x=x^{obs}-u$,

$$y=\beta x+\epsilon=\beta x^{obs}+\underbrace{(\epsilon-\beta u)}_{\textstyle \nu}, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(x^{obs},\nu)=\operatorname{Cov}(x+u,\ \epsilon-\beta u)=-\beta\sigma_u^2\neq0$$

That is the whole asymmetry, and it is the sentence to say out loud in an interview: **in (a) the noise appears only in the error term, which was already assumed orthogonal to the regressor; in (b) the same $u$ appears in the regressor *and* in the error, so it manufactures a correlation between them.** (a) preserves exogeneity; (b) destroys it — this is textbook **endogeneity**, and OLS is not merely biased but *inconsistent*.

**(c) Two independent measurements of the same latent quantity identify the signal variance.** The trick is that you never need $x$ itself — you need two noisy looks at it. Suppose

$$x^{(1)}=x+u_1,\qquad x^{(2)}=x+u_2$$

with $u_1,u_2$ mean-zero, independent of each other and of $x$. Then every cross term vanishes:

$$\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\right)=\operatorname{Var}(x)+\underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}(x,u_2)}_{0}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}(u_1,x)}_{0}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}(u_1,u_2)}_{0}=\sigma_x^2$$

**The covariance between independent replicates isolates the persistent signal variance; their disagreement is the measurement noise.** Since $\operatorname{Var}(x^{(1)})=\sigma_x^2+\sigma_{u_1}^2$ is directly observable, the **reliability ratio** is estimable with no knowledge of the latent $x$:

$$\lambda=\frac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_x^2+\sigma_u^2}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\right)}{\operatorname{Var}\!\left(x^{(1)}\right)},\qquad \beta_{\text{naive}}=\lambda\beta \implies \hat\beta_{\text{corrected}}=\frac{\hat\beta_{\text{naive}}}{\hat\lambda}$$

When the two replicates have equal noise variance, $\lambda$ is simply $\operatorname{Corr}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})$ — the classical test–retest reliability.

**The cleaner equivalent: use one replicate as an instrument for the other.** $x^{(2)}$ is correlated with $x^{(1)}$ (they share $x$) but uncorrelated with the equation error (since $u_2\perp\epsilon,u_1$), which is exactly the definition of a valid instrument. The IV estimator lands on $\beta$ with no correction factor at all:

$$\hat\beta_{IV}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(x^{(2)},y\right)}{\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(x^{(2)},x^{(1)}\right)}=\frac{\beta\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_x^2}=\beta$$

Other routes to the same missing information: a **validation subsample** where the true $x$ is recorded; a **known $\sigma_u^2$** from instrument specifications (then divide by $\hat\lambda$ directly, or run SIMEX); a **known error-variance ratio** between the two axes (Deming / total-least-squares regression — the [orthogonal-regression case in the lesson](/c/c1/c1.4)); or **averaging $K$ replicates**, which shrinks the noise to $\sigma_u^2/K$ and pushes $\lambda\to1$ — a partial fix that never quite arrives.

**(d) The quant version: split the evidence, not the sample.** You regress forward returns on $s^{obs}_{i,t}=s^{true}_{i,t}+u_{i,t}$ and get an attenuated loading — your measured IC understates the true relationship by the factor $\lambda$. Because $s^{true}$ is unobservable, manufacture the replicates by splitting the *raw evidence* the signal is computed from:

1. For each stock-date, **randomly split that day's news articles into two disjoint groups**.
2. Compute the sentiment score independently on each group, giving $s^{(1)}=s+u_1$ and $s^{(2)}=s+u_2$ — same latent sentiment, independent article-sampling noise.
3. Across many stock-dates, $\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(s^{(1)},s^{(2)}\right)=\operatorname{Var}(s^{true})$: the **persistent cross-replicate covariance is the real signal**, and everything by which the halves disagree is noise.
4. Compare that against $\operatorname{Var}\!\left(s^{(1)}\right)$ to get reliability, then de-attenuate.

Two details that separate a strong answer:

- **Half-sample correction.** Each half is built from half the articles, so it carries roughly *twice* the noise of the production signal. The half-sample reliability therefore understates the real one; correct with Spearman–Brown, $\lambda_{\text{full}}=2\lambda_{\text{half}}/(1+\lambda_{\text{half}})$.
- **Independence is the load-bearing assumption, and it is the one that fails.** If both halves inherit a common upstream source — the same wire story reprinted by ten outlets, two vendors both reselling the same feed — then $\operatorname{Cov}(u_1,u_2)\neq0$, the covariance picks up shared noise, and you will conclude your signal is *cleaner* than it is. Also compute the covariance in the units you trade (cross-sectionally demeaned per date), or a common market-wide drift masquerades as signal.

**Other market analogues of the same construction:** two independent data vendors scoring the same fundamental; two model estimates trained on disjoint subsamples or different seeds; splitting the trade tape / order book into odd–even trades or two disjoint sampling grids; multiple analysts' estimates of the same earnings number.

**Challenge the standard story — attenuation is an *inference* problem, not automatically a *prediction* problem.** If $s^{obs}$ is the signal you will actually have at trade time, then the attenuated coefficient is the *correct* one: OLS gives the best linear predictor of returns given the noisy input you will really observe, and de-attenuating it would systematically over-bet. The bias matters when you want the structural $\beta$ (how much true sentiment moves returns), when you are **ranking signals of unequal data quality** against each other, or when you are pricing whether better data is worth buying. Conflating those two questions is the standard mistake.

**(e) No — and this is the point of the question.**

$$\boxed{\text{More observations do not eliminate classical errors-in-variables bias.}}$$

As $n\to\infty$, $\hat\beta$ converges to $\lambda\beta$, not to $\beta$. The estimator is **inconsistent**, not merely noisy, so more data buys you an ever-tighter confidence interval centred on the wrong number — eventually one that excludes the truth with arbitrarily high confidence. Nothing in the regression output flags it: the residuals look fine, the $t$-statistic is large, the diagnostics pass. Fixing it requires **additional information or assumptions about the measurement process** — a replicate, an instrument, a validation sample, or a known noise variance — and no amount of the same mismeasured data supplies that.

**Bonus (the multivariate escalation).** The clean $\lambda$ formula is a *simple*-regression result. With several regressors and classical error in $x_1$ only, the attenuation is governed by the **residual** variance of $x_1$ after projecting out the other predictors, $\sigma_{r_1}^2/(\sigma_{r_1}^2+\sigma_u^2)$ — so collinearity makes the attenuation strictly **worse**, because it shrinks the numerator. Worse still, the *correctly measured* coefficients also become inconsistent, with a sign that depends on the correlation structure and can go either way. Once two or more regressors are mismeasured, there is no attenuation guarantee at all.

**Rubric:**
- (a) $y^{obs}$ folds into $\epsilon'=\epsilon+u$; exogeneity intact → $\hat\beta$ unbiased *and* consistent; only $\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon')=\sigma_\epsilon^2+\sigma_u^2$ grows → SEs inflate, $t$ shrinks, $R^2$ falls
- (a) Separates the two assumptions: $\mathbb E[u\mid x]=0$ buys unbiasedness; $\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon,u)=0$ only buys the clean variance decomposition
- (b) Derives $\operatorname{Cov}(x+u,y)=\beta\sigma_x^2$ and $\operatorname{Var}(x+u)=\sigma_x^2+\sigma_u^2$ with every cross term justified by independence
- (b) Lands $\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta=\beta\sigma_x^2/(\sigma_x^2+\sigma_u^2)$; identifies $\lambda\in(0,1]$ ⇒ shrunk toward zero, sign preserved; checks both limits
- (b) Geometric statement: noise adds horizontal spread without adding covariance → denominator grows, numerator does not → line flattens
- (b) States the asymmetry as the mechanism: in (a) $u$ enters only the error; in (b) $u$ enters regressor *and* error, giving $\operatorname{Cov}(x^{obs},\nu)=-\beta\sigma_u^2\neq0$ → endogeneity → inconsistency
- (c) $\operatorname{Cov}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})=\sigma_x^2$ from independent replicates; reliability $\hat\lambda=\operatorname{Cov}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})/\operatorname{Var}(x^{(1)})$; de-attenuate via $\hat\beta_{\text{naive}}/\hat\lambda$
- (c) Bonus: recognizes one replicate is a valid **instrument** for the other and that IV hits $\beta$ exactly; names validation subsample / known $\sigma_u^2$ / Deming as alternatives
- (d) Proposes splitting the raw evidence (articles per stock-date) into two independent groups; cross-replicate covariance = signal variance, disagreement = noise
- (d) Bonus: Spearman–Brown half-sample correction; flags shared-upstream-source violation of independence; cross-sectional demeaning; names other replicate constructions (vendors, seeds, tape splits, analysts)
- (d) Separates inference from prediction: the attenuated slope is *optimal* for trading the noisy signal; the bias bites for structural estimates and cross-signal comparison
- (e) States inconsistency explicitly — $n\to\infty$ tightens around $\lambda\beta$; no diagnostic reveals it; the fix requires outside information, not more rows
- Bonus: multivariate case — attenuation set by the *residualized* variance (worse under collinearity), clean coefficients contaminated with indeterminate sign

</details>

**Q14 · must-know. How would you actually compute the eigenvalues?** You have said PCA is an eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix $C=\frac1nX^\top X$. The interviewer asks how that gets computed.

**(a)** Explain why "solve $\det(C-\lambda I)=0$" is the wrong answer, on two separate counts. Then say which direction the relationship between polynomial roots and eigenvalues actually runs in numerical software.

**(b)** Start from $\max_{\lVert v\rVert=1}v^\top Cv$. Take any $q_0$, expand it in the eigenbasis, and multiply by $C$ repeatedly. Derive what $C^tq_0/\lVert C^tq_0\rVert$ converges to and under exactly which two conditions. Give the convergence rate, then show how you recover the eigenvalue once you have the vector.

**(c)** You want the top $k$ components, so you run (b) from $k$ different random starting vectors. What goes wrong, and what is the minimal fix? Name the resulting algorithm and say precisely what the QR step is doing.

**(d)** Now the full spectrum. Factor $A_t=Q_tR_t$ and re-multiply in the reverse order, $A_{t+1}=R_tQ_t$. Show in two lines why this leaves the eigenvalues unchanged, and say what a symmetric $A_t$ converges to and where the eigenvectors come from. Then: one dense QR step costs $O(d^3)$, so why is this algorithm not hopeless? Name the preprocessing step, the reflection that performs it, and where the total cost actually lands.

**(e)** You have $d=10{,}000$ features and want $k=50$ components. Say why you would not run (d) at all, name what you would run instead, and explain why the whole computation needs only $Xv$ and $X^\top u$ products. Give two separate reasons — one about memory, one about numerical accuracy — for running SVD on $X$ rather than eigendecomposing $X^\top X$.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**(a) Two independent objections.**

*No closed form.* By Abel–Ruffini there is no radical formula for polynomial roots past degree 4, so for $d>4$ you would need an iterative root-finder anyway. You have not escaped iteration; you have moved it somewhere worse.

*Conditioning.* Root-finding from polynomial coefficients is badly conditioned — tiny perturbations in the coefficients move the roots enormously (Wilkinson's polynomial is the standard demonstration). Forming the characteristic polynomial discards the structure of the matrix and converts a well-conditioned symmetric eigenproblem into an ill-conditioned root problem. You would be throwing away the very property that makes the problem easy.

*The direction reverses.* Software goes the other way. To find the roots of a polynomial you build its **companion matrix** and compute *its* eigenvalues — that is what `numpy.roots` does internally. Eigenvalue algorithms are the primitive; polynomial root-finding is the client.

**(b) Power iteration.** $C$ is symmetric, so it has an orthonormal eigenbasis $C=V\Lambda V^\top$ with $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_d\ge0$. Write $q_0=\sum_jc_jv_j$. Each multiplication scales each eigenvector by its own eigenvalue:

$$C^tq_0=\sum_jc_j\lambda_j^tv_j=\lambda_1^t\left[c_1v_1+\sum_{j\ge2}c_j\left(\frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_1}\right)^tv_j\right]$$

If $\lambda_1>\lambda_2$ every ratio $(\lambda_j/\lambda_1)^t\to0$, so the normalized iterate $\to\pm v_1$.

*The two conditions.* (i) A **strict gap** $\lambda_1>\lambda_2$ — with a repeated top eigenvalue the iteration converges to *some* vector in the top eigenspace, not a unique direction. (ii) $c_1\neq0$ — the start must have a component along $v_1$. In practice a random start satisfies this with probability 1, and rounding noise reintroduces the component even if you started orthogonal to it.

*Rate.* Linear, with ratio $\lambda_2/\lambda_1$: the error falls by that factor each step. Close leading eigenvalues means slow convergence, which is the honest weakness of the method.

*Eigenvalue.* Once $q\approx v_1$, $Cq\approx\lambda_1q$; left-multiply by $q^\top$ and use $\lVert q\rVert=1$:

$$q^\top Cq\approx\lambda_1q^\top q=\lambda_1$$

the **Rayleigh quotient**. Worth adding: it is accurate to *second* order — an $O(\epsilon)$ error in the eigenvector gives only $O(\epsilon^2)$ error in the eigenvalue, because the Rayleigh quotient is stationary at an eigenvector. The eigenvalue is always better converged than the vector.

**(c) They all collapse onto $v_1$.** Independent starts do not help: every start has a $v_1$ component, and $v_1$ dominates all of them equally. You get $k$ noisy copies of the same direction.

*Minimal fix.* Iterate them as a block and re-orthogonalize every step. With $Q$ holding $k$ orthonormal columns:

$$Z_t=CQ_t,\qquad Q_{t+1}=\operatorname{orth}(Z_t)\ \text{ via }\ Z_t=QR,\ \text{keep }Q,\ \text{discard }R$$

This is **orthogonal iteration** (simultaneous power iteration). The QR step is doing the anti-collapse work: multiplication by $C$ pushes every column toward $v_1$, and orthogonalization projects out the directions already claimed by earlier columns, so column $j$ retains the leading direction *not* spanned by columns $1..j-1$. The span converges to $\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_k)$ at a rate set by $\lambda_{k+1}/\lambda_k$ — the gap at the *edge* of the block you kept.

**(d) The QR algorithm.** $A_t=Q_tR_t$ with $Q_t$ orthogonal gives $R_t=Q_t^\top A_t$, hence

$$A_{t+1}=R_tQ_t=Q_t^\top A_tQ_t$$

a similarity transform. It preserves eigenvalues: if $Av=\lambda v$ and $B=Q^\top AQ$, set $w=Q^\top v$; then $Bw=Q^\top AQQ^\top v=Q^\top Av=\lambda Q^\top v=\lambda w$.

For symmetric $A_t$ the iterates converge to $\Lambda$ — eigenvalues on the diagonal. The eigenvectors are the **accumulated rotations** $\bar Q_t=Q_0Q_1\cdots Q_t\to V$; you get them by carrying the product along, not by a second pass. (Non-symmetric matrices converge instead to real Schur form, quasi-upper-triangular with $2\times2$ blocks for complex-conjugate pairs.)

*Why it is not hopeless.* Reduce before you iterate. Householder reflections $H=I-2vv^\top$ applied on both sides, $C\to HCH$ — still a similarity since $H$ is symmetric and orthogonal — drive $C$ to **tridiagonal** form (Hessenberg for a general matrix). Symmetry means clearing below the subdiagonal simultaneously clears above the superdiagonal.

The cost then lands as: a one-time $O(d^3)$ for the reduction, after which each QR step on a tridiagonal matrix is $O(d)$ instead of $O(d^3)$. With shifts (Wilkinson) and deflation the iteration takes $O(1)$ steps per eigenvalue. So the total is $O(d^3)$ **dominated by the reduction, not the iteration** — which is the whole reason "reduce, then iterate" is the standard shape of a symmetric eigensolver.

**(e) You want 50 of 10,000, so do not compute 10,000.** Running (d) means producing $9{,}950$ eigenvectors to discard them.

*What to run.* A Krylov method — **Lanczos** for the symmetric case (`scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh`, ARPACK underneath), or **randomized SVD** (`PCA(svd_solver="randomized")` in scikit-learn). Power iteration already showed that $q,Cq,C^2q,\ldots$ carries increasing information about the dominant eigenvectors, so keep the whole sequence: build an orthonormal basis $Q_m$ of $\mathcal K_m(C,q)=\operatorname{span}\lbrace q,Cq,\ldots,C^{m-1}q\rbrace$, project

$$T_m=Q_m^\top CQ_m$$

which is only $m\times m$ (and tridiagonal when $C$ is symmetric), solve the small problem $T_my=\theta y$, and lift back with $v\approx Q_my$. Those are the Ritz pairs.

*Why only matrix-vector products.* Krylov methods never need $C$ itself, only its action:

$$Cv=\tfrac1nX^\top Xv=\tfrac1nX^\top(Xv)$$

so $v\to Xv\to X^\top(Xv)$, two passes over $X$ and no $d\times d$ matrix anywhere.

*Two reasons to SVD $X$ directly.* **Memory and compute:** $C$ is $d\times d=10^8$ entries you never have to store, and forming it costs $O(nd^2)$ you never have to spend. **Accuracy:** $\kappa(X^\top X)=\kappa(X)^2$. Squaring the condition number costs you roughly half your significant digits, and it destroys exactly the small singular values — which matters whenever the design is near-collinear. A matrix $X$ that is perfectly workable can produce an $X^\top X$ that is numerically singular.

*The bridge.* $X=UDV^\top\Rightarrow X^\top X=VD^2V^\top$, so the right singular vectors of $X$ *are* the eigenvectors of $C$, with $\lambda_j=d_j^2/n$. You get the same answer, computed on a better-conditioned object.

**Rubric:**
- (a) Both objections, kept separate: no closed form past degree 4, *and* root-finding is ill-conditioned — not just one of them
- (a) Knows the dependency runs the other way: companion matrix, `numpy.roots` calls an eigensolver
- (b) Expands $q_0$ in the eigenbasis and factors out $\lambda_1^t$ — the derivation, not just the name
- (b) Both conditions ($\lambda_1>\lambda_2$ strictly; $c_1\neq0$) and what breaks under a repeated top eigenvalue
- (b) Rate $\lambda_2/\lambda_1$; Rayleigh quotient for the eigenvalue; bonus for its second-order accuracy
- (c) Identifies that independent starts all collapse to $v_1$, and that the fix is blocking + re-orthogonalization
- (c) Says what QR is *for* (projecting out claimed directions), not merely that it appears; rate $\lambda_{k+1}/\lambda_k$
- (d) $R_t=Q_t^\top A_t\Rightarrow A_{t+1}=Q_t^\top A_tQ_t$, plus the one-line similarity proof
- (d) Eigenvectors come from the accumulated product $\bar Q_t$, not a second pass
- (d) Householder $\to$ tridiagonal as a one-time $O(d^3)$; per-step drops to $O(d)$; total dominated by the reduction
- (e) Names Lanczos/Krylov or randomized SVD and the project–solve–lift structure
- (e) $Cv=\frac1nX^\top(Xv)$ — only the action of $C$ is ever needed
- (e) Both reasons distinctly: memory/compute *and* $\kappa(X^\top X)=\kappa(X)^2$ losing half the digits
- (e) Connects $X=UDV^\top$ to $C=VD^2V^\top/n$

## Rapid-fire: 21 questions that actually get asked

These 21 questions don't depend on Q1–Q14 above, so you can study this section on its own. Each one should take 2 to 5 minutes to answer out loud at a whiteboard. Where there is math, it is there to check that you understand the idea. None of these are proof exercises.

1. **Regress both ways.** Derive the slope of $y$ on $x$ and the slope of $x$ on $y$ in terms of $\rho,\sigma_x,\sigma_y$, and show their product is $\rho^2$. Explain why the second is *not* the reciprocal of the first even though the algebra of a fitted line suggests it should be — one sentence on which distances each line minimizes. Quant version: you estimate a hedge ratio by regressing A on B, a colleague regresses B on A, and the numbers don't invert. Who is right?
2. **Which assumption buys what.** Map each Gauss–Markov assumption — linearity, strict exogeneity, homoskedasticity, no perfect collinearity — to precisely which conclusion dies when it, and only it, fails: the existence of $\hat\beta$, unbiasedness, the BLUE property, or valid standard errors. Which single failure makes $\hat\beta$ biased *and* inconsistent, and which merely wrecks inference while leaving $\hat\beta$ unbiased?
3. **Behind the regression table.** Where does the randomness in $\hat\beta$ actually come from? Substitute $y=X\beta+\varepsilon$ into the OLS formula to get $\hat\beta-\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon$ in two lines, then use $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta\mid X)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ to say what makes a standard error large or small. Then: why the reference distribution is $t_{n-p}$ and not the Normal — you standardize by an *estimated* $\sigma$ — say operationally what the $n-p$ degrees of freedom are, and why dividing RSS by $n$ under-estimates $\sigma^2$. At what sample size does the $t$-vs-$z$ distinction stop mattering in practice?
4. **FWL, and does big $n$ cure collinearity?** State the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell result — $\hat\beta_j$ equals the slope from regressing residualized $y$ on residualized $x_j$ — and use it to explain what a coefficient in a multiple regression actually measures. From $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j)=\sigma^2/\lVert\tilde x_j\rVert^2$, say in words where the $1/(1-R_j^2)$ inflation factor comes from: once you residualize $x_j$ against the features it correlates with, less variation is left to estimate from. No proof needed. Then argue from $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j)\approx\frac{\sigma^2}{n\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(x_j)}\operatorname{VIF}_j$ that at fixed VIF the standard errors still shrink at $1/\sqrt n$ — so when is $\operatorname{VIF}=50$ fatal, when is it irrelevant, and what does that do to the rule "VIF above 10 is serious"?
5. **Omitted-variable bias, exactly.** Omit $x_2$ from $y=\beta_1x_1+\beta_2x_2+\varepsilon$ and derive the bias of $\hat\beta_1$ in terms of $\beta_2$ and the auxiliary regression of $x_2$ on $x_1$. Use the formula to predict the *direction* of the bias in a concrete case, state the two conditions under which omission is harmless, and reconcile with the quant experience "my alpha vanished the moment I added an industry factor."
6. **The sign flipped when I added a control.** Your signal has a significantly *positive* coefficient on its own; you add one control and it turns significantly *negative*. Explain the mechanism — "multicollinearity" as a slogan is not an answer. Use the omitted-variable formula to say exactly what the direction of the flip tells you about $\beta_2$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(x_1,x_2)$. Which of the two regressions answers "should I trade this signal," and what is the aggregation-level version of the same reversal called?
7. **Measurement-error attenuation.** You observe $x^{obs}=x+u$ with $u$ independent of everything. Derive $\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta=\beta\cdot\sigma_x^2/(\sigma_x^2+\sigma_u^2)$, and give the geometric reading: noise adds horizontal spread without adding covariance, so the denominator grows while the numerator does not. Why is this *inconsistency* rather than extra noise — i.e. why does $n\to\infty$ not fix it, and why does no diagnostic in the regression output flag it? Last part: if $x^{obs}$ is the signal you will actually have at trade time, is the attenuated coefficient the wrong one?
8. **Leverage vs residual vs influence.** Define leverage $h_{ii}$, its bounds, and its average $p/n$. Construct (a) a point with an enormous residual but negligible influence on $\hat\beta$, and (b) a high-leverage point whose residual is tiny *precisely because* it dragged the fit to itself — and explain why residual-based outlier screening misses (b). How does Cook's distance combine the two?
9. **What a $p$-value is not.** Write the two-sided $p$ as a tail probability under $H_0$, then refute in one line each: that it is the probability the null is true, the probability the result is due to chance, a measure of effect size, and evidence of causality. Explain the asymmetry — why the calculation can quantify "unlucky under $H_0$" but never "probability $H_0$ is wrong." Then: two studies report an *identical* point estimate, one significant and one not. What differs, and why is "one replicated and one didn't" the wrong reading?
10. **When the standard errors lie.** Your residuals show volatility clustering and autocorrelation. Reconcile "the point estimate is fine" with "every $p$-value is wrong" — say which OLS outputs survive and which break. Name the fix — White/HC standard errors — and say in words what the sandwich does differently from $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$, and which of the two factors in it is the one carrying the heteroskedasticity. Since HC errors do nothing about serial correlation, say what Newey–West adds and the bandwidth trade-off it forces.
11. **Two ways a regression lies to you.** (a) Regress one trending non-stationary series on an unrelated one and you get a large $R^2$ and a huge $t$: explain the mechanism that manufactures the significance, how you would detect it, and what differencing or a cointegration test changes. (b) You test 200 candidate signals and 12 clear $|t|>2$: reconcile that with a 5% false-positive rate, describe how you would adjust the bar, and say what an out-of-sample $R^2$ near zero would have told you that the in-sample table did not. Both are the same underlying failure — name it.
12. **Scaling and the intercept.** Multiply one column by $1000$ and say what happens to its OLS coefficient, its $t$-statistic, and its ridge coefficient. Use the asymmetry to explain why you must standardize before penalized fitting but not before OLS — define equivariance while you do it. Why is the intercept conventionally left unpenalized, and what concretely goes wrong if you penalize it?
13. **Correlated predictors: ridge vs lasso vs elastic net.** You have two nearly identical features. Say what ridge does to their coefficients, what lasso does, and which of the two answers changes if you refit on a bootstrap resample. Why is that instability a problem when you have to explain which feature drives the signal, even if predictions are fine either way? State what elastic net adds and which of the two failure modes it fixes. When do you actually want the grouping behavior rather than the sparse one?
14. **Why L1 makes exact zeros and L2 does not.** Answer through gradients, not the diamond-and-circle picture: compare each penalty's gradient as $w\to0$ and say why one keeps a constant pull toward zero while the other's vanishes. Say why answering "L1 gives sparsity" alone fails the question. Then the practical follow-up: when lasso zeroes a coefficient, does that mean the feature is useless?
15. **From Bernoulli to cross-entropy.** Start from "$y\mid x$ is Bernoulli with parameter $p(x)$" plus the choice to make the **log-odds** linear, $\log\frac{p}{1-p}=w^\top x+b$. Invert to the sigmoid, write the log-likelihood over $n$ points, and show that maximizing it *is* minimizing binary cross-entropy. Then show what breaks if you wrap squared error around the sigmoid instead: the objective is no longer convex in $w$, and the gradient carries a $\sigma'(z)=\sigma(1-\sigma)$ factor that vanishes exactly when the model is confidently *wrong*.
16. **Gradient, Hessian, no closed form.** Write down $\nabla_w=X^\top(\sigma(Xw)-y)$ — you should be able to get there from the chain rule in a couple of lines, and notice it has the same shape as the OLS gradient. State the Hessian $H=X^\top\operatorname{diag}\!\left(p_i(1-p_i)\right)X$ and use $H\succeq0$ to say why the problem is convex and the optimum unique. Then explain why setting the score to zero yields a *transcendental* system with no closed form — unlike OLS, where the same step gives you $\hat\beta$ outright. When would you reach for IRLS/Newton versus first-order SGD, and roughly where does the crossover in $n$ and $p$ sit?
17. **Read a coefficient, then draw the boundary.** A fitted coefficient is $\hat\beta_j=0.7$. Interpret it exactly: why is "the probability increases by $0.7$" wrong twice over, and what is $e^{0.7}$ in plain words? Why does the effect of $x_j$ on the *probability* depend on where you sit on the curve while its effect on the *log-odds* does not? Then say why the decision boundary comes out linear in $x$ and where $p=0.5$ lands in terms of $w$ and $b$, and explain why $0.5$ is a business choice rather than a statistical one.
18. **Perfectly separated data breaks it.** Suppose one hyperplane classifies every training point correctly. Scale that coefficient vector up and track the predicted probabilities and the likelihood. Use it to explain why the unregularized MLE is not finite, why coefficients and standard errors explode, and why the optimizer may never converge. Give two fixes and state precisely what each one changes — then say how you would recognize this in a fitted model you did not train.
19. **Downsampling, calibration, and AUC.** An ads CTR model is trained on negatively-downsampled data. Explain why its probabilities are inflated, give the correction that undoes a downsampling rate $w$, and explain the deeper fact that makes the correction so clean: outcome-dependent sampling shifts *only the intercept* of a logistic regression, leaving the slopes consistent. Then say why an excellent ROC-AUC fails to certify the model is safe to bid with, and what you would measure instead.
20. **PCA: what you decompose, and what you scale.** Explain when running PCA on the covariance matrix versus the correlation matrix changes the components, and what it means that PCA is *not* scale-equivariant. Why is centering mandatory while scaling is a judgment call? Then say how the eigendecomposition of $X^\top X$ relates to the SVD $X=UDV^\top$ — which object holds the components, how singular values map to eigenvalues, and why libraries run the SVD instead. Finally: why can the top principal components be the wrong features for predicting $y$?
21. **How would you actually compute the eigenvalues?** (Asked directly in an interview.) Q20 says PCA is an eigendecomposition of $C=\frac1nX^\top X$. This one asks how that gets computed, and it is a chain — each step exists because the previous one broke. **Start:** why is "solve $\det(C-\lambda I)=0$" the wrong answer, on two separate counts, and which direction does the dependency between polynomial roots and eigenvalues actually run in software? **Power iteration:** expand any starting vector in the eigenbasis, multiply by $C$ repeatedly, factor out $\lambda_1^t$, and say what survives and under which two conditions. Give the convergence rate, and say how you get the eigenvalue once you have the vector. **Top $k$:** you run that from $k$ random starts to get $k$ components — what goes wrong, and what is the one-line fix? Say what the QR step is doing, not just that it is there. **Full spectrum:** factor $A_t=Q_tR_t$ and re-multiply the other way, $A_{t+1}=R_tQ_t$. Show in one line why the eigenvalues survive, say what a symmetric matrix converges to, and say where the eigenvectors come from. A dense QR step is $O(d^3)$ — why is that not fatal? Name the preprocessing step and the reflection that performs it. **In practice:** $d=10{,}000$ features, $k=50$ components. What do you actually run, why does it only ever need $Xv$ and $X^\top u$ products, and give two separate reasons — one about memory, one about numerical accuracy — for running SVD on $X$ rather than eigendecomposing $X^\top X$.
