**Q1.** Derive the bias-variance decomposition from $y=f(x)+\varepsilon$ over the randomness of both the training set $\mathcal D$ and the noise $\varepsilon$. State exactly which assumption kills each of the two cross terms, and why the identity fails for 0-1 loss.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

Let $\hat f=\hat f_{\mathcal D}$, $\bar f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[\hat f(x)]$, with $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0$, $\text{Var}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2$, $\varepsilon\perp\mathcal D$.

$$\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D,\varepsilon}[(y-\hat f)^2]=\mathbb{E}[(\varepsilon+(f-\hat f))^2]=\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2]+2\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]\,\mathbb{E}[f-\hat f]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[(f-\hat f)^2]$$

**First cross term** $2\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]\mathbb{E}[f-\hat f]=0$ because $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0$ and $\varepsilon\perp\mathcal D$ (so the expectation factorizes). That leaves $\sigma^2+\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[(f-\hat f)^2]$. Split the last term with $\pm\bar f$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[(f-\hat f)^2]=\underbrace{(f-\bar f)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[(\hat f-\bar f)^2]}_{\text{Var}}+2(f-\bar f)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[\bar f-\hat f]}_{=0}$$

**Second cross term** vanishes because $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[\hat f]=\bar f$ by definition. Result:

$$\mathbb{E}[(y-\hat f)^2]=\sigma^2_{\text{noise}}+\text{Bias}^2+\text{Var}$$

**0-1 loss.** The derivation relies on the algebra of squares (the cross terms). For 0-1 loss there's no such additive identity; the "variance" can be *good or bad* depending on whether it pushes you across the decision boundary (Domingos 2000 gives a unified but loss-dependent, non-additive version). So the clean $\sigma^2+\text{Bias}^2+\text{Var}$ split is a squared-loss fact.

**Rubric:**
- Expand $(\varepsilon+(f-\hat f))^2$; first cross term killed by $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0,\varepsilon\perp\mathcal D$
- Split with $\pm\bar f$; second cross term killed by $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[\hat f]=\bar f$
- Land on $\sigma^2+\text{Bias}^2+\text{Var}$
- Squared-loss only; 0-1 loss non-additive (Domingos 2000)

</details>

**Q2.** Sketch the learning curves (train and validation error vs training-set size) for a high-bias model and a high-variance model. From the shapes alone, decide for each whether (a) more data, (b) more capacity, (c) more regularization will help.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**High-bias (underfit).** Train and validation errors **converge to each other at a high floor** — both are high, the gap is small, and adding data flattens them quickly at that floor. The dominant term is $\text{Bias}^2$, a property of the model class. Fixes: **(b) more capacity** (a richer model / more features / less regularization). **(a) More data does NOT help** — the floor is fixed by the model, not the sample size. (c) More regularization would *hurt* (raises bias further).

**High-variance (overfit).** Train error is **low**, validation error is **much higher** — a persistent **gap** that *narrows* as data grows. The dominant term is $\text{Var}$. Fixes: **(a) more data** (shrinks the gap), and **(c) more regularization** (reduces variance). (b) More capacity would *worsen* it.

**Decision rule:** *converged-and-high* → bias → add capacity; *low-train, high-val gap* → variance → more data or regularization. (This is exactly the c1.9 §5 diagnosis.)

**Rubric:**
- High-bias: curves converge at a high floor (small gap)
- High-variance: low train, high val, persistent narrowing gap
- Bias → more capacity (not more data); variance → more data / regularization
- Map each of (a)/(b)/(c) correctly to each regime

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q3.** Prove that choosing the hyperparameter minimizing CV error gives an optimistically biased estimate of that model's true risk. Quantify the optimism for $m$ independent candidates each with fold-noise $\tau$, and explain precisely what nested CV is unbiased *for*.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

Write $\widehat{\text{CV}}(\lambda)=R(\lambda)+Z_\lambda$ with $\mathbb{E}[Z_\lambda]\approx0$, $\text{Var}(Z_\lambda)=\tau^2$. For a *single fixed* $\lambda$, $\widehat{\text{CV}}$ is ~unbiased. But you report the minimum over candidates. By Jensen (the min of random variables is $\le$ the min of their means):

$$\mathbb{E}\bigl[\min_\lambda\widehat{\text{CV}}(\lambda)\bigr]\le\min_\lambda\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\text{CV}}(\lambda)]=\min_\lambda R(\lambda)$$

So the reported minimum **systematically under-estimates** the true risk of the selected model — the "winner's curse." With $m$ roughly-independent candidates whose noise is $\approx\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2)$, the expected minimum sits about $\tau\sqrt{2\ln m}$ below the true best — so the optimism **grows with the number of configurations tried**. (This is the same statistical effect as tuning on the test set, c1.1 data-snooping; the more you search, the more you overfit the validation set.)

**Nested CV.** The *inner* CV loop selects $\lambda$; the *outer* loop's folds are never seen during selection, so the outer estimate is unbiased — but **for the risk of the entire *procedure that includes tuning***, not for one fixed model. That's the right quantity to report: "how well does my model-plus-tuning pipeline generalize."

**Rubric:**
- Single fixed $\lambda$ ~unbiased; min-over-candidates biased by Jensen
- $\mathbb{E}[\min\widehat{\text{CV}}]\le\min R$ → optimistic; optimism $\approx\tau\sqrt{2\ln m}$
- Same phenomenon as data-snooping; grows with #configs
- Nested CV unbiased for the *tuned procedure's* risk, not a fixed model

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q4.** Explain the bias-variance tradeoff *of the CV estimator itself*: why is LOO nearly unbiased yet high-variance? Reconcile with Kohavi's finding that 10-fold beats LOO for selection. For least-squares linear regression, show LOO is computable from a single fit.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Bias.** $\widehat{\text{CV}}_K$ trains each surrogate on $n(1-1/K)$ points — weaker than the final $n$-point model — so it **over-estimates** error (pessimistic bias), and the bias **grows as $K$ shrinks**. LOO trains on $n-1$ points ⇒ almost the full model ⇒ **nearly unbiased**.

**Variance.** LOO's $n$ training sets pairwise overlap in $n-2$ points, so the $n$ fold-errors are **highly positively correlated**. The variance of an average of correlated terms doesn't shrink toward 0 (it's floored by the average covariance), so $\widehat{\text{CV}}_{\text{LOO}}$ has **high variance**. Smaller $K$ → less overlap → less-correlated, lower-variance estimate.

**Reconciliation (Kohavi 1995).** For model *selection* you care about ranking candidates reliably, which favors a lower-variance estimator; LOO's near-zero bias doesn't compensate for its high variance (and its $n$ refits). 10-fold stratified CV hit the better bias-variance-of-the-estimator balance empirically — hence the $K=5$–$10$ default.

**OLS shortcut (PRESS).** For least-squares, the $i$-th LOO residual equals $e_i/(1-h_{ii})$ where $e_i$ is the full-fit residual and $h_{ii}$ the hat-matrix diagonal (leverage). So $\text{LOO-MSE}=\frac1n\sum_i\bigl(\frac{e_i}{1-h_{ii}}\bigr)^2$ — **computable from one fit**, no $n$ refits. So "LOO is expensive" is false *specifically* for linear models.

**Rubric:**
- Smaller $K$ → more pessimistic bias (smaller surrogate training sets); LOO ~unbiased
- LOO high variance because folds overlap $n-2$ → correlated errors → average doesn't concentrate
- Kohavi: 10-fold better for selection (variance + compute beat LOO's low bias)
- PRESS: LOO from one OLS fit via $e_i/(1-h_{ii})$

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q5.** Derive the "rule of 59": how many random hyperparameter configurations you need to land in the top-5% region with 95% probability. Explain why this bound is independent of the number of hyperparameters while grid coverage is not.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Derivation.** Suppose the top-$\beta$ fraction of configurations is "good enough." A single random draw misses the good region with probability $1-\beta$; $n$ i.i.d. draws all miss with probability $(1-\beta)^n$. To hit it with probability $\ge1-\gamma$:

$$(1-\beta)^n\le\gamma\;\Longrightarrow\;n\ge\frac{\log\gamma}{\log(1-\beta)}$$

For $\beta=0.05$ (top 5%), $\gamma=0.05$ (95% confidence): $n\ge\frac{\log0.05}{\log0.95}=\frac{-3.00}{-0.0513}\approx58.4\Rightarrow \boxed{n=59}$ (often quoted as "60 trials").

**Dimension independence.** The bound only involves $\beta$ and $\gamma$ — **not $d$**. Random search samples *every* hyperparameter at $n$ distinct values, so its coverage of the "good region" (defined by whatever few hyperparameters matter) doesn't degrade as you add irrelevant dimensions. Grid search, by contrast, probes only $v$ distinct values *per axis* at cost $v^d$; adding a dimension multiplies the cost by $v$ while still resolving each important axis at only $v$ points. So grid's coverage of the important axes is fixed at $v$ regardless of budget spent on irrelevant ones — random search adapts to the *effective* dimensionality for free (Bergstra & Bengio 2012).

**Rubric:**
- $(1-\beta)^n\le\gamma\Rightarrow n\ge\log\gamma/\log(1-\beta)$
- Plug in $\beta=\gamma=0.05\Rightarrow n\approx59$
- Bound has no $d$ → dimension-free
- Grid resolves only $v$ values/axis at cost $v^d$; random gives $n$ distinct values/axis → adapts to $d_{\text{eff}}$

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q6.** Construct a hyperparameter landscape where grid search with a generous $v^d$ budget systematically *misses* the optimum that random search finds. Quantify.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Construction.** Two hyperparameters, but only one matters: the response is $g(\theta_1,\theta_2)=h(\theta_1)$ with a sharp optimum at, say, $\theta_1^\star=0.37$, and $\theta_2$ irrelevant. Use a $5\times5$ grid ($v=5$, 25 trials) on $[0,1]^2$ with grid lines at $\{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$. The grid probes $\theta_1$ at only **5 distinct values**, none near 0.37 — so all 25 trials evaluate $h$ at the same 5 wrong $\theta_1$ values (each repeated 5× across the irrelevant $\theta_2$), and the optimum is missed regardless of budget.

**Random search**, with the *same* 25 trials, draws 25 distinct $\theta_1$ values uniformly — the chance that none lands within $\pm0.05$ of $0.37$ (a 0.1-wide good region, $\beta=0.1$) is $(1-0.1)^{25}\approx0.072$, so random finds the good region with ~93% probability. The grid's 25 trials gave 5 distinct $\theta_1$ values; random's gave 25 — a 5× resolution gain on the axis that matters, *for the same budget*.

**The general point:** grid wastes its budget resolving the irrelevant axis ($\theta_2$) at full resolution while under-resolving the important one. Whenever $d_{\text{eff}}<d$, this happens.

**Rubric:**
- Landscape with one relevant + one (or more) irrelevant hyperparameter, optimum off the grid lines
- Grid probes only $v$ distinct values on the relevant axis (repeated across irrelevant) → misses
- Random gives $n$ distinct values on every axis → finds it (quantify the hit probability)
- Generalize: $d_{\text{eff}}<d$ ⇒ grid wastes budget on irrelevant axes

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q7.** State the double-descent curve precisely. Explain the mechanism in the over-parameterized regime — why does effective variance *fall* as capacity grows past the interpolation threshold? Why does this not contradict the bias-variance decomposition?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The curve** (Belkin et al. 2019). As model capacity increases: test risk follows the classical U **up to the interpolation threshold** (the capacity at which the model first fits the training data exactly, roughly #params $=n$), where it **spikes**; then, as capacity grows *beyond* the threshold, test risk **descends a second time**, often below the classical U's minimum.

**Mechanism.** Past the threshold there are infinitely many parameter settings that interpolate (zero training error). The training procedure's **inductive bias selects the minimum-norm interpolant** (e.g. gradient descent / the pseudoinverse picks the smallest-norm solution). As capacity grows, the *norm* of that minimum-norm interpolant **keeps decreasing** (more directions to spread the fit over, less weight per direction), which is an **implicit ridge** — so the effective variance of the estimator *falls* even though training error is pinned at zero. At the threshold itself the variance spikes because $X^\top X$ is barely invertible (smallest singular value → 0).

**No contradiction.** The bias-variance decomposition $\sigma^2+\text{Bias}^2+\text{Var}$ is an *exact identity* — always true. Double descent is a statement about how the **variance term** behaves as a function of capacity: it isn't monotone increasing (as the classical story assumes); it rises to the interpolation threshold and then comes *back down* under the min-norm inductive bias. The decomposition holds; the assumed *shape* of its variance term was incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- Define interpolation threshold; U up to it, spike at it, second descent beyond
- Min-norm interpolant + its norm decreasing with capacity = implicit ridge → variance falls
- Variance spike at threshold ($X^\top X$ ill-conditioned)
- Decomposition still exact; it's the variance term's *shape* (non-monotone) that classical story got wrong

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q8.** Distinguish model-wise, epoch-wise, and sample-wise double descent (Nakkiran 2021). Define Effective Model Complexity (EMC), and construct a scenario where adding training data *increases* test error.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Effective Model Complexity (EMC).** The maximum number of training samples on which the training procedure achieves ≈ zero training error. It's a property of the *procedure* (architecture + optimizer + epochs), not just parameter count, so it unifies the three axes below.

**Three flavors** — test error peaks when EMC ≈ $n$ (the interpolation threshold), and descends on either side:
- **Model-wise:** hold data/epochs fixed, grow the model — error peaks at the size where it just interpolates, then descends.
- **Epoch-wise:** hold model/data fixed, train longer — more epochs raise EMC, so test error can rise then fall as training proceeds (a model that's "too small to interpolate early, large enough late").
- **Sample-wise:** hold model/epochs fixed, vary $n$ — error peaks when $n\approx$ EMC.

**More data hurts (sample-wise).** Take a model whose EMC is, say, 5000. With $n=3000$ it's comfortably over-parameterized (second-descent side, low error). **Increase $n$ to 5000** — now $n\approx$ EMC, the interpolation threshold, where variance spikes → **test error increases** even though you added data. Pushing further to $n=8000$ moves it back into the under-parameterized regime and error falls again. So near the threshold, more data can transiently *raise* test error — a direct violation of "more data always helps."

**Rubric:**
- EMC = max #samples the procedure can fit to ~0 train error; property of the whole procedure
- Model/epoch/sample-wise: all peak when EMC ≈ $n$
- Construct: model with EMC ≈ N; raising $n$ to ≈ EMC spikes error (then falls again)
- Conclude: more data can transiently hurt near the threshold

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q9.** For successive halving with budget $B$, $n$ initial configs, and elimination factor $\eta$, describe the rungs and the $n$-vs-$r$ tradeoff. Construct a case where successive halving eliminates the eventual winner early, and say what Hyperband does about it.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Successive halving.** Start $n$ configs at a small per-config budget $r$ (e.g. few epochs / small data). Evaluate, keep the top $1/\eta$, **multiply each survivor's budget by $\eta$**, repeat. After $\log_\eta(n)$ rungs you're left with one config that has run at the full budget. The total budget is spread so each rung costs roughly the same; $\eta\in\{3,4\}$ is typical.

**The $n$-vs-$r$ tradeoff.** With a fixed total budget $B$, you choose between **many configs at a small starting budget** (explore widely, but early performance is a noisy proxy for final performance) and **few configs at a large starting budget** (reliable early signal, but you explore less). There's no universally right choice — it depends on how predictive low-budget performance is of high-budget performance.

**SH eliminates the winner (construction).** Take a config whose learning curve is **non-monotone / slow-starting** — e.g. a high-capacity model with a low warm-up learning rate that looks worst at 1 epoch but is best at 50. At the first rung (1 epoch) it ranks in the bottom $1-1/\eta$ and is **cut**, even though it would have won at full budget. SH assumes low-budget rank ≈ high-budget rank, which this curve violates.

**Hyperband's fix.** Hyperband runs SH as an inner loop over a **grid of brackets** with different $(n,r)$ starting points — some brackets start with few configs at large budgets (which would *not* prematurely cut the slow-starter), others with many configs at small budgets. By hedging across the $n$-vs-$r$ tradeoff, Hyperband bounds the worst-case from any single SH bracket's wrong bet.

**Rubric:**
- SH: $n$ configs at budget $r$, keep top $1/\eta$, multiply survivors' budget by $\eta$, ~$\log_\eta n$ rungs
- $n$-vs-$r$ tradeoff: explore-many-cheap vs few-reliable; depends on low-budget predictiveness
- Construct a slow-starting / non-monotone curve that SH cuts early
- Hyperband runs SH over multiple $(n,r)$ brackets to hedge

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q10.** Write the AIC and BIC penalties. For which $n$ do they diverge? Explain the qualitative claim that AIC is asymptotically efficient but not consistent, while BIC is consistent. Which would you use to (a) pick a forecasting model, (b) recover the true sparse model?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Penalties.** $\text{AIC}=-2\hat\ell+2k$, $\text{BIC}=-2\hat\ell+k\ln n$ ($\hat\ell$ = maximized log-likelihood, $k$ = #parameters, $n$ = #observations). They penalize the *same* fit term differently: AIC's per-parameter penalty is the constant $2$; BIC's is $\ln n$. They **diverge once $\ln n>2$, i.e. $n>e^2\approx7.4$** — so for any realistic dataset BIC penalizes complexity harder, and increasingly so as $n$ grows.

**Consistency vs efficiency.**
- **BIC is consistent:** as $n\to\infty$, if the true model is among the candidates, BIC selects it with probability → 1. Its $k\ln n$ penalty grows fast enough to reject spurious extra parameters asymptotically.
- **AIC is efficient but not consistent:** it's derived to minimize predictive risk (KL divergence to the truth) and asymptotically achieves the best prediction error *as if the true model were unknown/infinite-dimensional*, but its constant $2k$ penalty leaves a positive probability of **over-selecting** (keeping junk parameters) even as $n\to\infty$.

**Which to use.** (a) **Forecasting / prediction → AIC** (it targets predictive risk; you don't care about identifying the "true" model, just predicting well). (b) **Recover the true sparse model → BIC** (consistency: it'll converge on the true parsimonious set). (In small samples, AICc — AIC with a finite-sample correction — is often preferred.)

**Rubric:**
- $\text{AIC}=-2\hat\ell+2k$, $\text{BIC}=-2\hat\ell+k\ln n$; diverge at $\ln n>2$ ($n>7.4$)
- BIC consistent (selects true model as $n\to\infty$); AIC efficient (min prediction risk) but over-selects
- (a) forecasting → AIC; (b) true sparse model → BIC

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q11.** Design a validation protocol for a model with leakage-prone, grouped, time-ordered data that *also* needs hyperparameter tuning. State what each loop does and what the final number estimates. What's the failure if you used plain shuffled k-fold?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Protocol — nested CV with group + temporal constraints.**
- **Outer loop (temporal):** forward-chaining splits — train on $[\,\text{start},t)$, test on $[t,t+\Delta)$, advancing $t$; never train on data later than the test window. Each outer test fold gives one honest performance number.
- **Inner loop (also temporal + grouped), on each outer training block:** forward-chaining CV to select hyperparameters, with **all rows of any entity (user/session) kept on one side** of every split (`GroupKFold`-style) so no entity straddles train/validation.
- After the inner loop picks $\lambda$, refit on the full outer-train block and score the untouched outer-test fold. **Report the mean ± std over outer folds** — this estimates the generalization of *the entire tune-and-fit procedure* on future, unseen entities (honoring both leakage constraints).

**Failure of plain shuffled k-fold.** Two leaks at once: (1) **look-ahead** — shuffling mixes future rows into training, so the model "sees the future" and the metric is optimistic (c1.1's churn-AUC example); (2) **group leakage** — the same entity's rows land in both train and validation, so the model memorizes entity identity and the metric reflects memorization, not generalization. Plus the tuning would be selection-biased without the outer loop. The reported number would be far above live performance.

**Rubric:**
- Nested CV: outer = honest estimate, inner = tuning
- Outer & inner both forward-chaining (temporal, no look-ahead) AND grouped (entity on one side)
- Final number = risk of the tuned procedure on future unseen entities
- Shuffled k-fold → look-ahead + group leakage + selection bias → optimistic metric

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q12.** A candidate (scikit-learn 1.9) hits three problems: (a) `from sklearn.model_selection import HalvingRandomSearchCV` raises `ImportError`; (b) they tune with `scoring='neg_log_loss'` and are confused that `best_score_` is negative; (c) they run the search on the *whole* dataset and report `best_score_` as the generalization estimate. Identify and fix all three.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**(a) Halving import.** `HalvingGridSearchCV`/`HalvingRandomSearchCV` are **experimental**, so they aren't importable until you enable the feature flag:

```python
from sklearn.experimental import enable_halving_search_cv  # noqa: F401
from sklearn.model_selection import HalvingRandomSearchCV
```

(The `enable_*` import must come first; this is the same pattern as other experimental sklearn estimators.)

**(b) Negative score.** scikit-learn follows a **higher-is-better** convention for all scorers, so loss functions are *negated*: `neg_log_loss` returns $-\log\text{loss}$. A `best_score_` of $-0.31$ just means a log-loss of $0.31$; pick the model with the **least negative** (largest) value. Nothing is wrong.

**(c) Generalization estimate.** `best_score_` is the **cross-validation score of the selected model on the data used to select it** — it's optimistically biased (selection bias / winner's curse, Q3). It is *not* an unbiased generalization estimate. Fix: hold out a separate test set that the search never touches and evaluate `best_estimator_` on it, **or** use **nested CV** (outer loop estimates the tuned procedure). Report *that* number, not `best_score_`.

**Rubric:**
- (a) `from sklearn.experimental import enable_halving_search_cv` before importing the halving estimator
- (b) sklearn negates losses (higher-is-better); negative `neg_log_loss` is expected; pick the largest
- (c) `best_score_` is selection-biased (not generalization); use a held-out test set or nested CV

</details>

</Quiz>

## Rapid-fire: bias, variance & evaluation statistics

Additional interview-calibrated questions — the ones that tend to separate candidates at top-tier ML interviews (frontier labs, FAANG, and quant funds, where this material opens the loop). No model answers on purpose: if you can't answer one cleanly, out loud, in ~2 minutes, that's the gap to close. Several bundle multiple sub-questions; a strong answer hits every part.

1. **"Tradeoff" is not a theorem.** The bias-variance *decomposition* is an identity; the bias-variance *tradeoff* is folklore. Construct a concrete change to a model or training procedure that lowers bias and variance *simultaneously* — and say precisely what extra ingredient (more data? a better inductive bias?) made the "trade" avoidable. What, then, does the tradeoff actually claim, and over what is it a statement?
2. **$k$ as the dial.** For $k$-NN regression at a fixed test point, start from $\hat f(x)=\frac1k\sum_{i\in N_k(x)} y_i$ and derive the bias and variance as explicit functions of $k$ (show $\text{Var}=\sigma^2/k$). Which regime is $k=1$ and which is $k=n$, where does the noise floor $\sigma^2$ enter the risk — and when is the *interpolating* $k=1$ rule nonetheless a reasonable choice?
3. **Bagging vs. boosting, by term.** Both are ensembles, but they attack different terms of the decomposition. Using the variance of an average of $n$ equicorrelated predictors, $\rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{n}\sigma^2$, derive what bagging can and cannot reduce — and explain why boosting's base learners are deliberately high-bias (shallow trees) while a random forest's are grown deep.
4. **Bessel, then betrayal.** The $n-1$ sample variance is unbiased; show that the MLE version with denominator $n$ has strictly lower MSE for Gaussian data (and denominator $n+1$ lower still). Reconcile: why does everyone still use $n-1$, and what does this say about "unbiased" as a model-selection criterion?
5. **When biased beats BLUE.** Under the Gauss–Markov assumptions OLS is the best linear *unbiased* estimator, yet ridge — biased — can dominate it in MSE. In the orthonormal-design case where $\hat\beta_j^{\text{ridge}}=\hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}/(1+\lambda)$, derive the ridge estimator's bias and variance and prove there always exists $\lambda>0$ with strictly lower MSE than OLS; give the data condition (collinear features, $p$ comparable to $n$, ill-conditioned $X^\top X$) under which you'd bet on the biased estimator — and connect to James–Stein: what's the dimension condition under which shrinking coordinate-wise-admissible estimators jointly becomes a strict win?
6. **In-sample $R^2$ lies.** Prove that adding any regressor — even pure noise — can never decrease in-sample $R^2$. Now you have 1,000 candidate features and 60 observations: what does unregularized OLS do in this regime, and what selection protocol survives it?
7. **Early stopping is ridge — and selection.** For a quadratic loss, analyze gradient descent in the eigenbasis of the Hessian and show that stopping at step $t$ acts as a spectral filter roughly equivalent to ridge with $\lambda\approx1/(\eta t)$ — which eigendirections get fit first, and what kind of error does stopping early protect you from? Then argue that early stopping is *also* hyperparameter search with the epoch count as the hyperparameter: what does reporting the validation curve's minimum overstate?
8. **Benign overfitting vs memorization.** An interpolating model fits every noisy label exactly — training error is zero *on the wrong labels* — yet can still generalize well. Reconcile this: what structural property of the estimator lets it absorb label noise locally without corrupting the global fit, and when does interpolation stop being benign (noise level, dimensionality, eigenvalue decay)?
9. **Train above validation.** Your training error comes out *higher* than your validation error — the gap runs the "wrong" way. Construct three distinct mechanisms that produce this (regularization like dropout active only at train time, training loss averaged over an epoch while validation is scored at the epoch's end, an unluckily easy validation split), and say which one you can confirm from the training logs alone, without retraining.
10. **Loss up, accuracy up.** Late in training, validation cross-entropy *worsens* while validation accuracy still improves. Construct the prediction pattern that produces this (growing overconfidence concentrated on a shrinking set of errors), and defend which curve you should early-stop and select on — for a ranker vs. for a calibrated risk model.
11. **Too good to be true.** Train *and* validation error are both near zero on a task you know is hard. Argue why this is more alarming than a large train-val gap, and rank the leakage mechanisms you'd hunt for first (duplicate rows across the split, target-derived features, one entity straddling both sides).
12. **Offline great, online flat.** Validation AUC is 0.87 but the A/B test shows nothing. Rank the explanations — training-serving skew, distribution shift between validation and live traffic, leakage into the validation set, offline-metric/business-metric mismatch — and give one test that isolates each.
13. **Sizing the microscope.** You want to claim model A beats model B by 0.3 accuracy points. Derive the test-set size at which the binomial standard error can resolve that gap — then explain why evaluating both models on the *same* test set (paired errors, McNemar-style) shrinks the required $n$.
14. **Fold scores aren't i.i.d.** You paired-t-test the ten fold scores of two models. Derive the variance of a mean of $K$ correlated fold errors and show it's floored by the average covariance — indeed no unbiased estimator of $\text{Var}(\widehat{\text{CV}}_K)$ exists (Bengio & Grandvalet 2004). What do overlapping training sets make the folds share, in which direction does the naive $\hat\sigma/\sqrt{K}$ error bar bias the p-value, and what do the Nadeau–Bengio corrected resampled t-test and Dietterich's 5×2cv each change?
15. **Threshold on which split?** You choose the decision threshold maximizing validation F1, then report test F1 at that threshold. Is the reported number honest? Contrast with tuning the threshold on the test set itself, and state exactly which reported quantities remain unbiased under each protocol.
16. **Shuffle the labels.** Defend target permutation as a validation tool: what should your CV score collapse to when labels are shuffled, and what failure class does a score that *doesn't* collapse reveal that ordinary CV can never catch?
17. **A hundred signals.** You test 100 candidate signals at $p<0.05$ and 7 pass. How many passes do you expect under the global null, what do Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg each control (FWER vs. FDR), and which is the right frame when you'll deploy a *portfolio* of the survivors?
18. **The 63.2% freebie.** Derive the large-$n$ probability that a given point is absent from a bootstrap sample. Why does that make a random forest's out-of-bag error nearly-free cross-validation, and relative to which $K$-fold estimate is OOB pessimistically biased?
19. **The .632 story.** Why is leave-one-out *bootstrap* error pessimistic (each surrogate trains on only ~63.2% of the distinct points), what two quantities does Efron's .632 estimator interpolate between, and what overfitting pathology forced the .632+ correction?
20. **Seeds are hyperparameters.** A paper reports its best run over five random seeds; you report the mean. Explain why max-over-seeds is optimism of exactly the winner's-curse kind, and derive how many seeds you'd need before the standard error of the mean supports a claimed 0.4-point improvement.

## Rapid-fire: honest validation, holdout reuse & backtest overfitting

Same format — no answers, ~2 minutes each. This set leans quant and frontier-lab: "backtest overfitting" is the finance name for everything this lesson derived, and quant-research loops (Two Sigma, Citadel, HRT, D.E. Shaw) lead with it — while the LLM-era versions (contamination, judge validation, µTransfer) are what the labs ask instead.

1. **The deployed model was never validated.** Standard practice: pick $\lambda$ by CV, retrain on 100% of the data, ship. The artifact you deployed has *never been evaluated*. State the assumption that connects the fold surrogates' performance to the final model's, and construct a situation — an unstable procedure, or non-monotone sample-size behavior near an interpolation threshold — where that assumption fails.
2. **One set, three jobs.** A single validation set drives early stopping, hyperparameter choice, and the final model comparison. Explain which of the three estimates degrades first and why, and design the cheapest split scheme that makes the final comparison honest again.
3. **One SE of what?** The one-standard-error rule picks the simplest model within one SE of the best CV score — but there is provably no unbiased estimator of K-fold CV's variance (Bengio & Grandvalet 2004). Reconcile: what SE do libraries actually compute, what correlation does it ignore, what is the rule *implicitly* assuming about the loss surface near the minimum and the cost of complexity — and construct a case where it selects a substantially worse model than the CV-minimizer.
4. **When random search loses.** Bergstra–Bengio makes random search the default. Construct the conditions under which grid search is genuinely preferable, and the conditions under which Bayesian optimization decisively beats both — then explain why BO's edge erodes under a noisy objective, high parallelism, or a nearly-flat response surface. What property of the problem is each method's bet?
5. **Fifty peeks.** During development you evaluated on the validation set about 50 times, keeping whatever looked better. What has that set become, statistically? Explain how the reusable holdout / Thresholdout (Dwork et al. 2015) restores guarantees under *adaptive* reuse, and why the static $\tau\sqrt{2\ln m}$ picture understates adaptive damage.
6. **Leaderboard shakeup.** Explain the Kaggle public-to-private reshuffle as an estimator phenomenon: once thousands of teams adaptively submit against the public split, what is the public score actually estimating, and why should you trust your own CV over the leaderboard when the two disagree? What does the Ladder algorithm's limited-precision score reporting bound that a raw public leaderboard doesn't?
7. **Labels that arrive late.** Fraud chargebacks land 30–90 days after the transaction, so last month's "validation labels" are censored. Design a validation scheme that doesn't wait a quarter, and state the direction of the bias that partially-matured labels induce in measured precision and PR-AUC.
8. **Stale by serving time.** In a feed where trends die within hours, any frozen holdout is stale before you deploy. What does prequential ("test-then-train") evaluation estimate, how does it interact with retraining cadence, and when does it beat a frozen holdout as a model-selection signal?
9. **Contaminated benchmark.** A base LLM scores suspiciously well on a public benchmark. Give three detection approaches (n-gram overlap against the pretraining corpus; a performance drop on paraphrased or freshly-written items; canary strings) and argue that contamination is exactly this chapter's leakage — at corpus scale, with a "test set" that has been public for years.
10. **Judge the judge.** You select checkpoints by LLM-as-judge score. The judge is a model too: design the validation-of-the-validator — how do you estimate judge-human agreement and on what sample, and what Goodhart failure appears once you *optimize* against the judge?
11. **Tune small, transfer up.** You can't afford hyperparameter search at full scale, so you tune at 1/100th scale. When does the argmin fail to transfer under standard parametrization, and what does µP/µTransfer (Yang et al. 2021) change so that the optimal learning rate becomes approximately stable across model width?
12. **Buy tokens or buy parameters?** Fixed training compute for an LLM: bigger model on fewer tokens, or smaller model on more? State what quantity the Chinchilla analysis (Hoffmann et al. 2022) fits its compute-optimal frontier on, why the earlier Kaplan-scaling recipe systematically over-sized models, and what changes once inference cost enters the objective.
13. **Purge and embargo.** Forward-chaining CV can *still* leak when the label is a horizon return: show how a label computed over $[t,\,t+h]$ contaminates a training fold that ends at $t$, and explain what López de Prado's *purging* (dropping training points whose label window overlaps the test window) and *embargo* (a buffer after each test window) each remove.
14. **One path isn't a distribution.** Walk-forward backtesting gives you a single path through history. What does combinatorial purged CV construct instead, and what does the resulting *distribution* of path-level Sharpe ratios let you estimate that one walk-forward number cannot (probability of backtest overfitting)?
15. **Deflate the Sharpe.** Your best variant backtests at Sharpe 1.8 — after 200 variants. The deflated Sharpe ratio (Bailey & López de Prado) treats that as the maximum of $m$ trials: name the inputs the correction needs beyond the Sharpe itself (effective number of trials, variance of Sharpes across trials, return skew and kurtosis, track length), and explain how you estimate the *effective* trial count when your 200 variants are highly correlated.
16. **Retrain more, earn less.** Annual retraining backtests at Sharpe 1.8; monthly retraining of the same signal backtests at 0.6. Diagnose: why can *more frequent* refitting hurt, and how would you distinguish "each refit chases noise" from "the signal itself decayed mid-sample"?
17. **The index that ate itself.** You backtest on today's S&P 500 constituents over 2010–2024. Name the bias and its direction, construct the fix — what property must the universe data have (point-in-time membership) — and explain where the same bug reappears in churn and credit-risk modeling.
18. **Bull-trained, bear-tested.** A model trained on a 7-year bull market collapses on a 3-year bear test. Argue both hypotheses — overfitting vs. regime shift — and design the evaluation that distinguishes them (does the model also fail on held-out *bull* data?).
19. **Ship the boring one.** Two models tie within CV noise; one is a small neural net, the other logistic regression. Make the staff-level case for the simpler model — variance of its risk estimate, monitoring and retraining cost, experimentation surface — and explain why "decline the fancy model" is itself a model-selection decision that interviewers explicitly reward.
20. **Design the honest benchmark.** Your team will run ~500 experiments over six months, all judged against one internal test set, with architecture decisions adapting to the results. Design the evaluation protocol end-to-end — holdout tiers, query budget, aggregation and reporting rules, criteria for minting a fresh test set — so the final reported number survives a skeptical reviewer, and state plainly what your protocol still *cannot* guarantee.
