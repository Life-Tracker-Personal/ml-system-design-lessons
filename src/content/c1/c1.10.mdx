## TL;DR

**AUC is a ranking statistic** — the *concordance probability* $P(\text{score}_+ > \text{score}_-)$ for a random positive/negative pair, identical to the normalized Mann–Whitney $U$. That single fact explains both of its famous properties: it is **invariant to class balance** (because TPR and FPR are each computed *within one class*) and it is **invariant to any monotone rescaling of scores** (so it says *nothing* about calibration). Everything in this lesson hangs off that — when ROC misleads (imbalanced data → use PR), why a perfect-AUC model can be useless (wrong operating point, or miscalibrated), and how to actually pick a threshold (from the cost matrix, not habit). (c1.1 *defined* precision/recall/ROC/AUC; this lesson derives and stress-tests them.)

## What you'll be able to do after this lesson

- Derive that $\text{AUC} = P(\text{score}_+ > \text{score}_-)$ and that this equals the normalized Wilcoxon/Mann–Whitney $U$.
- Explain *mechanically* why ROC is invariant to prevalence but precision/PR is not, and quantify the imbalanced-precision collapse.
- Resolve the c1.1 "95%-accuracy trap" precisely.
- State Davis–Goadrich dominance and choose `average_precision_score` over `auc(recall, precision)`.
- Separate *ranking* from *calibration* and predict what recalibration does to AUC.
- Recognize non-monotone signal at AUC ≈ 0.5, flip at AUC below 0.5, and set a cost-optimal threshold.

## Why metrics deserve their own lesson

> **Goal — stop choosing metrics by habit; choose them from what the decision costs.** c1.1 gave you the vocabulary as a refresher; here the point is that the headline metric everyone quotes (AUC) measures only *ranking* — not the operating point you deploy, not whether the probabilities are real. Get this wrong and you ship a 0.95-AUC model that buries analysts in false positives.

> **Vocabulary primer.** The "rusty staff engineer" assumption from c1.1 still holds.
>
> - **TPR/recall, FPR** — $\text{TP}/(\text{TP+FN})$, $\text{FP}/(\text{FP+TN})$; each normalized *within one true class* (column).
> - **Precision** — $\text{TP}/(\text{TP+FP})$; *mixes* both classes (row), hence prevalence-dependent.
> - **ROC / PR curve** — TPR-vs-FPR / precision-vs-recall as the threshold sweeps.
> - **AUC** — area under the ROC; the concordance probability. **AP** — average precision, the recommended PR-AUC.
> - **Calibration** — whether a predicted "0.9" happens 90% of the time (c1.3).

## Part 1 — Confusion matrix and the column/row distinction

> **Goal — see that the rate's *normalizer* is what makes it prevalence-(in)dependent.**

| Quantity | Formula | Normalized over |
|---|---|---|
| Recall = TPR | $\text{TP}/(\text{TP+FN})$ | actual positives (**column**) |
| FPR = 1 − Specificity | $\text{FP}/(\text{FP+TN})$ | actual negatives (**column**) |
| Precision | $\text{TP}/(\text{TP+FP})$ | predicted positives (**row** — mixes classes) |
| Accuracy | $(\text{TP+TN})/N$ | everything (prevalence-dependent) |

**F1** $=\frac{2PR}{P+R}=\frac{2\text{TP}}{2\text{TP}+\text{FP}+\text{FN}}$ — the harmonic mean punishes the smaller of precision/recall, and **it ignores TN entirely**, which is exactly why it's the imbalanced-data default (TN is the huge, uninformative cell). **Fβ** $=(1+\beta^2)\frac{PR}{\beta^2P+R}$ weights recall $\beta$ times as much as precision. **The threshold should be read off a cost matrix:** with per-error costs $c_{\text{FP}},c_{\text{FN}}$, expected cost at threshold $t$ is $\pi(1-\text{TPR}(t))c_{\text{FN}}+(1-\pi)\text{FPR}(t)c_{\text{FP}}$, and you minimize it (Part 5).

## Part 2 — AUC = concordance = Wilcoxon

> **Goal — derive the one identity that everything else follows from.**

*In one sentence:* sweep the classifier's threshold from ∞ down to −∞, plot (FPR, TPR) at every value → that's the ROC curve. AUC is the area under it, and (as we'll derive) it equals the fraction of (positive, negative) pairs whose scores are in the right order — a pure ranking statistic.

Let positives have scores $X\sim F_+$, negatives $Y\sim F_-$. The ROC curve plots $\text{TPR}=1-F_+(t)$ against $\text{FPR}=1-F_-(t)$; parameterize by $u=\text{FPR}=1-F_-(t)$:

$$\text{AUC}=\int_0^1\text{ROC}(u)\,du=\int_0^1\bigl[1-F_+(F_-^{-1}(1-u))\bigr]du$$

Substitute $t=F_-^{-1}(1-u)$ (so $du=-f_-(t)\,dt$):

$$\text{AUC}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl[1-F_+(t)\bigr]f_-(t)\,dt=\int \Pr(X>t)\,f_-(t)\,dt=\mathbb{E}_Y[\Pr(X>Y\mid Y)]=\Pr(X>Y)$$

**So AUC is the probability that a random positive outscores a random negative** — the *concordance probability*. Its empirical estimator is the fraction of concordant pairs,

$$\widehat{\text{AUC}}=\frac{1}{n_+n_-}\sum_{i,j}\Bigl[\mathbb{1}(x_i>y_j)+\tfrac12\mathbb{1}(x_i=y_j)\Bigr]=\frac{U}{n_+n_-}$$

the normalized **Mann–Whitney $U$** (= Wilcoxon rank-sum; Hanley & McNeil 1982). Two consequences drive the rest of the lesson:
1. AUC is a **rank statistic** → **distribution-free** and **invariant to any strictly increasing transform** of the scores (logit, ×10, sigmoid) → the calibration blind spot (Part 4).
2. It has a **sampling distribution** (Hanley–McNeil standard error; **DeLong's test** compares two correlated AUCs) — so "0.91 vs 0.90" may be noise; report a CI.

## Part 3 — ROC vs PR, and why balance breaks one of them

> **Goal — know mechanically why ROC ignores prevalence and PR doesn't, and quantify the trap.**

**Mechanism.** TPR uses only $\text{TP},\text{FN}$ (the positive column); FPR uses only $\text{FP},\text{TN}$ (the negative column). Neither references the other class's count, so multiplying the number of negatives by $k$ scales $\text{FP},\text{TN}$ together and leaves FPR (and TPR) unchanged — **the entire ROC curve, hence AUC, is invariant to prevalence.** Precision mixes columns; via Bayes it's exactly the posterior:

$$P=\frac{\text{TPR}\cdot\pi}{\text{TPR}\cdot\pi+\text{FPR}\cdot(1-\pi)}$$

Hold the scorer fixed (TPR, FPR fixed) and change only $\pi$ → precision and PR-AUC move, ROC-AUC doesn't.

**Worked example.** Scorer with TPR $=0.9$, FPR $=0.1$:
- **Balanced ($\pi=0.5$):** $P=\frac{0.9(0.5)}{0.9(0.5)+0.1(0.5)}=0.90$ — looks great.
- **Imbalanced ($\pi=0.01$):** $P=\frac{0.9(0.01)}{0.9(0.01)+0.1(0.99)}=\frac{0.009}{0.108}\approx0.083$ — **8.3% precision**, same scorer, *same ROC-AUC*.

**The imbalanced trap (the c1.1 "95%-accuracy trap," resolved).** At $\pi=1\%$ over 100k cases (1k positive, 99k negative), a TPR-0.9/FPR-0.1 model flags $0.9(1000)+0.1(99000)=900+9900=10{,}800$ cases — only 900 real. The tiny 0.1 FPR, multiplied by the 99× larger negative pool, **floods** the flagged set. ROC-AUC ≈ 0.9 and the system is still useless. **So for imbalanced problems, report PR-AUC** (Saito & Rehmsmeier 2015); the no-skill PR baseline is the prevalence $\pi$, not 0.5. (Davis & Goadrich 2006: a curve that *dominates* in ROC space dominates in PR space — but a larger ROC-*area* does **not** imply a larger PR-area, and crossing curves have no dominance.)

> **Stop and think.** Your model has AUC = 0.5. Does it mean "no signal"? *Hint: no — it means no monotone-rank signal. If positives concentrate at both score extremes and negatives in the middle, AUC ≈ 0.5 while the feature is strongly (non-monotonically) predictive — visible as a non-monotone ROC or a U-shaped reliability diagram. And AUC below 0.5 means the score is anti-correlated → flip its sign to get $1-\text{AUC}>0.5$.*

## Part 4 — Ranking is not calibration

> **Goal — internalize that a perfect ranker can lie about probabilities, and what fixes it.**

Because AUC depends only on score *order* (Part 2), apply any strictly increasing $g$ to all scores and AUC is unchanged — while the probabilities can become arbitrarily miscalibrated. Pass a perfectly-ranked model's scores through $p\mapsto p^{10}$: AUC stays at 0.99, but every probability is crushed toward 0 → useless for any threshold/expected-value decision. So **a model can have perfect AUC and be uncalibrated.** This is why you also need *proper scoring rules* — **log-loss** and **Brier** (derived in c1.3) — and **reliability diagrams**, which *do* penalize miscalibration (Brier = reliability − resolution + uncertainty, c1.3's Murphy decomposition). The recalibration maps — **Platt scaling** (fit a sigmoid) and **isotonic regression** (monotone step function) — are *monotone*, so they **improve log-loss/Brier while leaving AUC unchanged**: the cleanest proof that ranking and calibration are orthogonal.

## Part 5 — Picking the threshold

> **Goal — choose the operating point AUC can't give you.**

- **Youden's J:** $J=\text{Sensitivity}+\text{Specificity}-1=\text{TPR}-\text{FPR}$ (the two are algebraically identical) — maximizing it = the ROC point of maximum vertical distance above the diagonal. Optimal only under an *implicit* equal-cost assumption.
- **Cost-optimal threshold (the general answer):** predict positive iff the calibrated $p\ge t^\star=\dfrac{c_{\text{FP}}}{c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}}}$ (derived by comparing expected costs of the two actions). Equivalently, the optimal ROC operating point is where the iso-cost line of slope $\frac{(1-\pi)c_{\text{FP}}}{\pi\,c_{\text{FN}}}$ is tangent to the curve. A **calibrated model + a cost matrix** beats picking F1's argmax.

*Iso-cost intuition:* an iso-cost line is the set of ROC points with equal expected cost (the same way an indifference curve or a budget line collects points with equal utility in economics). You want the *highest* curve point that touches such a line — the point where cost per additional TPR is exactly the FP-cost ratio.

## The stats / quant-research lens

- **AUC = concordance = normalized Wilcoxon/Mann–Whitney $U$** — a distribution-free rank statistic with a known sampling distribution (Hanley–McNeil SE; **DeLong** test for comparing two correlated AUCs). "Is ΔAUC significant?" is a real statistical question.
- **Proper scoring rules:** log-loss and Brier are *strictly proper* (uniquely minimized by the true probabilities, c1.3); AUC and accuracy are **not** proper. Train/select on a proper rule; AUC measures discrimination only.
- **Prevalence-dependence is a feature of precision** (it's the posterior $P(y{=}1\mid\hat y{=}1)$); choose ROC vs PR by whether the decision depends on the *prior* (PR) or only the *separability of the class-conditionals* (ROC).
- **Threshold = expected-cost minimization** ($t^\star=c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}})$ on the calibrated posterior); metric choice is decision theory.
- **Calibration ⊥ ranking:** monotone recalibration (Platt/isotonic/temperature) changes log-loss/Brier but not AUC — the rigorous rebuttal to "high AUC = good model."

## Decision matrix

| Goal | Metric |
|---|---|
| Compare rankers, balanced-ish | ROC-AUC (+ DeLong CI) |
| Compare rankers, imbalanced | **PR-AUC / average precision** (no-skill baseline = $\pi$) |
| Need trustworthy probabilities | log-loss / Brier + reliability diagram (c1.3) |
| Operating-point performance | precision/recall/Fβ **at your threshold**, or expected cost |
| Only the top-$k$ matters | partial AUC (`max_fpr`) |

## Edge cases & gotchas

- **AUC below 0.5** → the score is anti-correlated; **flip the sign** ($1-\text{AUC}$).
- **Partial AUC** (`max_fpr`) when only the low-FPR region matters (you can review only the top 1%); sklearn returns it McClish-standardized (a random model still scores 0.5 in that region).
- **Ties in scores** → the correct AUC adds $\tfrac12$ per tie (the trapezoidal/strict-inequality forms disagree on ties).
- **Multiclass AUC** → one-vs-rest (`'ovr'`, *sensitive* to imbalance) vs one-vs-one (`'ovo'` Hand–Till, *insensitive* under macro-averaging).

## Worked decision walk-throughs

**Scenario A: 1%-positive fraud model.** Report **PR-AUC via `average_precision_score`** (not ROC-AUC — it hides the precision collapse), calibrate the scores, then set the threshold from the cost matrix ($t^\star=c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}})$).

**Scenario B: probabilities feed an expected-value pipeline.** Optimize/monitor **log-loss or Brier** and check a **reliability diagram** — AUC alone is blind to the calibration you depend on.

**Scenario C: you can only review the top 1% of flagged cases.** **Partial AUC** over the low-FPR region (`max_fpr=0.01`); the rest of the ROC curve is irrelevant to your operating budget.

**Scenario D: "model B's AUC is 0.91 vs A's 0.90."** Don't celebrate yet — use **DeLong's test** on the (correlated) AUCs to check the difference is real, not sampling noise.

## Production failure modes

1. **Shipping a high-AUC model uncalibrated.** Offline AUC is great; the 0.9-bucket is right 70% of the time; every EV calculation is biased. Calibrate (c1.3) and monitor a reliability diagram.
2. **`auc(recall, precision)` for PR-AUC.** Trapezoidal PR-area linearly interpolates (invalid in PR space — Davis–Goadrich) and **over-estimates**; use **`average_precision_score`** (a non-interpolated step sum).
3. **ROC-AUC on heavily imbalanced data.** Visually deceptive — small FPR changes hide huge precision collapses; use PR-AUC.
4. **Multiclass `'ovr'` AUC on imbalanced classes.** Sensitive to imbalance even with `average='macro'`; use `'ovo'` if you want imbalance-insensitivity.
5. **`log_loss` API drift.** The 2nd positional arg moved to `y_proba` (`y_pred` deprecated, removal in 1.11); pass `labels=` when not all classes appear.

## Interview gotchas

- **"Derive AUC = concordance."** $P(\text{score}_+>\text{score}_-)$, = $U/(n_+n_-)$ = Wilcoxon; it's a rank statistic.
- **"Why is ROC-AUC unaffected by class balance but precision isn't?"** Column- vs row-normalization; precision is the posterior, so it depends on the prior $\pi$.
- **"Imbalanced data — ROC-AUC or PR-AUC?"** PR-AUC / average precision (Saito–Rehmsmeier); show the 0.90→0.083 collapse.
- **"Can a model have great AUC and be useless?"** Yes — wrong operating point, or miscalibrated (AUC is monotone-invariant). Pair AUC with a proper scoring rule + cost-based threshold.
- **"`average_precision_score` vs `auc(recall, precision)`?"** AP is the non-interpolated step sum; the trapezoidal version over-estimates.
- **"How would you set the threshold?"** From the cost matrix: $t^\star=c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}})$ on a calibrated $p$; or Youden's J under equal costs.
