## TL;DR

**AUC is a ranking statistic** — the *concordance probability* $P(\text{score}_+ > \text{score}_-)$ for a random positive/negative pair, identical to the normalized Mann–Whitney $U$ statistic. The rest is a consequence of that one sentence. Because the two axes of the ROC curve, TPR and FPR, are each computed *within one class*, AUC is **invariant to class balance**. Because it looks only at the order of the scores, it is **invariant to any monotone rescaling of scores**, which means it says *nothing* about calibration. Those two invariances explain the rest: when ROC misleads (imbalanced data → use PR), why a perfect-AUC model can be useless (wrong operating point, or miscalibrated), and how to actually pick a threshold (from the cost matrix, not habit). (Precision, recall, ROC and AUC are *defined* in c1.1; here they are derived and stress-tested.)

Then the second half, which is the same story told from the data side. Imbalance is **not a problem in itself** — it's only a problem when it degrades the metric you actually care about, and the cleanest fix is almost never to touch the data: **train normally, then move the decision threshold and recalibrate.** The key result: **every resampling/reweighting scheme is mathematically equivalent to an implicit shift of the decision threshold on the base rate** — so once the threshold is chosen explicitly, the resampling was at best a detour and at worst it destroyed the calibration. This unifies the imbalance threads already in the course: c1.2's loss levers (focal/class-balanced, decoupling), c1.1's gradient-share and 95%-accuracy trap, and c1.9's PR-AUC-over-ROC-AUC.

## Why metrics deserve their own lesson

> **Goal — stop choosing metrics by habit; choose them from what the decision costs.** c1.1 supplies the vocabulary; the point here is that the headline metric everyone quotes (AUC) measures only *ranking* — not the operating point you deploy, not whether the probabilities are real. Get this wrong and you ship a 0.95-AUC model that buries analysts in false positives.

That failure is the one to pin down first. Two things are true at the same time: the model ranks well, and the review queue is unusable. Neither claim is a lie. They come apart because AUC answers a question about pairs of examples, and your ops team lives with a question about one threshold. Most of what follows is bookkeeping on exactly where those two questions diverge.

Before the derivations, the words.

> **Vocabulary primer.** The definitions from c1.1, restated in the form this lesson uses them.
>
> - **TPR/recall, FPR** — $\text{TP}/(\text{TP+FN})$, $\text{FP}/(\text{FP+TN})$; each normalized *within one true class* (column).
> - **Precision** — $\text{TP}/(\text{TP+FP})$; *mixes* both classes (row), hence prevalence-dependent.
> - **ROC / PR curve** — TPR-vs-FPR / precision-vs-recall as the threshold sweeps.
> - **AUC** — area under the ROC; the concordance probability. **AP** — average precision, the recommended PR-AUC.
> - **Calibration** — whether a predicted "0.9" happens 90% of the time (c1.2).

## Part 1 — Confusion matrix and the column/row distinction

> **Goal — see that the rate's *normalizer* is what makes it prevalence-(in)dependent.**

Every rate in this lesson is a count of mistakes divided by something. The numerators are not what separates these metrics. The *denominators* are. So read the table below by its third column first, and only then by its second.

| Quantity | Formula | Normalized over |
|---|---|---|
| Recall = TPR | $\text{TP}/(\text{TP+FN})$ | actual positives (**column**) |
| FPR = 1 − Specificity | $\text{FP}/(\text{FP+TN})$ | actual negatives (**column**) |
| Precision | $\text{TP}/(\text{TP+FP})$ | predicted positives (**row** — mixes classes) |
| Accuracy | $(\text{TP+TN})/N$ | everything (prevalence-dependent) |

Recall divides by a count you get by looking only at the true positives. FPR divides by a count you get by looking only at the true negatives. Precision divides by the number of alerts you produced, and alerts come from both classes. Accuracy divides by $N$, the total number of examples in the evaluation set, which is why it moves with the class mix. That is the whole distinction, and it will explain the ROC/PR split in Part 3 without any new machinery.

Two composite metrics show up constantly. Write $P$ for precision and $R$ for recall. **F1** $=\frac{2PR}{P+R}=\frac{2\text{TP}}{2\text{TP}+\text{FP}+\text{FN}}$ — the harmonic mean punishes the smaller of precision/recall, and **it ignores TN entirely**, which is exactly why it's the imbalanced-data default (TN is the huge, uninformative cell). Look at the second form: TN does not appear anywhere in it. Multiply your true negatives by a hundred and F1 does not budge. **Fβ** $=(1+\beta^2)\frac{PR}{\beta^2P+R}$ weights recall $\beta$ times as much as precision, so $\beta$ is the dial you turn when the two errors are not equally bad. The lever almost nobody reaches for by default is the one that makes that trade explicit: **the threshold should be read off a cost matrix.** Let $\pi$ denote the *prevalence*, the fraction of cases that are truly positive, and let $c_{\text{FP}},c_{\text{FN}}$ be the cost charged for one false positive and one false negative. Expected cost at threshold $t$ is $\pi(1-\text{TPR}(t))c_{\text{FN}}+(1-\pi)\text{FPR}(t)c_{\text{FP}}$, and you minimize it (Part 5). Read that expression left to right: the share of cases that are positive, times the fraction of them you missed, times what a miss costs, plus the share that are negative, times the fraction you wrongly flagged, times what a false alarm costs.

In this layout (rows = Actual, cols = Predicted) Recall/TPR and FPR are within-actual-class rates — they use one row's totals only, so they don't move when you rescale the other class → ROC's prevalence invariance. ## Part 2 — AUC = concordance = Wilcoxon

> **Goal — derive the one identity that everything else follows from.**

The punchline comes before the derivation. AUC looks like a geometric quantity: it is literally an area under a plotted curve, and nothing about "area under a curve" suggests a probability about pairs of examples. But it is exactly that probability, and once you believe it you can stop thinking about the curve at all.

*In one sentence:* sweep the classifier's threshold from ∞ down to −∞, plot (FPR, TPR) at every value → that's the ROC curve. AUC is the area under it, and (as the derivation below shows) it equals the fraction of (positive, negative) pairs whose scores are in the right order — a pure ranking statistic.

Now the derivation. Set it up with two score distributions, one per class. Let $X$ be the score of a randomly drawn positive and $Y$ the score of a randomly drawn negative, with cumulative distribution functions $F_+$ and $F_-$ respectively — so $F_+(t)$ is the probability a positive scores at or below $t$, and likewise for $F_-$ on negatives. Sweeping the threshold $t$, the ROC curve plots $\text{TPR}=1-F_+(t)$ against $\text{FPR}=1-F_-(t)$; parameterize by the horizontal coordinate $u=\text{FPR}=1-F_-(t)$:

$$\text{AUC}=\int_0^1\text{ROC}(u)\,du=\int_0^1\bigl[1-F_+(F_-^{-1}(1-u))\bigr]du$$

That reads: as $u$ walks from 0 to 1, look up which threshold produces that false positive rate, and record the true positive rate there. The nested inverse $F_-^{-1}$ is only doing that lookup. Integrating in $u$ is awkward for exactly that reason, so change the variable back to the threshold.

Substitute $t=F_-^{-1}(1-u)$, and write $f_-$ for the density of the negative scores — the derivative of $F_-$ — so that $du=-f_-(t)\,dt$:

$$\text{AUC}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl[1-F_+(t)\bigr]f_-(t)\,dt=\int \Pr(X>t)\,f_-(t)\,dt=\mathbb{E}_Y[\Pr(X>Y\mid Y)]=\Pr(X>Y)$$

Walk the last line in English. $1-F_+(t)$ is the chance a positive scores above $t$. $f_-(t)\,dt$ is the chance a negative lands right at $t$. Multiply and integrate over all $t$, and you have averaged "positive beats $t$" over wherever the negative happened to fall. That average is just the probability that the positive beats the negative.

**So AUC is the probability that a random positive outscores a random negative** — the *concordance probability*. On a finite test set you estimate it by counting. Let there be $n_+$ positives with scores $x_1,\dots,x_{n_+}$ and $n_-$ negatives with scores $y_1,\dots,y_{n_-}$, let the index $i$ run over the positives and $j$ over the negatives, and let $\mathbb{1}(\cdot)$ be the indicator that is 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise. The hat on $\widehat{\text{AUC}}$ marks it as an estimate from that sample rather than the population quantity. The estimator is the fraction of concordant pairs,

$$\widehat{\text{AUC}}=\frac{1}{n_+n_-}\sum_{i,j}\Bigl[\mathbb{1}(x_i>y_j)+\tfrac12\mathbb{1}(x_i=y_j)\Bigr]=\frac{U}{n_+n_-}$$

where $U$ is the value of that double sum. This one you can compute by hand. Form every positive/negative pair, of which there are $n_+n_-$. Score 1 for each pair the model got in the right order, score a half when the two scores tie, add them up, divide by the number of pairs. That is the whole statistic — no curve, no integral, no probabilities.

That sum has a name, and it is not a new object: it is the normalized **Mann–Whitney $U$** (= Wilcoxon rank-sum; Hanley & McNeil 1982). Two consequences drive the rest of the lesson:
1. AUC is a **rank statistic** → **distribution-free** and **invariant to any strictly increasing transform** of the scores (logit, ×10, sigmoid) → the calibration blind spot (Part 4). If a transform preserves order, it preserves every indicator in the sum above, so it cannot change the total.
2. It has a **sampling distribution** (Hanley–McNeil standard error; **DeLong's test** compares two correlated AUCs) — so "0.91 vs 0.90" may be noise; report a confidence interval. AUC is an estimate from a finite sample, like any other, and it wobbles when you resample.

## Part 3 — ROC vs PR, and why balance breaks one of them

> **Goal — know mechanically why ROC ignores prevalence and PR doesn't, and quantify the trap.**

Take Part 1's third column and turn it into an experiment. Freeze the model, freeze the scores, freeze the threshold, and multiply only the number of negatives. Which metrics move?

**Mechanism.** TPR uses only $\text{TP},\text{FN}$ (the positive column); FPR uses only $\text{FP},\text{TN}$ (the negative column). Neither references the other class's count, so multiplying the number of negatives by $k$ scales $\text{FP},\text{TN}$ together and leaves FPR (and TPR) unchanged — **the entire ROC curve, hence AUC, is invariant to prevalence.** Both the numerator and the denominator of FPR grew by the same factor $k$, and the ratio survives. Precision behaves differently, because it mixes columns. Via Bayes it is exactly the posterior probability that a flagged case is positive — written $P$ below, with $\pi$ the prevalence as in Part 1:

$$P=\frac{\text{TPR}\cdot\pi}{\text{TPR}\cdot\pi+\text{FPR}\cdot(1-\pi)}$$

In words: of everything you flagged, the share that was really positive equals the true positives you caught (the positives you had, $\pi$, times the fraction you caught, TPR) divided by everything you flagged (those true positives plus the negatives you had, $1-\pi$, times the fraction you wrongly flagged, FPR). The prior $\pi$ sits right there in the formula. TPR and FPR do not contain it; precision does.

Hold the scorer fixed (TPR, FPR fixed) and change only $\pi$ → precision and PR-AUC move, ROC-AUC doesn't.

**Worked example.** Scorer with TPR $=0.9$, FPR $=0.1$:
- **Balanced ($\pi=0.5$):** $P=\frac{0.9(0.5)}{0.9(0.5)+0.1(0.5)}=0.90$ — looks great.
- **Imbalanced ($\pi=0.01$):** $P=\frac{0.9(0.01)}{0.9(0.01)+0.1(0.99)}=\frac{0.009}{0.108}\approx0.083$ — **8.3% precision**, same scorer, *same ROC-AUC*.

Nothing about the model changed between those two lines. The only edit was the population it runs on. Precision fell from 0.90 to 0.083 and ROC-AUC did not move a decimal place.

Now put counts on it, because rates hide the size of the pile.

**The imbalanced trap (the c1.1 "95%-accuracy trap," resolved).** At $\pi=1\%$ over 100k cases (1k positive, 99k negative), a TPR-0.9/FPR-0.1 model flags $0.9(1000)+0.1(99000)=900+9900=10{,}800$ cases — only 900 real. The tiny 0.1 FPR, multiplied by the 99× larger negative pool, **floods** the flagged set. A 10% error rate on the negatives sounds forgivable until you notice there are 99,000 of them. ROC-AUC ≈ 0.9 and the system is still useless. **So for imbalanced problems, report PR-AUC** (Saito & Rehmsmeier 2015); the no-skill PR baseline is the prevalence $\pi$, not 0.5. That baseline shift matters when you read a plot: the height a no-skill model sits at moves with the prevalence, so a low PR curve has to be judged against $\pi$ rather than against 0.5. One curve *dominates* another when it sits on or above it at every single point, with no crossings anywhere. That pointwise relation carries between the two spaces: a curve that dominates in ROC space dominates in PR space (Davis & Goadrich 2006). Areas do not carry, though — a larger ROC-*area* does **not** imply a larger PR-area, and crossing curves have no dominance in either space. Dominance is the strong, pointwise statement; area is a scalar summary, and the implication only runs one way.

A low AUC also has a natural reading that is wrong.

> **Stop and think.** Your model has AUC = 0.5. Does it mean "no signal"? *Hint: no — it means no monotone-rank signal. If positives concentrate at both score extremes and negatives in the middle, AUC ≈ 0.5 while the feature is strongly (non-monotonically) predictive — visible as a non-monotone ROC or a U-shaped reliability diagram. And AUC below 0.5 means the score is anti-correlated → flip its sign to get $1-\text{AUC}>0.5$.*

## Part 4 — Ranking is not calibration

> **Goal — internalize that a perfect ranker can lie about probabilities, and what fixes it.**

Part 2 proved AUC only sees order, and that proof has a price attached. Apply any strictly increasing function $g$ to all the scores — any map that never sends a higher score below a lower one — and AUC is unchanged, while the probabilities can become arbitrarily miscalibrated. The cheapest demonstration takes one line. Pass a perfectly-ranked model's predicted probabilities $p$ through $p\mapsto p^{10}$: AUC stays at 0.99, but every probability is crushed toward 0 → useless for any threshold/expected-value decision. Raising to a power never reorders anything, so every pair in the concordance sum keeps its verdict. Meanwhile every reported probability is now far below the frequency it is supposed to describe, and any expected-value calculation downstream is wrong. **So a model can have perfect AUC and be uncalibrated.**

AUC therefore cannot be the only number you report. You also need *proper scoring rules* — **log-loss** and **Brier** (derived in c1.2) — and **reliability diagrams**, which *do* penalize miscalibration (Brier = reliability − resolution + uncertainty, c1.2's Murphy decomposition). The fix, when you need it, is a second small model fitted on top of the first. The recalibration maps — **Platt scaling** (fit a sigmoid) and **isotonic regression** (monotone step function) — are *monotone*, so they **improve log-loss/Brier while leaving AUC unchanged**: the cleanest proof that ranking and calibration are orthogonal. Read that last clause carefully, because it is a two-way statement. Recalibration cannot help your AUC, and it cannot hurt it either. It operates in a dimension AUC does not measure.

## Part 5 — Picking the threshold

> **Goal — choose the operating point AUC can't give you.**

A curve is not a decision. At some point you pick one number, and everything the ROC curve was averaging over collapses to that one point. There are two common ways to pick it, and only one of them is honest about what it assumes.

- **Youden's J:** $J=\text{Sensitivity}+\text{Specificity}-1=\text{TPR}-\text{FPR}$ (the two are algebraically identical) — maximizing it = the ROC point of maximum vertical distance above the diagonal. Optimal only under an *implicit* equal-cost assumption. The assumption is buried in the arithmetic: TPR and FPR enter with coefficients +1 and −1, which is a statement that one missed positive and one false alarm are equally bad.
- **Cost-optimal threshold (the general answer):** predict positive iff the calibrated $p\ge t^\star=\dfrac{c_{\text{FP}}}{c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}}}$ (derived by comparing expected costs of the two actions). Read the fraction: the threshold is what share of the total error cost sits on false positives. Make false alarms expensive relative to misses and $t^\star$ climbs toward 1, so you flag less. Make misses expensive and $t^\star$ falls toward 0, so you flag more. Equivalently, the optimal ROC operating point is where the iso-cost line of slope $\frac{(1-\pi)c_{\text{FP}}}{\pi\,c_{\text{FN}}}$ is tangent to the curve. A **calibrated model + a cost matrix** beats picking F1's argmax.

Note the word "calibrated" in that rule. It is load-bearing. Comparing $p$ to a cost-derived number only means anything if $p$ is a real probability, which is exactly what Part 4 said AUC cannot promise you.

*Iso-cost intuition:* collect all the ROC points that would cost you the same amount in expectation and you get a straight line; economics collects points of equal utility the same way and calls the result an indifference curve or a budget line. Here the line is called an iso-cost line. You want the *highest* curve point that touches such a line — the point where cost per additional TPR is exactly the FP-cost ratio.

## Edge cases & gotchas

Four places where the AUC number in front of you does not mean what it usually means.

- **AUC below 0.5** → the score is anti-correlated; **flip the sign** ($1-\text{AUC}$). That is a repair, not a defect report: a systematically backwards ranker is a good ranker wearing a minus sign.
- **Partial AUC** (`max_fpr`) when only the low-FPR region matters (you can review only the top 1%); sklearn returns it McClish-standardized (a random model still scores 0.5 in that region). Standardizing matters because otherwise a sliver of area under a small region would look tiny no matter how good the model is.
- **Ties in scores** → the correct AUC adds $\tfrac12$ per tie (the trapezoidal/strict-inequality forms disagree on ties). The half-credit term is the one in the empirical estimator from Part 2.
- **Multiclass AUC** → one-vs-rest (`'ovr'`, *sensitive* to imbalance, because the pooled "rest" class is rebuilt out of whatever the other classes' proportions happen to be) vs one-vs-one (*insensitive* under macro-averaging, because each pair's AUC involves only two class-conditional score distributions and inherits Part 3's prevalence invariance; that pairwise construction is `'ovo'`, due to Hand–Till).

## Why imbalance gets its own (consolidating) lesson

> **Goal — turn the scattered imbalance folklore into one decision framework, and replace "always oversample" with "calibrate + threshold, and only resample if it provably helps your metric."** The contrarian position, and the evidence backs it: the reflex to SMOTE is usually unnecessary, often harmful to calibration, and almost always reproducible by a threshold move you should make explicitly anyway.

The first half was about reading a model. The second is about the temptation to change the data before the model ever sees it. That temptation is mostly a mirage, and Part 4 below carries the arithmetic that makes it one.

> **Vocabulary primer.** The terms this half uses, stated once.
>
> - **Imbalance ratio (IR)** — $N_{\text{maj}}/N_{\text{min}}$; "mild" ≤ 4:1, "extreme" ≥ 100:1; fraud/CTR routinely $1000{:}1$+.
> - **Over/under-sampling** — duplicate minority (ROS) / drop majority (RUS) rows.
> - **SMOTE** — synthesize minority points by interpolating between minority neighbors.
> - **Class weight / cost-sensitive** — scale each class's loss; act on a cost matrix.
> - **Threshold moving / prior correction** — adjust the decision cutoff / recalibrate predicted probabilities for the deployment base rate.

## Part 1 — When is imbalance actually a problem?

> **Goal — stop treating imbalance as automatically bad; locate the real harm in the metric.**

The reflex answer is "imbalance is bad, rebalance it." Before accepting that, name the harm. There are exactly two mechanisms by which a skewed base rate hurts you, and one famous place where it does not hurt you at all.

Accuracy is *prevalence-weighted*, so "always predict negative" scores 95% at 5% positives (c1.1's 95%-accuracy trap) — useless. That is harm number one, and it is a harm to the *metric*, not to the model. Harm number two is to training itself: under plain cross-entropy, the $q-y$ gradient (c1.2, with $q$ the predicted probability and $y$ the 0/1 label) is ~99% sourced from negatives at 1% prevalence, so the minority barely moves the weights. Set against those two, **ROC-AUC is invariant to prevalence** (c1.9), so imbalance doesn't hurt *ranking*; it hurts the *operating point* (precision collapses) and the *gradient signal*. Part 3 of the first half proved that invariance, and it is the reason "imbalance ruins everything" is too coarse a claim. The honest statement: **imbalance is a problem only insofar as it degrades the metric you care about.** A well-calibrated model evaluated by PR-AUC and deployed at a cost-chosen threshold often needs **no** resampling at all.

## Part 2 — Resampling (and its leakage trap)

> **Goal — synthesize/drop rows to rebalance, and know the assumption and the leakage failure that come with it.**

Suppose you do decide to rebalance. The two obvious moves each throw something away.

**Random oversampling** duplicates minority rows (adds no information → overfits the duplicates); **random undersampling** drops majority rows (discards data, especially hard negatives near the boundary). The two failures are not symmetric. ROS makes the model memorize a handful of rare rows it now sees many times. RUS deletes exactly the majority examples sitting nearest the boundary — the ones that were teaching the model where the boundary is.

SMOTE is the attempt to get new minority rows that are not literal copies. **SMOTE** (Chawla et al. 2002) synthesizes minority points by interpolating between a minority point and one of its $k$ minority neighbors:

$$x_{\text{new}} = x_i + u\,(x_i^{(\text{nn})} - x_i),\qquad u\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)$$

Here $x_i$ is the feature vector of a minority example, the superscript $(\text{nn})$ marks one of its $k$ minority nearest neighbors picked at random, $u$ is a fresh uniform draw on the unit interval giving the fraction of the way to travel, and $x_{\text{new}}$ is the synthetic point that results. (This $u$ is a step length in feature space; it has nothing to do with the ROC coordinate $u$ of Part 2.) In words: stand on a minority point, pick one of its minority neighbors, walk a random fraction of the way toward it, and drop a new labeled point there. That is all it is.

So new points lie on segments of the minority $k$-NN graph — **assuming the minority class is locally convex/linear** in feature space. The assumption is doing real work: a point on the segment between two minority examples is only a plausible minority example if the region between them belongs to the minority class. Variants concentrate synthesis on the boundary: **Borderline-SMOTE** (only "in-danger" points) and **ADASYN** (density-adaptive: more synthesis where the minority is harder). For mixed types, **SMOTENC** takes the neighborhood *mode* of categoricals (plain SMOTE on one-hots yields meaningless fractions) — an interpolated one-hot says a row is 0.4 of one category and 0.6 of another, which corresponds to no actual record.

<SmoteInterpolation caption="SMOTE, in one picture. Pick a minority point (red, ringed), find its k=5 minority neighbors, and place synthetic points (open red circles) on the line segments to each. Great when the minority manifold is locally linear/convex — bad when it curves. Look at the inset for the failure: a banana-shaped minority distribution has synthesized points landing off the true manifold entirely, in territory the minority class never occupies. That's why Borderline-SMOTE and ADASYN exist, and why 'SMOTE on high-dim categoricals' is nonsense unless you use SMOTENC." />

Now the bug behind most inflated cross-validation scores in this area.

**The critical leakage trap.** Resampling is a *fit*-time transform that uses labels. Doing it **before** the split (or before CV folding) lets a synthetic/duplicated point derived from a row land in a *different* fold than that row → the validation label leaks into training (c1.1's information-availability rule), the exact structural bug as c1.6's non-OOF stacking. The synthetic point is a blend of two real rows, so if one of those rows is sitting in your validation fold, the model has already trained on a near-copy of the thing it is about to be scored on. **Resample inside each fold, on the training partition only.** Use `imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline`, whose samplers run only on `fit`/`fit_resample` and are **bypassed on `transform`/`predict`** — so the held-out fold is scored at the true prevalence. (`sklearn.pipeline.Pipeline` can't host a resampler, which is *why* imbalanced-learn ships its own.)

<SmoteLeakageSplit caption="Wrong (left) vs right (right). Trace one synthetic point back to its two parents in each panel. Left: SMOTE runs on the full dataset, then you split — every synthetic point is an interpolation of two real points, and if one of those real points ends up in val, its synthesized neighbors are already in train. Val is contaminated. Right: split first, then run SMOTE only on the training fold; val is scored at the true prevalence, on rows the model has never seen or interpolated from. ## Part 3 — Reweighting and cost-sensitive learning

> **Goal — re-weight the loss instead of the data, and know when that differs from resampling.**

If the problem is that the minority contributes too little to the gradient, there is a way to fix that without touching a single row: multiply its loss.

Class weighting scales each class's loss by a constant. For a class $c$, sklearn's `class_weight='balanced'` uses $w_c=\frac{N}{K\,n_c}$, where $w_c$ is that class's loss multiplier, $N$ the number of training rows, $K$ the number of classes and $n_c$ the number of rows in class $c$ (n_samples / (n_classes × class count)) — e.g. 100 pos / 900 neg gives $w_{\text{pos}}=1000/(2\cdot100)=5$, $w_{\text{neg}}\approx0.56$. Check the arithmetic: 1000 total rows, 2 classes, 100 positives, so each positive counts for 5, and each of the 900 negatives counts for about 0.56. The two classes now contribute the same total weight.

Which raises the question of whether reweighting is oversampling under another name. Mostly it is, with one real exception. **Reweighting ≡ resampling in expectation** over a full epoch (both rescale the minority's total gradient contribution), but they **diverge under minibatch SGD**: oversampling changes the *empirical distribution* (more steps on duplicated rows → overfitting), reweighting changes per-example gradient *magnitude* (noisier/larger steps, may need LR retuning). This is c1.2's Stop-and-think made precise — and neither is free, because both shift the base rate (Part 4).

*Concretely — where they diverge:* oversampling puts more *gradient steps* on duplicated rare rows (they appear twice each epoch), so the model sees them more often. Class weighting takes fewer steps but each step's rare-class contribution is bigger. Identical under full-batch GD; genuinely different under minibatch SGD, where step count and step magnitude both affect the trajectory.

## Part 4 — Threshold moving and prior correction (the central insight)

> **Goal — see that the right lever is a calibrated probability plus a cost-derived threshold, and that resampling is just an implicit, worse-calibrated version of it.**

Resampling feels like it adds information. It does not. The algebra below shows exactly what it does instead.

**Cost-optimal threshold.** With per-error costs, predict positive iff the calibrated $p\ge t^\star=\dfrac{c_{\text{FP}}}{c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}}}$ (c1.9). Nothing in that rule mentions the base rate. Imbalance enters only through how it affects your ability to *estimate* $p$ — not through any need to rebalance.

**Resampling = an implicit threshold shift.** Write $\tau$ for the true positive prevalence in deployment (the quantity the first half called $\pi$) and $\bar y$ for the positive rate in the training set after resampling — the bar is the usual mark for an average over the training rows, and balancing to 50/50 makes $\bar y = 0.5$. Train on $\bar y$ instead of $\tau$ and, by Bayes, balancing **multiplies the predicted odds** by $(1-\tau)/\tau$ relative to the truth. Call the resulting probability $p_{\text{bal}}$ and the honest one $p_{\text{true}}$:

$$\frac{p_{\text{bal}}}{1-p_{\text{bal}}}=\frac{p_{\text{true}}}{1-p_{\text{true}}}\cdot\frac{1-\tau}{\tau}$$

Read it as: the odds your rebalanced model reports equal the honest odds times a constant that depends only on the true prevalence. Not a function of the example. A constant, applied uniformly to every prediction.

Two things follow, and they are the whole argument. First, the predicted probabilities are now systematically **over-estimated** for the minority — *miscalibrated* (exactly what van den Goorbergh et al. 2022 measured: resampling leaves ROC-AUC ~unchanged but wrecks calibration). Second, the *decision* is identical to leaving probabilities alone and moving the threshold by the corresponding amount. A constant multiplier on the odds cannot reorder anything, which is why AUC survives. It also maps one cutoff onto another cutoff, which is why the decisions are reproducible by a threshold move. **"Resampling improved my classifier" almost always means "resampling silently moved my threshold."** For a logistic model the damage is confined to one number, the intercept, so undoing it is one subtraction: with $\hat\beta_0$ the intercept fitted on the resampled data and $\beta_0^{\text{corr}}$ the value to deploy, $\beta_0^{\text{corr}}=\hat\beta_0-\ln\!\bigl[\frac{1-\tau}{\tau}\frac{\bar y}{1-\bar y}\bigr]$ recovers the original model. That subtraction is **King & Zeng's** intercept correction. The same idea in more than two classes adds each class's log-prior back onto its score: with $f_y$ the model's logit for class $y$ and $\pi_y$ that class's prevalence, the adjusted score is $f_y+\log\pi_y$, known as **logit adjustment** (Menon et al. 2021 — from c1.2; and Kang 2020's decoupling shows the *classifier head*, not the representation, is the bottleneck).

*What the King–Zeng correction is doing:* if you trained on 50/50 but will deploy at 1/99, every log-odds output is offset by the same constant $\ln[(1-\tau)/\tau \cdot \bar y/(1-\bar y)]$. Subtract that constant from every logit and the probabilities snap back to the true prevalence — no retraining needed.

> **Stop and think.** A colleague reports a jump from 0.78 to 0.95 PR-AUC after applying SMOTE to the whole dataset and then doing 5-fold CV. Why is that number almost certainly fake? *Hint: SMOTE-before-split is leakage — synthetic minority points are interpolations of real points, so a validation point's near-duplicate (or its very neighbors) sit in the training folds; the model is scored on rows it effectively trained on (c1.1 / c1.6 OOF). Resample inside each fold via `imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline` and the inflated score collapses back toward 0.78.*

## Part 5 — Extreme imbalance: reframe as anomaly detection

> **Goal — recognize when the "positive class" is too sparse to model as classification.**

Push the imbalance ratio far enough and the framing itself breaks, not just the metric. At ~0.01% positives, there aren't enough positives to define a decision boundary; model the **majority (normal) distribution** and flag outliers instead. You stop asking "what separates the two classes" and start asking "what does normal look like, and what doesn't fit."

Two standard tools do this. **One-Class SVM** (Schölkopf 2001) learns a boundary enclosing a $\nu$-fraction of normal data — best when training data is *clean* (novelty detection). **Isolation Forest** (Liu et al. 2008): anomalies are isolated in *fewer* random splits → shorter average path length; robust to contaminated/multimodal data, with `contamination` setting the flagged fraction. The Isolation Forest intuition: a point sitting far from the crowd gets cut off from everything else after only a couple of random splits, while a point inside a dense cluster takes many. This is the same "is it really imbalance, or a different problem?" move as c1.1's click-data-is-causal-inference framing.

However you model it, the evaluation discipline does not relax. **Evaluate under imbalance with PR-AUC, not ROC-AUC** (c1.9), use per-class metrics, and **stratify your splits** (c1.1) — at 1% positives a 1000-row test set has ~Binomial(1000, 0.01) positives (SD ≈ 3.15), so recall estimates are wildly noisy without stratification. Ten expected positives, give or take three, is the entire denominator of your recall estimate. One unlucky split and recall swings by tens of points for no modelling reason at all.

## Tradeoff matrix

Read this top to bottom and stop at the first row that fits. The ordering is deliberate: the cheap, honest lever is first.

| Lever | What it does | Cost | Reach for it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Threshold move + calibrate** | sets the operating point on a calibrated $p$ | ~free, honest | **default** — almost always first |
| Class weights / `pos_weight` | reweights the loss | cheap; shifts base rate | gradient signal too weak; recalibrate after |
| Random over/under-sample | rebalances data | overfit (ROS) / data loss (RUS) | RUS to *scale* at extreme IR |
| SMOTE / ADASYN | synthesizes minority | leakage risk; high-dim/categorical failure | low-dim, continuous, *and* it beats threshold on your metric |
| Anomaly detection | models normal, flags outliers | different paradigm | extreme IR (~0.01%) |

## The stats / quant-research lens

The same material, restated in the vocabulary of statistics rather than of ML tooling.

- **AUC = concordance = normalized Wilcoxon/Mann–Whitney $U$** — a distribution-free rank statistic with a known sampling distribution (Hanley–McNeil SE; **DeLong** test for comparing two correlated AUCs). "Is ΔAUC significant?" is a real statistical question, with a real test attached, and not a rhetorical one.
- **Proper scoring rules:** log-loss and Brier are *strictly proper* (uniquely minimized by the true probabilities, c1.2); AUC and accuracy are **not** proper. Train/select on a proper rule; AUC measures discrimination only. "Strictly proper" is the property that reporting your honest belief is the uniquely optimal strategy, which is exactly what you want from a training objective.
- **Prevalence-dependence is a feature of precision** (it's the posterior $P(y{=}1\mid\hat y{=}1)$); choose ROC vs PR by whether the decision depends on the *prior* (PR) or only the *separability of the class-conditionals* (ROC).
- **Threshold = expected-cost minimization** ($t^\star=c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}})$ on the calibrated posterior); metric choice is decision theory. The argument about which metric to report is really an argument about a loss function nobody wrote down.
- **Calibration ⊥ ranking:** monotone recalibration (Platt/isotonic/temperature) changes log-loss/Brier but not AUC — the rigorous rebuttal to "high AUC = good model."

- **Prior correction is the headline result** — resampling changes the effective base rate, so predicted probabilities are biased by a known log-odds offset; the King–Zeng intercept correction recovers the true posterior. This is the same machinery as **case-control sampling** in epidemiology and **importance weighting** (c1.1: oversampling the minority = a proposal $q$; weights $p/q$ restore unbiased gradients). Case-control sampling is exactly this move: collect the rare cases at a deliberately inflated rate, then correct the intercept afterwards.
- **SMOTE's manifold assumption, as statistics** — interpolation assumes local convexity. In high dimensions it provably fails: Blagus & Lusa (2013) show SMOTE **leaves class means ~unchanged while shrinking within-class variance and inducing inter-sample correlation**, so it doesn't reduce majority bias for most classifiers; on categoricals it produces nonsense (→ SMOTENC). Averaging pairs of points is a variance-shrinking operation, which is why the synthesized cloud is tighter than the real one.
- **Calibration cost** — all corrections leave ROC-AUC ~unchanged (discrimination is a monotone-invariant rank statistic, c1.9) but RUS/ROS/SMOTE systematically **over-estimate minority probability** (van den Goorbergh 2022) — a reliability/Brier failure (c1.2's Murphy decomposition). The sensitivity "gain" is reproducible by threshold-shifting alone.
- **Threshold-vs-resample as expected-cost minimization** — estimate calibrated $p$, set $t^\star=c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}})$; resampling is then exposed as a prior shift that implicitly re-derives a threshold you should set explicitly.


## Worked decision walk-throughs

Four situations for the metrics half. Each one names the decision first and the metric second.

**Scenario A: 1%-positive fraud model.** Report **PR-AUC via `average_precision_score`** (not ROC-AUC — it hides the precision collapse), calibrate the scores, then set the threshold from the cost matrix ($t^\star=c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}})$).

**Scenario B: probabilities feed an expected-value pipeline.** Optimize/monitor **log-loss or Brier** and check a **reliability diagram** — AUC alone is blind to the calibration you depend on.

**Scenario C: you can only review the top 1% of flagged cases.** **Partial AUC** over the low-FPR region (`max_fpr=0.01`); the rest of the ROC curve is irrelevant to your operating budget.

**Scenario D: "model B's AUC is 0.91 vs A's 0.90."** Don't celebrate yet — use **DeLong's test** on the (correlated) AUCs to check the difference is real, not sampling noise.

And four for the imbalance half, in the same shape.

**Scenario A: 1:50 imbalance, you're using XGBoost.** **Don't SMOTE.** Train normally, **calibrate**, and **move the threshold** to the cost-optimal point (Elor 2022: for strong probabilistic learners with a tuned threshold, SMOTE gives no benefit). Evaluate with PR-AUC.

**Scenario B: you've decided SMOTE genuinely helps (weak learner, low-dim, validated).** Put it **inside an `imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline`** so it resamples only the training fold; never SMOTE-then-split.

**Scenario C: 1:10,000 fraud.** Reframe as **anomaly detection** (Isolation Forest / One-Class SVM) — too few positives to learn a boundary; or aggressively undersample negatives + ensemble.

**Scenario D: you must report honest performance.** **PR-AUC** (not ROC-AUC), **stratified** splits, calibrated probabilities, and the operating point's precision/recall at the cost-chosen threshold.


## Production failure modes

These are the ones that survive code review and show up in a postmortem.

1. **Shipping a high-AUC model uncalibrated.** Offline AUC looks strong; the 0.9-bucket is right 70% of the time; every downstream expected-value calculation is biased. Calibrate (c1.2) and monitor a reliability diagram.
2. **`auc(recall, precision)` for PR-AUC.** Trapezoidal PR-area linearly interpolates (invalid in PR space — Davis–Goadrich) and **over-estimates**; use **`average_precision_score`** (a non-interpolated step sum). The two calls look interchangeable in a notebook and are not.
3. **ROC-AUC on heavily imbalanced data.** Visually deceptive — small FPR changes hide huge precision collapses; use PR-AUC.
4. **Multiclass `'ovr'` AUC on imbalanced classes.** Sensitive to imbalance even with `average='macro'`; use `'ovo'` if you want imbalance-insensitivity.
5. **`log_loss` API drift.** The 2nd positional arg moved to `y_proba` (`y_pred` deprecated, removal in 1.11); pass `labels=` when not all classes appear.

6. **SMOTE before the split.** Leakage → inflated CV scores that collapse in production. Resample inside the pipeline/fold.
7. **Resampling breaks calibration.** Predicted probabilities over-estimate the minority; apply prior correction or recalibrate before using $p$ for decisions.
8. **SMOTE on categorical features.** Interpolating one-hots is meaningless; use SMOTENC.
9. **SMOTE in high dimensions.** Doesn't move class means, shrinks variance (Blagus–Lusa); often no benefit.
10. **ROC-AUC for imbalanced evaluation.** Hides the precision collapse; use PR-AUC (c1.9).
11. **`class_weight` + the mean-reduction gotcha** (c1.2): weighted losses can change the effective learning rate; tune accordingly.


## Interview gotchas

The questions, and the one-line answers that show you derived them rather than memorized them.

- **"Derive AUC = concordance."** $P(\text{score}_+>\text{score}_-)$, = $U/(n_+n_-)$ = Wilcoxon; it's a rank statistic.
- **"Why is ROC-AUC unaffected by class balance but precision isn't?"** Column- vs row-normalization; precision is the posterior, so it depends on the prior $\pi$.
- **"Imbalanced data — ROC-AUC or PR-AUC?"** PR-AUC / average precision (Saito–Rehmsmeier); show the 0.90→0.083 collapse.
- **"Can a model have great AUC and be useless?"** Yes — wrong operating point, or miscalibrated (AUC is monotone-invariant). Pair AUC with a proper scoring rule + cost-based threshold.
- **"`average_precision_score` vs `auc(recall, precision)`?"** AP is the non-interpolated step sum; the trapezoidal version over-estimates.
- **"How would you set the threshold?"** From the cost matrix: $t^\star=c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}})$ on a calibrated $p$; or Youden's J under equal costs.

- **"How do you handle class imbalance?"** First: does it actually hurt the *target metric*? If so, **calibrate + move the threshold** before resampling. Cite Elor 2022 (SMOTE's benefit vanishes for strong learners once you tune the threshold).
- **"Is SMOTE-then-train fine?"** Only inside a fold — SMOTE-then-split is leakage.
- **"Does resampling improve the model?"** It shifts the base rate → an implicit threshold move + worse calibration; correct the prior and you recover the original model.
- **"ROC-AUC or PR-AUC for imbalanced data?"** PR-AUC (c1.9) — ROC-AUC is prevalence-invariant and hides the operating-point collapse.
- **"Extreme imbalance (1:10⁴)?"** Reframe as anomaly detection; or undersample + ensemble.
