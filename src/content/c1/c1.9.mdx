## TL;DR

Model selection is **an estimation problem about an estimator.** Test error is a quantity you *estimate*; cross-validation is the *estimator*; and its own bias, its variance, and — crucially — the **selection bias you induce by optimizing on it** govern every choice in this lesson. The **bias-variance decomposition** tells you *what you're trading*; **cross-validation** tells you *how well you can measure the trade*; **double descent** tells you *where the classical trade stops being the whole story*. (CV *mechanics* — k-fold, stratified, group, temporal, nested — are in c1.1; here we treat CV as a statistical estimator and derive bias-variance from scratch.)

## What you'll be able to do after this lesson

- Derive the bias-variance decomposition from $y=f(x)+\varepsilon$ over the randomness of the training set, and say which assumption kills each cross term.
- Explain double descent and why it doesn't contradict the decomposition.
- Reason about CV's *own* bias (pessimistic, grows as $K$ shrinks) and variance (LOO high, via correlated folds), and why $K=5$–$10$ wins.
- Prove that selecting the hyperparameter that minimizes CV gives an optimistic estimate — and that nested CV fixes it.
- Explain why random search beats grid search in high dimensions (the "rule of 59" / effective dimensionality).
- Read a learning curve to decide whether more data, more capacity, or more regularization will help.

## Why "how do I validate?" is the deepest question in the chapter

> **Goal — see every "which model / which hyperparameter" decision as one question: how well can I estimate generalization, and what bias do I create by choosing on my estimate?** This is where data-snooping (c1.1) becomes a *statistical* phenomenon you can quantify, where regularization (c1.4) gets its bias-variance justification, and where the "more data / bigger model" instincts get stress-tested by double descent.

> **Vocabulary primer.** The "rusty staff engineer" assumption from c1.1 still holds.
>
> - **Risk / generalization error** — $R(\hat f)=\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[\ell(y,\hat f(x))]$; the test error you compute is a Monte-Carlo *estimate* of it.
> - **Bias / variance** — error of the *average* model across training sets vs. its sensitivity to *which* training set you drew.
> - **CV estimator** — $\widehat{\text{CV}}_K$, the average held-fold loss; itself a random quantity with bias and variance.
> - **Selection bias** — the optimism from reporting the *minimum* over many noisy candidate scores ("winner's curse").
> - **Interpolation threshold** — the capacity at which a model first fits the training data exactly.

## Part 1 — The bias-variance decomposition

> **Goal — derive, from scratch, the identity that names the entire trade.**

Data model: $y=f(x)+\varepsilon$, $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0$, $\text{Var}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2$, $\varepsilon\perp x$. The estimator $\hat f=\hat f_{\mathcal D}$ depends on a random training set $\mathcal D$; write $\bar f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[\hat f(x)]$. At a fixed test point $x$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D,\varepsilon}\bigl[(y-\hat f(x))^2\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\varepsilon+(f(x)-\hat f(x)))^2\bigr]=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2]}_{\sigma^2}+2\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]}_{0}\,\mathbb{E}[\cdots]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[(f(x)-\hat f(x))^2]$$

The cross term dies because $\varepsilon\perp\mathcal D$ and $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0$ — *that's* where zero-mean independent noise earns its keep. Now split the last term with $\pm\bar f(x)$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[(f-\hat f)^2]=\underbrace{(f(x)-\bar f(x))^2}_{\text{Bias}^2}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[(\hat f(x)-\bar f(x))^2]}_{\text{Var}}$$

(the second cross term vanishes because $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[\hat f-\bar f]=0$). So

$$\boxed{\ \mathbb{E}[(y-\hat f(x))^2]=\sigma^2_{\text{noise}}+\text{Bias}^2+\text{Var}\ }$$

- $\sigma^2$ — irreducible; if test error is near it, **stop tuning**.
- $\text{Bias}^2$ — how wrong the *average* model is; high for under-parameterized/over-regularized fits.
- $\text{Var}$ — sensitivity to the particular sample; high for flexible/under-regularized fits.

Sweeping complexity, Bias² falls and Var rises, so the sum is the classic **U-curve**. (Caveat: this clean additive split holds **only for squared loss**; for 0-1 loss the decomposition is loss-dependent and non-additive — Domingos 2000.)

## Part 2 — Double descent

> **Goal — know where the U-curve stops being the whole story.**

Past the **interpolation threshold** (roughly when #parameters $=n$, where the model first fits the training data exactly), test risk can **descend a second time** (Belkin et al. 2019). Mechanism: among the infinitely many interpolating solutions, the training procedure's inductive bias selects the **minimum-norm** (smoothest) one, and that norm keeps *decreasing* as capacity grows — an *implicit* ridge — so effective variance falls even at zero training error. This doesn't contradict Part 1: the decomposition is still exact; it's the **variance term** that, counterintuitively, comes *back down* in the over-parameterized regime. The classical U-curve is the *under-parameterized* story; modern over-parameterized models live on the second descent. (Nakkiran 2021 also shows *more data can transiently hurt* near the threshold.)

## Part 3 — Cross-validation, as an estimator

> **Goal — treat CV as a random quantity and reason about its bias, its variance, and the bias you create by selecting on it.**

**Its bias (choice of $K$).** Each surrogate trains on $n(1-1/K)$ points, so it's *weaker* than the final all-$n$ model ⇒ $\widehat{\text{CV}}_K$ is **pessimistically biased**, and the bias **grows as $K$ shrinks** ($K=2$ trains on half the data). LOO ($K=n$) trains on $n-1$ → nearly unbiased.

**Its variance.** LOO's $n$ training sets overlap in $n-2$ points ⇒ the fold errors are **highly correlated** ⇒ their average doesn't concentrate ⇒ **high variance**. Smaller $K$ → less-correlated folds → lower variance. So $K$ is itself a bias-variance knob *about the estimator*; **$K=5$ or $10$ is the empirical sweet spot** (Kohavi 1995 found 10-fold stratified beats LOO for *selection*). There's no unbiased estimator of $\text{Var}(\widehat{\text{CV}})$ (Bengio & Grandvalet 2004) — which is why people report a crude $\pm$ SE and use the **one-standard-error rule** (pick the simplest model within 1 SE of the best). (For least-squares linear models LOO is *free* via $\text{PRESS}=\sum_i (e_i/(1-h_{ii}))^2$ using the hat-matrix diagonal — so "LOO is expensive" doesn't apply there.)

**Selection bias (the winner's curse).** For a *single fixed* model, $\widehat{\text{CV}}$ is ~unbiased. But you report $\min_\lambda\widehat{\text{CV}}(\lambda)$, and by Jensen, $\mathbb{E}[\min_\lambda\widehat{\text{CV}}(\lambda)]\le\min_\lambda R(\lambda)$ — **selecting the argmin systematically under-estimates** the chosen model's true risk. With $m$ roughly-independent candidates of noise $\tau$, the optimism is $\approx\tau\sqrt{2\ln m}$. This is the *same statistical phenomenon* as tuning on the test set (c1.1 data-snooping), only milder. **Nested CV** fixes it: the inner loop selects $\lambda$, the outer loop (whose folds the selection never saw) estimates the risk of the *whole tuning procedure*.

> **Stop and think.** Your train and validation errors are both high and nearly equal. Will collecting more data help? *Hint: no — equal-and-high curves are the high-bias/underfit signature; the dominant term is $\text{Bias}^2$, a property of the model class, not the sample size. More data only closes a persistent train-val *gap* (high variance). The fix is more capacity / fewer constraints, not more rows.*

## Part 4 — Hyperparameter search

> **Goal — search the hyperparameter space efficiently, exploiting that few hyperparameters actually matter.**

**Random beats grid in high dimensions** (Bergstra & Bengio 2012). A grid of $v$ values over $d$ hyperparameters costs $v^d$ trials but probes only $v$ distinct values *per axis*; $n$ random points probe $n$ distinct values on *every* axis. If only $d_{\text{eff}}\ll d$ hyperparameters matter (usually true), the grid wastes its budget re-evaluating irrelevant axes while random search explores the important ones at full resolution. The probabilistic guarantee is **dimension-free**: $n$ random draws hit the top-$\beta$ region with probability $\ge 1-\gamma$ once $n\ge\frac{\log\gamma}{\log(1-\beta)}$ — for the top 5% at 95% confidence, **$n=59$** trials, regardless of $d$.

Beyond random: **Bayesian optimization** (fit a surrogate over tried configs, propose the point maximizing expected improvement — e.g. Optuna's TPE) and **Hyperband / successive halving** (Li et al. 2017 — start many configs at a small budget, keep the top $1/\eta$, multiply survivors' budget, repeat) for expensive training.

## Part 5 — Learning curves and information criteria

> **Goal — diagnose *which* term dominates, and select without a held-out set when you must.**

A **learning curve** (error vs training-set size) diagnoses the regime: **high bias** = train and val errors converge to a *high* floor (more data won't help → add capacity); **high variance** = a persistent train-val *gap* (more data or regularization helps). **Information criteria** select without held-out data via penalized likelihood: $\text{AIC}=-2\hat\ell+2k$ and $\text{BIC}=-2\hat\ell+k\ln n$. BIC's heavier penalty ($\ln n>2$ for $n>7$) makes it **consistent** (recovers the true model as $n\to\infty$ if it's in the candidate set); AIC is **efficient** (minimizes prediction risk) but can over-select.

## The stats / quant-research lens

- **The decomposition is the whole game** — derive it cold, state the squared-loss caveat, and connect "ridge accepts bias to cut variance" to c1.4's James–Stein (shrinkage dominates the MLE in dim ≥ 3).
- **CV error is an estimator with a sampling distribution** — three distinct effects (pessimistic bias from smaller folds, high variance from correlated LOO folds, optimistic *selection* bias from min-over-candidates); a staff-level answer separates all three.
- **Data-snooping = meta-level leakage:** running 100 CV configs and reporting the best is multiple testing; the number is an order statistic biased by $\approx\tau\sqrt{2\ln m}$. In quant this is **backtest overfitting** (Bailey & López de Prado's *deflated Sharpe ratio*); nested CV / a truly-held-out set is the only honest fix — the same root cause as "more factors mechanically raise in-sample $R^2$" (c1.4).
- **Double descent as statistics:** at $p=n$ the min-norm solution's variance spikes ($X^\top X$ ill-conditioned), then falls as $p\gg n$ because the pseudoinverse spreads and shrinks weight — an implicit ridge (the c1.4 SVD picture).

## Tradeoff matrix

| Search | Cost | Best when |
|---|---|---|
| Grid | $v^d$ | $d\le 2$–$3$, want reproducible axis coverage |
| **Random** | $n$ (dimension-free guarantee) | high-$d$, few hyperparameters matter (the default) |
| Bayesian (TPE/GP) | sequential | expensive evals, smooth response surface |
| Hyperband / SHA | multi-fidelity | training has a tunable budget (epochs/data) |

| $K$ | Bias of $\widehat{\text{CV}}$ | Variance | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2–3 | high (pessimistic) | low | quick/cheap |
| **5–10** | moderate | moderate | **default** |
| $n$ (LOO) | ~none | high (correlated folds) | small $n$; free for OLS via PRESS |

## Worked decision walk-throughs

**Scenario A: small dataset, you must tune hyperparameters AND report honest generalization.** **Nested CV** — inner loop tunes, outer loop estimates the tuned procedure's risk. Reporting `best_score_` from a single CV would be optimistically biased.

**Scenario B: 8 hyperparameters, only a couple matter.** **Random search** (or Bayesian) — grid wastes budget; ~60 random trials guarantee the top-5% region. 

**Scenario C: each training run is expensive (deep net).** **Hyperband / successive halving** — kill bad configs early at small budgets, promote survivors.

**Scenario D: is my model underfit or overfit?** Plot a **learning curve**: converged-high → bias (add capacity); persistent gap → variance (more data / regularization).

## Production failure modes

1. **Tuning on the test set / reporting the best of many CV runs as generalization.** Selection bias ($\approx\tau\sqrt{2\ln m}$) — use a held-out test set or nested CV (c1.1 data-snooping).
2. **Trusting `GridSearchCV.best_score_` as the generalization estimate.** It's optimistic by construction; it estimates the *selected* model on the *selection* data.
3. **Defaulting to LOO "because it's unbiased."** High variance + $n$ fits; $K=5$–$10$ usually better (except OLS, where PRESS makes LOO free).
4. **Grid search in high-dim.** Wastes budget on irrelevant axes; use random/Bayesian.
5. **Halving estimators import error.** `HalvingGridSearchCV`/`HalvingRandomSearchCV` are still experimental — you must `from sklearn.experimental import enable_halving_search_cv` first.
6. **Confused by negative scores.** sklearn negates losses (`neg_log_loss`, `neg_mean_squared_error`) so "higher is better"; `best_score_` being negative is expected.

## Interview gotchas

- **"Derive the bias-variance decomposition."** From $y=f+\varepsilon$ over $\mathcal D$; name which assumption kills each cross term; squared-loss-only caveat.
- **"Does more data always help?"** No — high-bias models plateau (converged-high learning curve). More data helps the variance regime.
- **"Is CV unbiased?"** For a fixed model ~yes (mildly pessimistic); after *selecting* on it, optimistic — nested CV estimates the tuned procedure.
- **"Grid or random search?"** Random in high-dim (rule of 59 / effective dimensionality, Bergstra–Bengio).
- **"Does bias always trade off with variance?"** Not past the interpolation threshold — double descent (Belkin 2019); the decomposition still holds, the variance term comes back down.
- **"LOO vs 10-fold?"** LOO ~unbiased but high-variance and expensive; 10-fold usually better for selection (Kohavi).
