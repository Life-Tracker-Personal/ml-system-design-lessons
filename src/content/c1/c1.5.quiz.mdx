**Q1.** A node has 8 class-A and 2 class-B examples. A candidate split sends $\{6A, 0B\}$ left and $\{2A, 2B\}$ right. Compute the **Gini** impurity decrease and the **entropy** information gain. Do they agree that this is a good split?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Gini.** Parent: $p_A=0.8$, $p_B=0.2$, $G = 1 - (0.8^2 + 0.2^2) = 1 - 0.68 = 0.32$.
- Left $\{6A,0B\}$: pure, $G_L = 0$.
- Right $\{2A,2B\}$: $G_R = 1 - (0.5^2 + 0.5^2) = 0.5$.
- Weighted child Gini: $\frac{6}{10}(0) + \frac{4}{10}(0.5) = 0.2$.
- **Gini decrease $= 0.32 - 0.2 = 0.12$.**

**Entropy** (bits). Parent: $H = -(0.8\log_2 0.8 + 0.2\log_2 0.2) = -(0.8(-0.3219) + 0.2(-2.3219)) \approx 0.722$.
- Left: pure, $H_L = 0$. Right: $\{2,2\}$, $H_R = 1$.
- Weighted child entropy: $0.6(0) + 0.4(1) = 0.4$.
- **Information gain $= 0.722 - 0.4 \approx 0.322$ bits.**

Both criteria give a positive decrease, so both agree it's a good split (it perfectly isolates 6 of the A's). In general Gini and entropy rarely disagree on the chosen split — both peak at $p=0.5$, where $G = 0.5$ equals half the entropy *in bits* — and Gini is cheaper (no logarithm).

**Rubric:**
- Parent Gini 0.32; weighted child 0.2; decrease 0.12
- Parent entropy ≈0.722; weighted child 0.4; gain ≈0.322
- Both positive → both favor the split
- Note Gini and entropy track closely (both peak at 0.5, where $G=0.5=H_{\text{bits}}/2$); Gini cheaper

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q2.** Derive the variance of an average of $B$ trees each with variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$. State the $B\to\infty$ limit. If a random forest cuts $\rho$ from 0.9 to 0.5 (with $\sigma^2$ fixed), by what factor does the large-$B$ variance fall?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Derivation.** For $\bar X = \frac1B\sum_b X_b$ with $\operatorname{Var}(X_b)=\sigma^2$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)=\rho\sigma^2$ for $i\ne j$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = \frac{1}{B^2}\left[\sum_b \operatorname{Var}(X_b) + \sum_{i\ne j}\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)\right] = \frac{1}{B^2}\left[B\sigma^2 + B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2\right]$$

$$= \frac{\sigma^2}{B} + \frac{B-1}{B}\rho\sigma^2 = \rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$$

**Limit.** As $B\to\infty$, $\operatorname{Var}(\bar X) \to \rho\sigma^2$ — a **floor set entirely by the correlation**. Adding trees only helps until you hit it.

**Factor.** The large-$B$ variance is $\rho\sigma^2$, so cutting $\rho$ from 0.9 to 0.5 multiplies it by $0.5/0.9 \approx 0.556$ — about a **44% reduction** in the variance floor. This is precisely why random forests inject per-split feature randomness: to drive $\rho$ down.

**Rubric:**
- Expand $\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)$ into variance + covariance terms
- Land on $\rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$
- $B\to\infty$ floor $=\rho\sigma^2$
- Factor $0.5/0.9\approx0.556$; connect to RF's purpose (lower $\rho$)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q3.** Derive the fraction of training rows left out of a single bootstrap sample as $n\to\infty$. For $n=1000$, how many rows are out-of-bag per tree, and how many *distinct* rows are in-bag? With 100 trees, roughly how many trees vote in a given row's OOB prediction?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Derivation.** A bootstrap draws $n$ rows with replacement. The probability a specific row is *not* picked in one draw is $1 - \frac1n$; over $n$ independent draws it's $\left(1-\frac1n\right)^n$. As $n\to\infty$:

$$\left(1 - \frac1n\right)^n \to e^{-1} \approx 0.368$$

So **~36.8% of rows are out-of-bag** per tree, and ~63.2% are in-bag.

**For $n=1000$:** $\approx 368$ rows OOB per tree; expected distinct in-bag $\approx n(1-e^{-1}) \approx 632$.

**With 100 trees:** each row is OOB for ~36.8% of trees, so ~**37 trees** never saw it and contribute to its OOB prediction. Averaging those gives the row's OOB estimate; doing this for all rows yields a near-free, CV-quality error estimate.

**Rubric:**
- $(1-1/n)^n \to e^{-1} \approx 0.368$
- ~368 OOB, ~632 distinct in-bag for $n=1000$
- ~37 of 100 trees score a given row OOB
- Note OOB ≈ free cross-validation

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q4.** Show that AdaBoost's stagewise coefficient comes from minimizing exponential loss, deriving $\beta = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\text{err}}{\text{err}}$. What is $\beta$ when $\text{err}=0$, $\text{err}=0.5$? Why does this make AdaBoost fragile to label noise?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Setup.** At stage $m$ we add $\beta G(x)$ to $f_{m-1}$, minimizing exponential loss over the (normalized) weights $w_i \propto e^{-y_i f_{m-1}(x_i)}$:

$$\sum_i w_i\, e^{-y_i \beta G(x_i)} = e^{-\beta}\!\!\sum_{y_i = G(x_i)}\!\! w_i + e^{\beta}\!\!\sum_{y_i \ne G(x_i)}\!\! w_i$$

Let $W = \sum_i w_i$ and $\text{err} = \frac{\sum_{\text{wrong}} w_i}{W}$. The objective is $W\!\left[e^{-\beta}(1-\text{err}) + e^{\beta}\,\text{err}\right]$.

**Minimize over $\beta$.** $\frac{d}{d\beta} = -e^{-\beta}(1-\text{err}) + e^{\beta}\,\text{err} = 0 \Rightarrow e^{2\beta} = \frac{1-\text{err}}{\text{err}} \Rightarrow \boxed{\beta = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\text{err}}{\text{err}}}$.

(Discrete AdaBoost often quotes the classifier weight $\alpha_m = \ln\frac{1-\text{err}}{\text{err}} = 2\beta$; the factor of 2 washes out under reweight-and-renormalize and doesn't change predicted signs.)

**Edge cases.** $\text{err}\to0 \Rightarrow \beta\to+\infty$ (a perfect learner gets infinite vote); $\text{err}=0.5 \Rightarrow \beta=0$ (a chance learner is ignored); $\text{err}>0.5 \Rightarrow \beta<0$ (flip it).

**Fragility to noise.** Exponential loss $e^{-yf}$ grows *exponentially* in the negative margin, so a persistently misclassified point (e.g. a mislabeled example) gets its weight blown up round after round, and the ensemble keeps contorting to fit it. That's why AdaBoost degrades under label noise, and why gradient boosting with a robust loss (or random forests) is preferred there.

**Rubric:**
- Split exp-loss into correct/incorrect sums; define err
- Differentiate, solve $e^{2\beta}=(1-\text{err})/\text{err}$ → $\beta=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\text{err}}{\text{err}}$
- Edge cases: err 0 → ∞, 0.5 → 0
- Exp-loss up-weights mislabeled points exponentially → noise fragility

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q5.** In gradient boosting, what does each new tree fit? Give the pseudo-residual for squared loss, MAE, and log loss. Why does plain gradient boosting with squared loss reduce to "fit the residuals," and why is a leaf's value the mean under squared loss but the median under MAE?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**What it fits.** Each tree fits the **negative gradient of the loss w.r.t. the current prediction** (the pseudo-residual), evaluated at $f_{m-1}$:

$$r_{im} = -\left[\frac{\partial L(y_i, f(x_i))}{\partial f(x_i)}\right]_{f = f_{m-1}}$$

This is gradient descent in function space: the tree approximates the steepest-descent direction.

**Specializations.**
- **Squared loss** $L=\tfrac12(y-f)^2$: $-\partial L/\partial f = (y-f) = r$, the **ordinary residual**.
- **MAE** $L=|y-f|$: $-\partial L/\partial f = \text{sign}(y-f)$.
- **Log loss** (binary, $f$ = logit, $p=\sigma(f)$): $-\partial L/\partial f = y - p$.

**Why "fit the residuals."** Because for squared loss the pseudo-residual *is* the residual $y-f$, so each round literally fits a tree to the current residuals and adds it — the classic intuition.

**Mean vs median leaf value.** After fitting the tree's structure, the leaf value is chosen to minimize the *actual loss* on that leaf's points. The constant $c$ minimizing $\sum(y_i - c)^2$ is the **mean**; the constant minimizing $\sum|y_i - c|$ is the **median** (the same mean-vs-median result as c1.2: squared loss → conditional mean, absolute loss → conditional median).

**Rubric:**
- Each tree fits the negative gradient (pseudo-residual)
- Squared → residual $y-f$; MAE → $\text{sign}(y-f)$; log → $y-p$
- L2 pseudo-residual = residual ⇒ "fit the residuals"
- Leaf value: mean for L2, median for L1 (argmin of the loss)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q6.** Derive XGBoost's optimal leaf weight $w_j^\star = -G_j/(H_j+\lambda)$ from its second-order objective. Show it reduces to the shrunk mean residual for squared loss. What goes wrong for MAE, and how does that connect to c1.2?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Objective.** A 2nd-order Taylor expansion of the loss around $f_{m-1}$, plus L2 leaf regularization, gives (per leaf $j$ with instance set $I_j$, $g_i, h_i$ the first/second derivatives):

$$\text{obj}_j(w_j) = G_j w_j + \tfrac12(H_j + \lambda) w_j^2, \qquad G_j = \sum_{i\in I_j} g_i,\ \ H_j = \sum_{i\in I_j} h_i$$

**Minimize.** $\frac{d}{dw_j} = G_j + (H_j+\lambda)w_j = 0 \Rightarrow \boxed{w_j^\star = -\dfrac{G_j}{H_j+\lambda}}$. Substituting back gives the structure score contribution $-\tfrac12 G_j^2/(H_j+\lambda)$, which drives the split-gain formula.

**Squared loss.** $L=\tfrac12(y-f)^2 \Rightarrow g_i = f - y_i = -r_i$, $h_i = 1$. So $G_j = -\sum_{i\in I_j} r_i$, $H_j = n_j$, and

$$w_j^\star = \frac{\sum_{i\in I_j} r_i}{n_j + \lambda} = \frac{n_j\,\bar r_j}{n_j+\lambda} = \frac{\bar r_j}{1 + \lambda/n_j}$$

— the **mean residual, shrunk** by the regularizer. With $\lambda=0$ it's exactly the mean residual, recovering plain gradient boosting.

**MAE.** $L=|y-f|$ has $h_i = 0$ almost everywhere (it's piecewise linear). Then $w_j^\star = -G_j/\lambda$ — it depends *only* on $\lambda$, and is undefined/degenerate at $\lambda=0$. The Newton leaf step has no curvature to work with. **This is the c1.2 connection:** naive MAE breaks XGBoost's second-order method (the "zero gradient" warning is really zero *Hessian*); the fix is `reg:pseudohubererror` (smooth, positive Hessian) or the special-cased `reg:absoluteerror` (a constant Hessian for split-finding, plus an adaptive leaf-update that sets each leaf to ~the median of its residuals).

**Rubric:**
- Per-leaf objective $G_j w + \tfrac12(H_j+\lambda)w^2$
- Minimize → $w^\star = -G_j/(H_j+\lambda)$
- Squared loss: $g=f-y$, $h=1$ → shrunk mean residual; $\lambda=0$ → mean (= plain GB)
- MAE: $h=0$ → degenerate; c1.2 tie (zero Hessian; pseudo-Huber / reg:absoluteerror)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q7.** Construct a learner for which bagging yields essentially zero improvement, and explain why using the decorrelation identity. Bonus: why might bagging even slightly *increase* its error?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The learner.** Any **stable, low-variance, high-bias** learner — e.g. **ordinary linear/OLS regression** (or LDA, or large-$k$ kNN). Fit on different bootstrap resamples, OLS produces nearly identical coefficients, so the bagged models are almost perfectly correlated: $\rho \approx 1$.

**Why, via the identity.** Bagging's variance is $\rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$. With $\rho\approx1$ this is $\approx\sigma^2$ for any $B$ — the $\frac{1-\rho}{B}$ term is ~0, so averaging buys nothing. Bagging reduces *variance*, and a stable learner has little variance to remove; it does nothing for the *bias* that dominates such a learner's error. (Bagging helps unstable, high-variance learners — deep trees, small-$k$ kNN — which is exactly why trees are the canonical bagging base learner.)

**Bonus — why it can hurt.** Each bootstrap contains only ~63% distinct rows, so each base model is trained on effectively less data than the full set → slightly higher bias/variance *per model*. For a stable learner, averaging near-identical slightly-worse models can't compensate, so bagged error can edge *above* the single full-data fit.

**Rubric:**
- Pick a stable high-bias learner (OLS / LDA / large-$k$ kNN); note $\rho\approx1$
- Apply identity: $\rho\approx1$ → variance $\approx\sigma^2$ regardless of $B$ → no gain
- Bagging removes variance, not bias; stable learners are bias-dominated
- Bonus: ~63% distinct rows → each base model slightly worse → can increase error

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q8.** You add 5,000 estimators to both a random forest and a gradient-boosted model. One overfits (test error rises); one doesn't. Which, and prove it. As a corollary, why does a random forest want *deep* trees while GBDT wants *shallow* ones?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**GBDT overfits; RF does not.**

**RF (doesn't).** The trees are i.i.d. draws from the same randomized procedure (bootstrap + feature subsampling). Averaging $B$ such trees, the generalization error converges almost surely as $B\to\infty$ (a strong-law-of-large-numbers result — Breiman 2001): more trees drive the Monte Carlo averaging error to zero and the error settles at a fixed limit (the $\rho\sigma^2$-floor regime). Adding trees can only *reduce* the averaging variance — it never increases test error. So RF needs no early stopping.

**GBDT (does).** Boosting is *sequential* and *adaptive*: each round fits the negative gradient of the residual loss, monotonically reducing training loss. Past the point where the signal is captured, additional rounds fit noise — training loss keeps falling while test loss turns up. So more rounds overfit, and early stopping on a validation set is essential.

**Corollary (tree depth).** RF reduces **variance** by averaging, so it wants each tree to be **low-bias** → grow **deep** trees (their high variance is washed out by averaging). GBDT reduces **bias** by adding many small corrections, so each tree should be a **weak, high-bias** learner → **shallow** trees (depth controls the interaction order; deep trees + boosting overfit immediately).

**Rubric:**
- GBDT overfits, RF doesn't
- RF: i.i.d. trees → LLN convergence → more trees never hurt (Breiman)
- GBDT: sequential bias reduction → eventually fits noise → early stop
- Corollary: RF deep (low-bias to average), GBDT shallow (weak learners)

</details>

</Quiz>

<Quiz id="9">

**Q9.** An engineer trains stacking base models on the full training set, predicts on that same set to build meta-features, and trains the meta-learner on them — reporting excellent training metrics. Identify the leak, explain why a 1-NN base model makes it catastrophic, and describe the fix.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The leak.** Building meta-features from base models' **in-sample** predictions is train/test leakage. Each base model has already seen the rows it's now predicting, so its meta-features reflect *memorization*, not generalization. The meta-learner then learns weights tuned to optimistic, in-sample base predictions that won't hold at test time.

**Why 1-NN makes it catastrophic.** A 1-nearest-neighbor base model has **zero training error** — predicting on the training set, every point's nearest neighbor is itself, so its meta-feature equals the true label exactly. The meta-learner sees a "base model" that's perfectly correct and hands it ~all the weight. At test time the 1-NN generalizes far worse, and the stack collapses. (Any low-bias/high-variance base model produces the same pathology in milder form.)

**The fix — out-of-fold (OOF) predictions.** Split the training data into $K$ folds. For each base model, produce each row's meta-feature from a copy trained on the *other* $K-1$ folds (so it never saw that row). Train the meta-learner on these honest OOF meta-features. Then refit each base model on all the data for inference. (This is the c1.1 leakage principle: a feature must be computable without the row's own label.) "Blending" is the simpler one-held-out-set variant.

**Rubric:**
- Identify in-sample meta-features as train/test leakage
- 1-NN has zero training error → its meta-feature = the label → meta over-weights it → collapse
- Fix: out-of-fold (K-fold) meta-features; refit base models on all data for inference
- Tie to c1.1 (feature must not use the row's own label)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q10.** A random forest's impurity (MDI) importance ranks a useless 1000-cardinality ID column #1, above a genuinely predictive binary feature. Explain the mechanism, and say what you'd use instead — including why that alternative is *also* unreliable under feature correlation.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Mechanism.** Impurity (Gini/MDI) importance sums the impurity decrease attributed to each feature across all splits. A high-cardinality feature offers **many candidate cut-points**, so by sheer multiple comparisons it can find a split that reduces impurity *on the training data* by chance — even with no real signal (Strobl et al. 2007). Combined with the fact that MDI is computed **on training data** (so it rewards overfitting), a noise ID column with 1000 distinct values can out-rank a real binary feature. This is a bias of the metric, not a finding about the data.

**Use instead — permutation importance on held-out data.** Permute one feature's values in a validation set and measure the drop in performance; an irrelevant feature causes no drop, regardless of cardinality. Because it's measured on held-out data against actual performance, it's not fooled by cardinality or training-set overfitting.

**Why permutation is also unreliable under correlation.** If two features are collinear, permuting one barely hurts performance because the model recovers the same information from its correlated partner — so *both* look unimportant even if the underlying signal is crucial. (Same redundancy that defeats drop-column leakage detection in c1.1 and inflates VIF in c1.4.) The fix is to **cluster correlated features and permute the cluster jointly**, or use conditional/grouped permutation importance.

**Rubric:**
- MDI bias: high-cardinality → many cut-points → spurious impurity decrease (Strobl 2007); computed on train data
- Use permutation importance on held-out data
- Permutation also fails under collinearity (partner carries the info) → both look unimportant
- Fix: cluster/permute correlated features jointly (c1.1/c1.4 tie)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q11.** Sketch the reliability (calibration) curves you'd expect from an out-of-the-box random forest and from AdaBoost. Explain the mechanism behind each distortion, and name the standard fixes (and which needs more data).

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Both are sigmoid-distorted — under-confident at the extremes.** The counterintuitive part: RF *and* AdaBoost push their probabilities *toward* 0.5 (away from 0 and 1), so both have the same qualitative reliability shape, for different reasons (Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana 2005).

**Random forest.** RF probabilities are the *average* of many trees' bounded $[0,1]$ votes. Averaging bounded values compresses the result toward the middle, so the forest rarely outputs values near 0 or 1 — for a prediction of, say, 0.8 the empirical accuracy is *higher* than 0.8. The reliability curve is sigmoidal, lying above the diagonal at the high-confidence end: under-confident at the extremes.

**AdaBoost.** AdaBoost maximizes margins; mapping its margin scores to $[0,1]$ pushes the probability mass *away* from 0 and 1, so its predictions are also too moderate — again a sigmoidal curve, under-confident at the extremes (*not* over-confident, despite the exponential loss).

**Fixes.** Post-hoc calibration on a held-out set: **Platt scaling** (fit a logistic/sigmoid to the scores) or **isotonic regression** (fit a monotonic step function). Wrap with `CalibratedClassifierCV`. **Isotonic is more flexible but needs more data** (it can overfit on small calibration sets, where Platt's two-parameter sigmoid is safer). Verify with a reliability diagram + ECE/Brier (c1.3).

**Rubric:**
- BOTH RF and AdaBoost are under-confident at the extremes (sigmoid curves; probabilities pushed toward 0.5) — Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana 2005
- RF mechanism: averaging bounded tree votes compresses toward the middle; AdaBoost mechanism: margin maximization pushes mapped scores away from 0/1
- Fixes: Platt (sigmoid) / isotonic on held-out data
- Isotonic needs more data (overfits small sets); Platt safer when small
- (c1.3 tie: reliability diagram, ECE, Brier)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q12.** You call `RandomForestRegressor()` in current scikit-learn expecting the textbook random forest with $m=p/3$ features per split. What actually happens, and how does it change the model's variance? Bonus: what is XGBoost's default `tree_method` since v2.0?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**What actually happens.** Modern scikit-learn's `RandomForestRegressor` defaults to **`max_features=1.0`** — i.e. it considers **all $p$ features at every split**. With no per-split feature randomness, the only randomization left is bootstrapping, so you've actually built **bagged trees, not a random forest**. (The classifier still defaults to `max_features="sqrt"`; the regressor's `1.0` default is the trap — it changed from `"auto"` in the 1.x line.)

**Effect on variance.** Considering all features at each split makes the trees pick the same dominant splits, so they're **more correlated** — $\rho$ is higher. By the decorrelation identity, the large-$B$ variance floor $\rho\sigma^2$ is **higher**, so the ensemble has more variance (worse generalization) than a true RF would. **Fix:** set `max_features="sqrt"` or `max_features=1/3` to restore per-split feature subsampling and decorrelate the trees.

**Bonus.** Since **XGBoost 2.0**, the default `tree_method` is **`"hist"`** (histogram-based split finding — fast, the former LightGBM-style approach), replacing the older auto-selection of exact/approximate methods. (Also: the unified `device="cpu"/"cuda"` parameter replaced `gpu_hist`.)

**Rubric:**
- `max_features=1.0` default (regressor) → all features per split → **bagged trees, not RF**
- Higher $\rho$ → higher variance floor $\rho\sigma^2$ → worse than a true RF
- Fix: `max_features="sqrt"` or `1/3`
- Bonus: XGBoost ≥2.0 default `tree_method="hist"`

</details>

</Quiz>

## Rapid-fire: trees, bagging & random forests

Additional interview-calibrated questions — the ones that tend to separate candidates at top-tier ML interviews (Anthropic, Meta, frontier startups, quant funds). No model answers on purpose: if you can't answer one cleanly, out loud, in ~2 minutes, that's the gap to close. Several bundle multiple sub-questions; a strong answer hits every part.

1. **Greedy is blind.** Construct a small dataset (XOR is the classic) on which *no* single axis-aligned split reduces impurity, yet a depth-2 tree is perfect. What does this reveal about CART's greedy search — is the "deep trees have near-zero bias" claim a statement about the hypothesis class or about the algorithm that searches it, and when does the gap between the two bite in practice?
2. **The axis-aligned tax.** The true decision boundary is the diagonal $x_1 + x_2 = 1$ on the unit square. What does a tree's fitted boundary look like, and roughly how does the number of leaves needed for error $\varepsilon$ scale? Contrast with a single oblique split $w^\top x = c$. Then defend the status quo: why do mainstream libraries still ship axis-aligned splits only — hit the compute, invariance-to-monotone-feature-transforms, and interpretability arguments — and what preprocessing partially buys obliqueness back?
3. **Trees cannot extrapolate.** Show that any tree ensemble predicts a *constant* along any direction leaving the training features' range — and that an RF regression prediction can never leave $[\min_i y_i, \max_i y_i]$. Quantify the failure: fit $y = t$ on $t \in [0, 100]$, predict at $t = 110$. Give two production fixes (target detrending/differencing, linear-model leaves or a linear+GBDT hybrid) and say why a quant forecasting pipeline must design around this.
4. **Variance with respect to what?** In $\rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$, over *which* randomness are $\sigma^2$ and $\rho$ defined — the bootstrap and feature subsampling given a fixed training set, the draw of the training set itself, or both? Show that as $B \to \infty$ the forest converges to the *expected* randomized tree, conclude that averaging removes only the randomization variance, and explain why bagging can therefore never beat the ideal aggregated predictor it is Monte-Carlo-estimating.
5. **The $m$ dial on wide data.** With the large-$B$ error $\approx \text{bias}^2 + \rho\sigma^2$, explain mechanistically why shrinking `max_features` $m$ lowers $\rho$ but raises per-tree bias. Now construct the failure: 2 relevant features among $p = 10{,}000$ noise features, $m = \sqrt{p} = 100$ — compute the probability a given split considers *any* relevant feature, and state what this implies for tuning $m$ on wide, sparse-signal data (genomics, alpha signals).
6. **The forest is a kernel.** Show that an RF regression prediction can be written $\hat f(x) = \sum_i w_i(x)\, y_i$ with $w_i(x) \ge 0$, $\sum_i w_i(x) = 1$ — an *adaptive nearest-neighbor* smoother (Lin & Jeon 2006). What determines the weights, which earlier facts does this view explain for free (no extrapolation, why RF smooths label noise), and what does it enable that point predictions don't — i.e. how do quantile regression forests get a full conditional distribution from the same weights?
7. **Vote or average?** For an RF classifier, hard majority vote over tree predictions and the argmax of averaged tree *probabilities* can disagree — construct a 3-tree, 2-class example where they do. Which one does scikit-learn's `predict` actually implement, and which would you feed into a downstream expected-value decision (and why does that one still need calibration per Q11)?
8. **Is the bootstrap load-bearing?** Replace the bootstrap ($n$ draws with replacement) with subsampling $63.2\%$ of rows *without* replacement. Argue the two are nearly equivalent for tree fitting, state what changes about duplicated rows and the OOB machinery, and conclude what is actually essential to bagging — the bootstrap specifically, or randomization in general. Bonus: which sampling scheme does stochastic gradient boosting (Friedman 2002) use, and why?
9. **OOB's fine print.** The $1/e$ result makes OOB error look like free cross-validation. Break the equivalence twice: (a) show that at small $B$ each row's OOB prediction averages only $\approx B/e$ trees, so OOB evaluates a *smaller* ensemble — which direction is that bias? (b) show that with near-duplicate rows or grouped data (multiple rows per patient/user), a row's "unseen" twin was in-bag — which direction now, and how does this reprise c1.1's grouped-split leakage?
10. **"Random forests don't overfit" — attack the slogan.** Breiman proved the generalization error *converges* as $B \to \infty$. Show that convergence in $B$ says nothing about the *level* converged to: describe a regime (deep trees, small noisy dataset) where the infinite forest is still badly overfit, and reconcile that with the practical advice "don't prune RF trees." Distinguish three separate dials: overfitting in $B$, in tree depth, and the $\rho\sigma^2$ floor.

## Rapid-fire: boosting, stacking & the tabular frontier

Same format — no answers, ~2 minutes each.

1. **The gradient step you never take.** "Gradient boosting is gradient descent in function space" — first make that precise, then break it: the negative gradient is an $n$-vector of pointwise values, not a function you can add; the tree performs a least-squares *projection* of that vector onto the base-learner class; and Friedman's TreeBoost then *re-solves* each leaf's value by a per-leaf line search on the actual loss. Which parts of the plain gradient-descent analogy survive, and what concretely goes wrong for MAE if you skip the leaf-wise line search and just add the fitted tree?
2. **Why Newton?** Derive from XGBoost's second-order Taylor expansion that each tree solves a *weighted* least-squares problem — targets $-g_i/h_i$ with weights $h_i$. For log loss ($h_i = p_i(1-p_i)$), what do those weights do to confidently-classified points, and why is that the right behavior? Name a loss for which Newton boosting collapses to first-order gradient boosting exactly.
3. **Boosting that refuses to overfit — reconcile.** AdaBoost's test error can keep *falling* for thousands of rounds after training error hits zero — the margin-maximization explanation (Schapire et al. 1998) — yet this lesson says "more rounds overfit, always early-stop." Reconcile the two stories: what roles do label noise, weak-learner capacity, and the choice of loss play in deciding which one you observe? Is "GBDT overfits in rounds" a theorem or an empirical regularity?
4. **Shrinkage is not just slow.** $\nu = 0.05$ with 4,000 rounds routinely beats $\nu = 0.5$ with 400 rounds *at matched training loss*. Explain the mechanism — why smaller steps trace a better regularization path through additive-model space — the interplay with early stopping, and the compute bill you pay. Why must `learning_rate` and `n_estimators` be tuned jointly rather than independently?
5. **Target encoding: leakage by construction.** You replace a 50,000-level categorical with the per-level training-set target mean. Explain quantitatively why a pure-noise ID column becomes a near-perfect *training* predictor (what does the encoding equal for a level with one row?). Show that leave-one-out encoding is still leaky — write what $x^{\text{enc}}_i$ equals as a function of $y_i$ for a 2-row level and say what a tree does with that. Then explain CatBoost's *ordered* target statistics and the "prediction shift" they remove.
6. **The $2^{K-1}-1$ subsets trick.** Finding the optimal binary partition of a $K$-level categorical naively requires searching $2^{K-1}-1$ subsets. State the classical result (Fisher 1958; ESL §9.2.4) that for squared loss — and binary classification — sorting levels by their mean target reduces the search to $K-1$ ordered splits, *exactly*. Sketch why it works, say where it fails (multiclass), and explain why one-hot encoding instead cripples trees: one level per split, depth and importance dilution, and gain starvation at high cardinality.
7. **Monotonic constraints, mechanically.** How does a GBDT actually enforce "output non-decreasing in $x_j$" — what does it check at each candidate split on $x_j$, and what bound propagates to the children's leaf values? What do you pay in bias when the constraint is (locally) wrong? Why do quant and credit shops insist on it anyway — hit the economic-prior, regulatory/explainability, and adversarial-robustness arguments.
8. **When do nets win on tabular?** Grinsztajn et al. 2022 give three mechanisms for GBDT's tabular dominance — state all three. Then construct the reversal: a tabular task where a neural net *should* win (think: enormous $n$, smooth rotation-invariant targets, high-cardinality categoricals wanting learned embeddings, multimodal side-inputs, transfer from pretraining). For each of the three mechanisms, say whether it's a property of the data or of the inductive bias.
9. **Leakage that survives OOF.** Your stack uses honest out-of-fold meta-features, yet the reported CV score is still optimistic. Find at least three remaining leaks: base-model hyperparameters tuned on the same folds, GBDT base learners early-stopped on the very fold they OOF-predict, preprocessing or target encoding fit on the full data before splitting, and the same CV reused to select the meta-model. What does the fully honest protocol (nested CV) cost, and when is that price worth paying?
10. **Missing values as signal — and as leak.** XGBoost and LightGBM learn a per-split *default direction* for missing values from the training gradients. Explain the mechanism and why it beats mean-imputation for trees. Then the trap: when missingness is informative because of how the data was *collected* — a lab test ordered only for already-sick patients, a field populated only after the outcome — the learned default direction encodes exactly the c1.1 kind of leakage. How would you detect that before shipping?
