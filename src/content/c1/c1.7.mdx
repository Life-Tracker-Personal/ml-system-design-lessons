## TL;DR

Clustering looks like a grab-bag of algorithms. It isn't. Every one of them is **a probabilistic generative model plus a hardening choice**, and once you see that, the keystone fact follows: **k-means is the hard-EM, infinite-precision (zero-variance), isotropic-shared-covariance limit of a Gaussian mixture**. Take that model seriously and you no longer have to memorize k-means's assumptions, failure modes, or the "how do I pick $k$?" advice. They all fall out of the model.

The second half of the lesson is the dual reframe, for visualization. **t-SNE and UMAP optimize a divergence between neighbor-*probability* distributions, not a metric.** Nothing in either objective pins down a distance. So their axes, their cluster sizes, and the gaps between their clusters are optimization artifacts, not data. (Linear, variance-preserving PCA — with interpretable axes and faithful distances — is in c1.3; this lesson is clustering + the nonlinear embeddings.)

## What you'll be able to do after this lesson

- Write the k-means objective, prove Lloyd's algorithm converges (to a *local* min), and explain why k-means++ and `n_init` exist.
- Derive the GMM E/M steps and prove EM never decreases the likelihood (the ELBO/Jensen argument).
- Show **k-means is the zero-variance hard-EM limit of a GMM**, and read off all three k-means assumptions from it.
- Explain why $J(k)$ is monotone in $k$ — so you must pick $k$ with silhouette / the gap statistic / BIC, not inertia.
- Say when DBSCAN/HDBSCAN beats k-means, and what density-based clustering buys over centroid-based.
- Explain *mechanistically* why t-SNE/UMAP axes, cluster sizes, and distances are meaningless — and how low perplexity manufactures fake clusters.

## Why unsupervised structure is its own skill

> **Goal — find structure without labels, and understand the *assumptions* each method smuggles in.** Clustering and embeddings are where you reason about data with no ground truth, so the danger is reading structure that isn't there. Two facts carry most of the weight: k-means isn't assumption-free — it's a specific GMM in disguise — and a t-SNE plot's most eye-catching features (cluster sizes, gaps) are exactly the ones you must *not* trust.

Everything else in this chapter had a label to argue with. You fit a model, you check it against the held-out truth, and the data tells you when you are wrong. Here there is no held-out truth. k-means will run on any dataset you hand it, including pure noise, and it will return $k$ tidy clusters with centroids and a nice inertia number. Nothing in the output says "there was no structure here." That is the whole reason to care about assumptions: the assumptions are the only thing standing between you and a confident answer to a question the data never answered.

> **Vocabulary primer.** Five terms the rest of the lesson leans on.
>
> - **WCSS / inertia** — within-cluster sum of squared distances to centroids; k-means minimizes it.
> - **Latent variable** — an unobserved label (which cluster a point came from); EM infers a distribution over it.
> - **Responsibility** $\gamma_{ij}$ — the posterior probability that point $i$ belongs to component $j$ (a soft assignment).
> - **Perplexity** — t-SNE's "effective number of neighbors" per point: $2^{H}$, where $H$ is the Shannon entropy of that point's neighbor distribution.
> - **Embedding** — a low-dimensional representation that aims to preserve *neighborhoods* (t-SNE/UMAP), as opposed to *variance* (PCA, c1.3).

## Part 1 — k-means

> **Goal — minimize within-cluster variance by alternating assign/update, and know exactly what that buys and assumes.**

Start with the thing you would actually do by hand. You have a cloud of points and you want to say "these belong together." You pick a few landmark positions, hand each point to its nearest landmark, then slide each landmark to the middle of the points it collected. Repeat. That is the entire algorithm, and you just invented it without a formula.

Now write down what that procedure is trying to make small. Each point is some distance from the landmark it got handed to. Square those distances and add them all up. Small total means every point sits close to its landmark, which is what "belongs together" meant.

k-means minimizes the **within-cluster sum of squares**. Write the $n$ data points as $x_1,\dots,x_n$, let $C_j$ be the set of points handed to cluster $j$, and let $\mu_j$ be that cluster's centroid. Over a partition $\{C_1,\dots,C_k\}$ into $k$ clusters:

$$J=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{x_i\in C_j}\lVert x_i-\mu_j\rVert_2^2$$

Read it left to right. The outer sum walks the $k$ clusters. The inner sum walks the points that were assigned to cluster $j$. The term inside is the squared Euclidean distance from that point to its cluster's centroid. So $J$ is one number: the total squared "reach" of every centroid to its own members. Two knobs control it, and they are different kinds of thing. One is the *labels* (which cluster each point belongs to). The other is the *centroids* (where the $\mu_j$ sit). Nobody can minimize both at once in closed form. So you minimize one at a time.

**Lloyd's algorithm** alternates: **assign** each point to its nearest centroid, then **update** each centroid to its cluster's mean. Handing every point to its closest centroid carves the space into one region per centroid, each region holding the points nearer to that centroid than to any other. A partition of that kind is called a Voronoi partition. Its cells are always convex polygons, because each boundary is the perpendicular bisector between two centroids. Each step weakly decreases $J$ (assign minimizes over labels with $\mu$ fixed; update minimizes over $\mu$ with labels fixed, and $\partial_{\mu_j}\sum\lVert x-\mu_j\rVert^2=0\Rightarrow\mu_j=\overline{C_j}$, where the bar denotes the mean of the points in $C_j$), $J\ge0$, and there are finitely many partitions — so it terminates. **But at a *local* minimum:** it's coordinate descent, and different seeds give different answers.

That parenthetical is the whole convergence proof, so unpack it slowly. Freeze the centroids. The only way to make $J$ smaller is to move some point to a closer centroid, and the assign step already puts every point at its closest one, so no relabeling can help. Now freeze the labels. Within a single cluster, $J$'s contribution is a sum of squared distances to $\mu_j$; set the derivative to zero and the minimizer is the mean of that cluster's points, which is exactly what the update step writes down. So each half-step is an exact minimization over one block of variables while the other is held still. Neither can ever raise $J$.

Monotone decrease alone doesn't prove you stop. You also need the sequence to bottom out, and it does for two separate reasons. $J$ is a sum of squares, so it can never go below zero. And the algorithm's state is really just the partition, of which there are finitely many for $n$ points and $k$ clusters. A strictly decreasing walk through a finite set has to run out of places to go.

Here is what none of that gives you: a *global* minimum. Alternating minimization over blocks is coordinate descent, and coordinate descent stops when no single block can improve alone, which is not the same as no move at all improving. Concretely, two centroids can end up sharing one genuine blob while a second genuine blob has to make do with one centroid, and no single assign step or single update step can undo that. The seed decided it. (Under $L_1$ the optimal center would be the *median* — k-medians — the same loss⇒statistic point as c1.2.)

If bad seeds are the problem, the fix is to stop drawing seeds uniformly at random and start biasing them toward positions far from the centers you have already picked. Not deterministically farthest, though. Randomly, with the odds tilted by distance.

**k-means++** (Arthur & Vassilvitskii 2007) seeds centers with probability $\propto D(x)^2$, where $D(x)$ is the distance from point $x$ to the nearest already-chosen center, with a provable $O(\log k)$-competitive guarantee against the optimal WCSS *before* a single Lloyd step (the $\log k$ scaling is the load-bearing part; the leading constant is not). That's why you also run multiple restarts (`n_init`).

Note what that guarantee is and is not. It bounds the seeding, in expectation, against the best possible WCSS, and it holds before Lloyd's algorithm has run even once. It does not promise the final answer is near-optimal. Restarts are still the practical insurance: run the whole thing several times from different random seeds and keep the run with the lowest $J$.

That leaves the question of how many clusters to ask for, and it has a trap in it.

**Picking $k$ is not optimization.** Write $J(k)$ for the best WCSS achievable with $k$ clusters. $J(k)$ is **monotonically non-increasing** ($J(n)=0$) — a finer partition can always replicate a coarser one and split — so minimizing inertia trivially returns $k=n$ (the exact analogue of $R^2$ rising with features, c1.3). You need a complexity-aware selector: the **elbow** (heuristic kink), the **silhouette** $s(i)=\frac{b(i)-a(i)}{\max(a(i),b(i))}\in[-1,1]$ scored for each point $i$, with $a(i)$ its mean distance to its own cluster-mates and $b(i)$ its mean distance to the nearest other cluster (peak of the mean), or the **gap statistic** (Tibshirani 2001 — the only one with a principled null: compare $\log W_k$, the pooled within-cluster dispersion at $k$, to its expectation under uniform reference data).

Test that with the degenerate case, because it settles the argument in one line. Set $k=n$: give every point its own cluster. Each centroid lands exactly on its single point, every distance is zero, and $J(n)=0$. You cannot do better than zero. So "choose the $k$ that minimizes inertia" always answers "$n$," and it is useless. The general reason is that a $k+1$ partition can copy the best $k$ partition and then split one cluster in two, which can only lower $J$, so $J$ can never rise as $k$ grows.

The silhouette is a per-point verdict, not a global score. For point $i$, $a(i)$ is how far it sits, on average, from the other members of its own cluster, and $b(i)$ is how far it sits from the members of the nearest *other* cluster. The numerator $b(i)-a(i)$ asks "is your cluster closer to you than the runner-up?" Dividing by the larger of the two squashes it into $[-1,1]$. Near $+1$ means the point is comfortably at home; near $0$ means it is on a boundary; negative means it would be happier in the neighboring cluster. Average over all points and pick the $k$ where that mean peaks.

The gap statistic is the only one of the three that names a null hypothesis and compares against it. It takes $\log W_k$, your within-cluster dispersion at $k$, and compares it to what you'd get on uniform reference data with no cluster structure at all. The $k$ that beats featureless data by the widest margin wins. That is a different sort of answer from the elbow, which is a kink you eyeball on a curve.

## Part 2 — Gaussian mixtures and EM

> **Goal — replace hard assignments with soft, probabilistic ones, and reveal k-means as a special case.**

k-means makes every point commit. A point sitting exactly between two centroids gets handed to one of them, and the fact that it was a coin flip is thrown away. The fix is to stop assigning points to clusters and start assigning *fractions* of points to clusters. To do that you need a model that can say how likely each cluster was to have produced this point, which means writing down a generative story: pick a cluster, then draw a point from that cluster's Gaussian.

A GMM models density as $p(x)=\sum_{j=1}^k \pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)$, a weighted sum over the $k$ components indexed by $j$. *In one sentence:* softmax over Gaussian likelihoods, then re-fit each Gaussian on its softly-weighted points — repeat.

Take that density apart. $\pi_j$ is the prior probability of picking component $j$, and the $\pi_j$ sum to one. $\mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)$ is how likely component $j$ was to emit this particular $x$, given its center $\mu_j$ and its shape $\Sigma_j$. The sum is over "which component did it?" because you never saw which one it was. That unseen choice is the latent variable.

If you *had* seen the component labels this would be trivial: split the data by label and fit each Gaussian by maximum likelihood, done in one pass. You didn't see them. So you do the next best thing: guess the labels softly using your current parameters, fit as if those soft guesses were real, and repeat.

**EM** (Dempster, Laird & Rubin 1977) alternates:

- **E-step (responsibilities):** $\gamma_{ij}=\dfrac{\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}{\sum_\ell \pi_\ell\,\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid\mu_\ell,\Sigma_\ell)}$, where $\ell$ runs over all $k$ components.
- **M-step (weighted MLE):** $N_j=\sum_i\gamma_{ij}$; $\pi_j=N_j/n$; $\mu_j=\frac1{N_j}\sum_i\gamma_{ij}x_i$; $\Sigma_j=\frac1{N_j}\sum_i\gamma_{ij}(x_i-\mu_j)(x_i-\mu_j)^\top$.

The E-step formula is a prior times a likelihood, renormalized — which is Bayes' rule and nothing else. Numerator: prior times likelihood, the joint probability that component $j$ was chosen *and* emitted $x_i$. Denominator: the same quantity summed over all components, which is $p(x_i)$, so the fractions across $j$ add to one for each point. The result $\gamma_{ij}$ is the posterior probability that point $i$ came from component $j$. That is your soft assignment.

The M-step is the one-pass fit you would have done with real labels, with $\gamma_{ij}$ standing in for them. $N_j$ is the *effective* number of points in component $j$, which need not be a whole number. $\pi_j=N_j/n$ is that component's share of the data. $\mu_j$ is the weighted mean of every point, where each point's vote is weighted by how much it believes in component $j$. $\Sigma_j$ is the weighted covariance around that mean, which is what lets a component be a tilted, stretched ellipse instead of a ball.

That leaves the step most treatments wave at: why does looping these two steps improve anything?

**EM never decreases the likelihood.** Let $z$ stand for the unseen component label and let $q_i$ be any distribution over $z$ you care to invent for point $i$. For any $q_i$, Jensen gives $\log p(x_i)\ge\sum_z q_i(z)\log\frac{p(x_i,z)}{q_i(z)}=:\text{ELBO}$, the evidence lower bound, with gap exactly $\text{KL}(q_i\,\|\,p(z\mid x_i))$ — the Kullback-Leibler divergence from your $q_i$ to the true posterior over $z$. The E-step sets $q_i=p(z\mid x_i)$ (KL $=0$, ELBO touches $\log p$); the M-step raises the ELBO. So $\log$-likelihood is monotone non-decreasing — converging to a *local* max (the likelihood is non-concave, and is even **unbounded**: a component collapsing onto one point sends its standard deviation $\sigma\to0$, likelihood $\to\infty$ — hence `reg_covar`).

Walk that with the picture in mind. The thing you actually want to maximize, $\log p(x_i)$, has an awkward sum inside a log. So you introduce any distribution $q_i$ over the hidden component and rewrite that sum as an average taken under $q_i$. Since $\log$ bends downward, the log of an average is never below the average of the logs — that inequality is Jensen's — and it hands you a lower bound, the ELBO, that has the log inside the sum instead. The bound is not loose by a mystery amount. The gap is exactly $\text{KL}(q_i\,\|\,p(z\mid x_i))$, the divergence between your chosen $q_i$ and the true posterior. So two moves are available. Move the bound up until it touches the curve, which is the E-step choosing $q_i$ to be the true posterior and driving the gap to zero. Then push the bound itself higher by changing the parameters, which is the M-step. Since the bound touched $\log p$ before you pushed, and the bound is never above $\log p$, raising the bound has to drag $\log p$ up with it. That is why the likelihood is monotone non-decreasing.

Monotone is not the same as correct. The likelihood surface is not concave, so you land in a *local* max and the seed decides which one, the same story as Lloyd's algorithm. Worse, the surface has no top at all. Let one component park itself on a single data point and shrink: its variance goes to zero, the density at that one point goes to infinity, and the likelihood follows. That is not a good fit; it is the model finding a hole in the objective. The floor you add to the covariance diagonal, `reg_covar`, is the patch that stops it.

**k-means is hard-EM of a GMM.** Fix $\Sigma_j=\varepsilon I$ — one shared scalar $\varepsilon>0$ times the identity matrix $I$, so every component is a ball of the same radius — and make the $\pi_j$ equal. The responsibility $\gamma_{ij}=\frac{\exp(-\lVert x_i-\mu_j\rVert^2/2\varepsilon)}{\sum_\ell\exp(-\lVert x_i-\mu_\ell\rVert^2/2\varepsilon)}$ is a softmax with temperature $\varepsilon$; as $\varepsilon\to0^+$ it collapses to a **hard indicator** of the nearest centroid. The E-step becomes the assign step, the M-step becomes the centroid mean, and the scaled negative log-likelihood $-2\varepsilon\log p\to$ WCSS.

Turn the knob and watch. Set every covariance to $\varepsilon I$, a ball of the same radius for every component, and make all the mixing weights equal. Those two choices kill everything in the responsibility formula except the exponent, and what's left is a softmax whose "logits" are negative squared distances divided by $2\varepsilon$. Now shrink $\varepsilon$. At large $\varepsilon$ the softmax is warm and a point spreads its belief across several centroids. As $\varepsilon\to0^+$ the divisor blows up every gap between distances, and the nearest centroid takes all the mass. The soft assignment becomes a hard one. At that point the E-step is literally "assign each point to its nearest centroid," the M-step's weighted mean is literally "average the points in the cluster," and the scaled negative log-likelihood turns into the WCSS objective you wrote in Part 1. Same algorithm, arrived at from a model.

**This single fact names all three k-means assumptions:** $\Sigma_j=\varepsilon I$ ⇒ *isotropic (spherical)* clusters; *shared* $\varepsilon$ ⇒ *equal spread*; equal $\pi_j$ + squared distance ⇒ biased toward *equal-size* clusters. A full-covariance GMM relaxes all three (elliptical, unequal, soft) — and, being a likelihood model, it can pick $k$ by **BIC/AIC**, the Bayesian and Akaike information criteria, which score a fit by its log-likelihood minus a charge for every parameter it spends. Bare k-means cannot.

This is why the derivation is worth the trouble. You no longer memorize three bullet points about when k-means fails. You read them off the three modeling choices you made to get there. Every one of them is a choice a full-covariance GMM declines to make, and because the GMM is an honest likelihood model, it can also answer the $k$ question with penalized likelihood instead of an eyeballed kink.

> **Stop and think.** Run k-means with the *correct* $k=2$ on two concentric rings. Why does it fail even with perfect initialization, and which methods succeed? *Hint: k-means partitions space by straight-line perpendicular bisectors (a Voronoi tessellation), so it can only carve convex regions — it will slice each ring in half rather than separate inner from outer. It's the spherical-cluster assumption (from the GMM limit) breaking. DBSCAN (density-connected) or spectral clustering (which clusters in a graph/affinity space) recover the rings.*

## Part 3 — Density-based and hierarchical clustering

> **Goal — cluster by connectivity and density when "nearest centroid" is the wrong model.**

The rings example is not a corner case. It is what you get whenever a cluster is defined by *connectivity* rather than by a center: a road network, a river delta, a crescent of GPS pings. There is no useful "middle" of a ring, so an algorithm that describes clusters by their middles has nothing to work with. Change what a cluster *is*. Instead of "the points near this center," say "the points you can walk to without leaving a dense region."

**DBSCAN** (Ester et al. 1996) grows clusters from *core* points — those with at least `minPts` neighbors inside a radius $\varepsilon$, a different $\varepsilon$ from Part 2's covariance scale, since both spellings are standard and context separates them — via density-reachability, finds **arbitrary shapes**, needs **no preset $k$**, and labels low-density points as **noise** (`-1`). Its weakness is a single global $\varepsilon$: clusters of very different density can't both be captured. **HDBSCAN** (in sklearn since 1.3) varies $\varepsilon$ and extracts the most *stable* clusters — prefer it over hand-tuning DBSCAN's `eps`.

Three things fall out of that definition, and they are exactly the three things k-means could not do. Shape is unconstrained, because "reachable through dense neighbors" says nothing about convexity. You never state $k$, because the number of clusters is however many separate dense regions the walk discovers. And a point in no dense region is not forced into a cluster at all; it comes back labeled `-1`, which is an output k-means has no vocabulary for.

The cost is the knob. One global $\varepsilon$ has to be loose enough to connect your sparsest real cluster and tight enough not to bridge your densest two, and if those two demands conflict, no single value works. HDBSCAN's answer is to stop picking one: sweep the density threshold, watch which clusters survive across a wide range of it, and keep those. "Survives a range of thresholds" is what *stable* means here.

The other way to avoid committing to $k$ is to refuse to produce a single clustering at all, and instead produce all of them at once, nested.

**Agglomerative (hierarchical)** clustering merges the closest clusters bottom-up, producing a dendrogram you can cut at any height. Linkage choice matters: **single** (min distance — chains, bridges noise), **complete** (max — compact, outlier-sensitive), **average**, and **Ward** (merge that minimizes the increase in WCSS — the agglomerative analogue of k-means, requires Euclidean; sklearn's default).

Start every point as its own cluster, repeatedly merge the closest pair, and record the height at which each merge happened. But "closest pair of *clusters*" needs a definition once clusters hold more than one point, and that definition is the linkage. Single linkage measures cluster distance by the closest pair of members, so a single stepping-stone point between two blobs is enough to fuse them. That is both how it traces elongated shapes and how noise ropes real clusters together. Complete linkage uses the farthest pair, which keeps clusters tight and makes one distant outlier able to veto a merge. Average splits the difference. Ward doesn't measure distance between clusters at all; it asks which merge raises the within-cluster sum of squares least. That is the same quantity k-means minimizes, which is why it inherits both k-means's taste for compact blobs and its need for a Euclidean space.

## Part 4 — Nonlinear embeddings: t-SNE and UMAP

> **Goal — visualize high-dimensional structure by preserving *neighborhoods* — and learn exactly which features of the picture are real.**

*In plain English:* t-SNE and UMAP turn "who your neighbors are" into a probability distribution — one from the high-dim data, one from the low-dim layout — and shove the layout around until those two distributions agree. Neighbor lists are the data; the actual distances are thrown away.

Hold onto that last clause. Everything in a t-SNE picture that turns out to be untrustworthy is a direct consequence of it. The objective is written entirely in terms of who is near whom. If a quantity in the final picture is not determined by neighbor relations, then the objective never had an opinion about it, and whatever value it takes was decided by the optimizer.

**t-SNE** (van der Maaten & Hinton 2008) converts pairwise distances into neighbor probabilities $p_{ij}$, one for each ordered pair of points $i$ and $j$ (Gaussian, with a per-point bandwidth set by **perplexity** = effective neighbor count, typically 5–50), builds matching low-dim affinities $q_{ij}$ from a **Student-t (Cauchy)** kernel, and minimizes $\text{KL}(P\,\|\,Q)$, where $P$ and $Q$ collect all the $p_{ij}$ and all the $q_{ij}$ respectively. The heavy t-tail fixes the **crowding problem** (in 2-D there isn't room to honor all moderate high-dim distances), but it also means *a given affinity maps to no fixed display distance.*

The asymmetry between the two kernels is deliberate. High-dim similarities use a Gaussian whose width is tuned *per point* so that each point ends up with roughly the same effective number of neighbors, which is what perplexity sets. Low-dim similarities use a Student-t, which has a much heavier tail. That heavy tail exists to solve the crowding problem: a 2-D plane does not have enough room to place all the points that were at moderate distance in high dimensions, so something has to give. The t-kernel lets moderately-similar points sit much farther apart on the page without the objective complaining. Which is a fix and a warning at once. If a wide range of display distances all produce acceptable affinities, then the display distance is not carrying information.

**UMAP** (McInnes et al. 2018) builds a fuzzy-neighbor graph and minimizes a fuzzy-set binary cross-entropy, optimized with **negative sampling** — each update pushes a point away from a handful of randomly drawn non-neighbors instead of from every non-neighbor, which is the real source of its speed. A common myth says t-SNE "lacks repulsion" and that UMAP's repulsion buys it global structure — but *both* objectives' gradients have attractive *and* repulsive terms (Böhm et al. 2022), and UMAP's apparent global-structure edge is largely an **initialization** artifact: with matched PCA/spectral init the two are comparable (Kobak & Linderman 2021). That's exactly why PCA-init is now the t-SNE default.

That myth is worth naming explicitly because it is the most common thing people repeat about these two methods, and it is a claim about the gradients that you can check. Both gradients pull neighbors together and push non-neighbors apart. When you control for the starting layout by giving both methods a PCA or spectral initialization, the global-structure gap largely closes. The difference people were attributing to the objective was mostly a difference in where the optimization started.

**What you must NOT read off these plots** (Wattenberg et al., *Distill* 2016):
- **Axes are meaningless** — the objective is invariant to rotation/reflection/translation/scale of the output.
- **Cluster sizes are meaningless** — the per-point bandwidth normalizes local density away, so a dense and a sparse true cluster can render the same size.
- **Inter-cluster distances are meaningless** — the heavy-tailed (Cauchy) low-dim kernel and the neighbor-only objective mean gaps aren't metric.
- **Low perplexity manufactures clusters** — t-SNE on pure Gaussian noise shows "clumps" at small perplexity.

Each of those is a mechanism, not a caution. Take the axes: rotate, reflect, translate, or rescale the whole output and the objective's value does not change, so the optimizer had no reason to prefer the orientation it gave you. "The x-axis means something" would require the objective to distinguish one orientation from another, and it doesn't. Take cluster sizes: the per-point Gaussian bandwidth is tuned to hit the target perplexity, which means a point in a dense region gets a narrow bandwidth and a point in a sparse region gets a wide one. Density is normalized away by construction, so a tight true cluster and a diffuse one can come out the same size on the page. Take the gaps: the heavy Cauchy tail means large display distances are cheap, and the objective only ever looked at neighbors anyway, so the width of a gap between two clusters is not a measurement of anything. And the last one is the degenerate-input test that settles the whole argument. Feed in pure Gaussian noise, which has no clusters by construction, set perplexity low, and you will see clumps. The method will always give you a picture.

So treat t-SNE/UMAP as **hypothesis-generating local-neighborhood views**: sweep perplexity/`n_neighbors`, set a seed, and verify any "cluster" or "gap" against the original-space data or a metric method. This is the opposite of **PCA** (c1.3), whose axes are interpretable eigenvectors, whose distances are a faithful variance-preserving approximation, and which is deterministic and invertible.

## The stats / quant-research lens

- **EM is MLE with latent variables, made tractable by the ELBO** — build a bound that touches the objective from below at your current parameters, maximize the bound, repeat. That skeleton is called minorize-maximize, and it is the same one behind variational inference, VAEs, and HMM training. GMM responsibilities are posterior state probabilities (e.g. soft risk-on/risk-off regime weights).
- **GMM admits BIC/AIC** for choosing $k$ (penalized likelihood) — a *statistical* model-selection answer where k-means has only the geometric elbow/silhouette.
- **The curse of dimensionality kills distance-based clustering** (c1.8): pairwise distances concentrate, their coefficient of variation (standard deviation over mean) shrinking as $\text{CoV}\sim1/\sqrt d$ in the number of dimensions $d$ (Beyer 1999), so k-means/DBSCAN/silhouette all degrade — reduce dimension (PCA from c1.3, or a learned embedding) *before* clustering.
- **Why embeddings can't be trusted metrically:** both t-SNE and UMAP minimize a divergence between *neighbor-probability* distributions, invariant to global isometries/scaling of the output — so axes carry no units, the adaptive per-point bandwidth erases density, and the heavy tail erases distance. PCA is the interpretable, metric contrast.

The third bullet is the one that quietly invalidates the other two in practice. Restate the coefficient-of-variation claim in words: as dimension $d$ grows, the spread of pairwise distances shrinks relative to their mean like $1/\sqrt d$. All your points end up roughly equidistant from each other. Every method in this lesson except the embeddings is built on the word "nearest," and "nearest" stops meaning anything when the nearest and the farthest neighbor are within a few percent of each other. That is why dimension reduction comes first and clustering second, not the other way around.

## Tradeoff matrix

| Method | Finds | Needs $k$? | Cluster shape | Soft? | Picks $k$ how |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| k-means | spherical, equal-size | yes | convex/Voronoi | no | elbow/silhouette/gap |
| GMM (EM) | elliptical, unequal | yes | ellipsoidal | yes | **BIC/AIC** |
| Agglomerative | nested hierarchy | cut height | linkage-dependent | no | dendrogram |
| DBSCAN / HDBSCAN | arbitrary + noise | **no** | any density-connected | no | — (density) |
| t-SNE / UMAP | (visualization only) | n/a | — | — | — |

## Worked decision walk-throughs

The table tells you what each method is. These four scenarios tell you how to get from a dataset to a choice. Each one names the property of the data that forces the pick.

**Scenario A: well-separated, roughly spherical clusters; you know $k$.** **k-means** (with k-means++ and `n_init`). Fast, scales, and its assumptions are met. **Standardize features first** (distance-based).

Every assumption you derived from the GMM limit happens to hold here, so there is no reason to pay for anything fancier. The standardization note is not boilerplate. The objective is a sum of squared Euclidean distances, so a feature measured in dollars and a feature measured in fractions contribute wildly different amounts to $J$, and the large-scale feature silently decides the clustering.

**Scenario B: overlapping, elliptical clusters; you want soft membership or a density model.** **GMM** with full covariance — and use **BIC** to pick $k$. (You also get per-point responsibilities for downstream uncertainty.)

Two of the three k-means assumptions are broken here, elliptical and overlapping, which is exactly the pair full covariance plus soft responsibilities relaxes. The bonus is that the responsibilities are real posterior probabilities, so a downstream system can act on "this point is 60/40" instead of a bare label.

**Scenario C: arbitrary shapes, noise, unknown $k$ (e.g. spatial data).** **DBSCAN / HDBSCAN** — no $k$, handles non-convex clusters, isolates noise. Prefer HDBSCAN if densities vary.

Three separate requirements point the same direction. Unknown $k$ rules out k-means and GMM as a first pass. Arbitrary shapes rule out anything centroid-based. And "isolates noise" is a capability, not a side effect: you get a `-1` label instead of being forced to file every outlier under some cluster.

**Scenario D: visualize a high-dim embedding (e.g. 768-d sentence vectors).** **UMAP** (or t-SNE) — but reduce with **PCA first**, sweep `n_neighbors`/perplexity, seed it, and never report cluster sizes or gaps as findings.

At 768 dimensions the distance-concentration problem is live, so PCA first is doing real work rather than just saving time. The sweep is there because Part 4's perplexity figure showed one dataset producing four different stories. The seed is there because the optimization is stochastic. And the last clause is the one that gets violated in real decks.

## Production failure modes

1. **`KMeans(init='k-means++')` got noisier after a scikit-learn upgrade.** `n_init='auto'` (default since 1.4) means **`n_init=1`** with k-means++ — down from the old 10. Set `n_init=10` if you relied on the restarts.
2. **k-means on unscaled or non-spherical data.** It silently mis-clusters (the spherical/equal-variance assumption); standardize, or use GMM/DBSCAN.
3. **Reading t-SNE cluster sizes/distances as real.** They're artifacts; verify against the data.
4. **GMM singular-covariance collapse.** A component latches onto one point → likelihood → ∞. Keep `reg_covar > 0`; use multiple inits.
5. **Clustering in raw high dimensions.** Distance concentration makes "nearest" arbitrary; reduce dimension first.

The word doing the damage in number 2 is *silently*. Nothing errors, nothing warns, and you still get centroids and an inertia score. Same for number 5. These methods have no way to tell you their assumptions were violated, which is why the assumptions have to live in your head.

## Interview gotchas

- **"How does k-means relate to GMMs?"** It's the hard-EM, $\varepsilon\to0$, isotropic-shared-covariance, equal-weight limit — which is why it assumes spherical, equal-size clusters.
- **"How do you choose $k$?"** Not by inertia (monotone in $k$) — silhouette, gap statistic, or BIC for a GMM.
- **"Does EM find the MLE?"** A *local* max (likelihood non-concave, and unbounded via component collapse); it's monotone via the ELBO.
- **"What's wrong with reading a t-SNE plot?"** Axes, cluster sizes, and inter-cluster distances are meaningless; low perplexity fabricates clusters. Sweep perplexity, verify on data.
- **"DBSCAN vs k-means?"** DBSCAN: no $k$, arbitrary shapes, noise — but a single global $\varepsilon$ fails on varying density (use HDBSCAN).

Notice that the second half of each answer is the payoff. Anyone can state that k-means is a limit of a GMM. The answer that lands is the one that continues "which is why it assumes spherical, equal-size clusters," because it shows the assumptions were derived rather than recalled.
