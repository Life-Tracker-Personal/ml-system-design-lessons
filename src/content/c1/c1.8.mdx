## TL;DR

Support vector machines and k-nearest-neighbors are the two purest **geometry-as-the-model** algorithms: an SVM classifies by the *widest separating margin*, kNN by the *majority vote of the closest points*. Two ideas decide whether that geometry works. The **kernel trick** lets an SVM draw nonlinear boundaries by computing inner products in an implicit — sometimes infinite-dimensional — feature space *without ever materializing it*. The **curse of dimensionality** is the flip side: in high dimensions distances concentrate, so "nearest" stops being meaningful and kNN (and any distance method) degrades. Master the **margin** and the **neighborhood**, and you understand why these methods shine on small/medium data and why modern retrieval had to invent approximate nearest-neighbor search.

Naive Bayes is the **generative** member of a generative–discriminative *pair* whose discriminative twin is logistic regression (c1.3). Both produce the **same log-linear decision rule** $\log\frac{p(y{=}1\mid x)}{p(y{=}0\mid x)} = w^\top x + b$ — but NB estimates it by **counting** (maximum likelihood on the joint $p(x,y)$), while logistic regression estimates it by **conditional optimization** of $p(y\mid x)$. That single difference explains everything: why NB is a linear classifier, why it trains in one pass, why it **wins on tiny data but loses asymptotically** (Ng–Jordan), and why it **classifies well yet outputs garbage probabilities** (Domingos–Pazzani) — the calibration thread from c1.2/c1.6, in its purest form.

## Why these two, together

> **Goal — learn the two canonical geometry-based methods and the two ideas (kernels, the curse of dimensionality) that govern all distance-based learning.** SVMs teach the margin, duality, and kernels — the backbone of classical statistical learning theory. kNN teaches nonparametric estimation and the curse of dimensionality — which quietly underpins every modern vector database and RAG system. Both are interview staples precisely because they force you to reason geometrically rather than reach for `model.fit`.

These are the methods that dominated before deep learning, and they're not gone: linear SVMs are still strong baselines on high-dimensional sparse data (text), and kNN — in its approximate form — *is* how large-scale embedding retrieval works.

> **Vocabulary primer.** The "rusty staff engineer" assumption from c1.1 still holds.
>
> - **Margin** — the distance from the decision boundary to the nearest training point. SVMs maximize it.
> - **Support vector** — a training point that touches or violates the margin; only these determine the SVM.
> - **Kernel** $K(x,x')$ — a function that computes an inner product $\langle\phi(x),\phi(x')\rangle$ in some feature space, *without* computing $\phi$.
> - **Gram matrix** — the $n\times n$ matrix $G_{ij}=K(x_i,x_j)$; $K$ is valid iff $G$ is positive semidefinite.
> - **Lazy / instance-based learning** — no model is fit; "training" just stores the data, and all work happens at query time (kNN).

## Part 1 — Support vector machines

> **Goal — find the boundary that separates the classes with the widest possible margin, then bend it with kernels.**

### The maximum-margin hyperplane

Labels $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$, decision function $f(x)=w^\top x + b$. Fix the scale so the closest points satisfy $y_i(w^\top x_i+b)=1$ (the "canonical" hyperplane). The two margin hyperplanes are $w^\top x+b=\pm 1$.

**Margin width $=2/\|w\|$.** Take points $x_+,x_-$ on opposite margin planes: $w^\top x_+ + b = +1$ and $w^\top x_- + b = -1$. Subtract: $w^\top(x_+-x_-)=2$. The width is the projection of $x_+-x_-$ onto the unit normal $w/\|w\|$:

$$\text{margin} = \frac{w^\top(x_+-x_-)}{\|w\|} = \frac{2}{\|w\|}$$

Maximizing $2/\|w\|$ is minimizing $\tfrac12\|w\|^2$, giving the **hard-margin primal**:

$$\min_{w,b}\ \tfrac12\|w\|^2 \quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge 1\ \ \forall i$$

We maximize the *geometric* margin (the functional margin $y_i f(x_i)$ can be inflated for free by rescaling $w$ — only $/\|w\|$ makes it scale-invariant).

Amber circles: the support vectors — the only points that sit on the margin planes and the only points whose removal would change the boundary. Maximizing 2/‖w‖ (equivalently minimizing ½‖w‖²) is just the algebraic version of 'push the two dashed lines as far apart as you can while every point stays on the correct side.'" />

### Soft margin = hinge loss + L2 regularization

Real data isn't separable. Introduce slack $\xi_i\ge0$ and penalize it (Cortes & Vapnik 1995):

$$\min_{w,b,\xi}\ \tfrac12\|w\|^2 + C\sum_i \xi_i \quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge 1-\xi_i,\ \ \xi_i\ge 0$$

At the optimum $\xi_i=\max(0,\,1-y_i f(x_i))$ — exactly the **hinge loss**. Substituting gives the unconstrained form:

$$\min_{w,b}\ \tfrac12\|w\|^2 + C\sum_i \max\bigl(0,\,1-y_i f(x_i)\bigr)$$

**This is the bridge to c1.2:** an SVM is hinge loss plus L2 (ridge) regularization on $w$. Compare the surrogate losses as functions of the margin $m=y f(x)$: 0-1 loss is discontinuous and non-convex; **hinge** $\max(0,1-m)$ is convex and **exactly zero for $m\ge1$** (correctly classified beyond the margin → no gradient → that point drops out); **logistic** $\log(1+e^{-m})$ is smooth and *never* zero (every point contributes, which is why logistic regression has no support vectors but does give probabilities). The flat region of hinge is precisely *why SVMs are sparse in support vectors*.

### The dual, support vectors, and why kernels need it

Form the Lagrangian and set derivatives to zero: $w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i x_i$ and $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$. Substituting yields the **dual**:

$$\max_{\alpha}\ \sum_i\alpha_i - \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j\,y_iy_j\,\langle x_i,x_j\rangle \quad\text{s.t.}\quad 0\le\alpha_i\le C,\ \ \sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$$

The soft margin only adds the box upper bound $\alpha_i\le C$. By complementary slackness (KKT):

- $\alpha_i=0$: point is outside the margin, correctly classified — **not** a support vector, zero influence.
- $0<\alpha_i<C$: point lies *exactly on* the margin ($y_i f(x_i)=1$) — a **free support vector** (used to solve for $b$).
- $\alpha_i=C$: point is inside the margin or misclassified — a **bounded support vector**.

So the solution depends only on the support vectors — usually a small fraction of the data. **Why the dual matters:** both the objective and the recovered $f(x)=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\langle x_i,x\rangle + b$ touch the data *only through inner products* $\langle x_i,x_j\rangle$. That's what lets us swap in a kernel.

### The kernel trick

Replace every inner product with a kernel $K(x,x')=\langle\phi(x),\phi(x')\rangle$:

$$f(x)=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\,K(x_i,x)+b$$

**Mercer's condition:** $K$ is a valid kernel iff it is symmetric and its Gram matrix is positive semidefinite for every finite sample — then it *is* an inner product in some (possibly infinite-dimensional) space. Common kernels: linear $x^\top x'$; polynomial $(\gamma x^\top x'+r)^d$; **RBF/Gaussian** $\exp(-\gamma\|x-x'\|^2)$.

**The RBF kernel is infinite-dimensional.** Factor $K(x,x') = e^{-\gamma\|x\|^2}e^{-\gamma\|x'\|^2}\exp(2\gamma x^\top x')$ and expand the cross term $\exp(2\gamma x^\top x')=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(2\gamma)^k(x^\top x')^k}{k!}$ — every polynomial degree $0,1,2,\dots$ appears, so the feature map $\phi$ has infinitely many coordinates. You *cannot* materialize $\phi(x)$; you only ever evaluate the scalar $K$. **This is the whole point of the trick** — it buys an infinite-dimensional boundary at the cost of an $n\times n$ Gram matrix, not an infinite feature vector.

*Concretely:* RBF's implicit feature vector has one coordinate for every polynomial degree — and every mixed monomial within each degree — a countably infinite total. You couldn't write it down if you wanted to; the dual formulation lets you avoid ever having to.

<KernelLift1Dto2D caption="The kernel trick in one glance. Left: 1D data with red points inside [-1, 1] and blue points outside. No linear boundary (a single threshold) separates them — you'd need two thresholds, and linear classifiers can't do 'both'. Right: the same points lifted to (x, x²) via ϕ(x) = (x, x²). Blue points sit above y = 1; red below. Now one linear boundary (the horizontal line y = 1) does the job. Kernels let you do this without ever computing ϕ — inner products in the lifted space are the kernel." />

**The two knobs, in bias-variance terms.** Large $C$ penalizes slack harder → harder margin, fits training data closely → *less* regularization, higher variance (overfit). Large RBF $\gamma$ → each point's influence is very local → a wiggly boundary, high variance. Tune them jointly by cross-validation.

> **Stop and think.** Someone proposes "just compute $\phi(x)$ for the RBF kernel and run a linear SVM on it — same thing, simpler." Why is this impossible, and what's the practical cost you pay *instead* by staying in the dual? *Hint: $\phi$ has infinitely many coordinates, so it can't be stored; the price is the $n\times n$ Gram matrix and $O(n^2)$–$O(n^3)$ training — which is exactly why kernel SVMs don't scale to big data.*

## Part 2 — k-nearest-neighbors

> **Goal — make no model at all: predict from the labels of the closest stored points, and understand exactly when "closest" is trustworthy.**

kNN predicts the majority vote (classification) or average (regression) of the $k$ training points nearest the query. "Training" is just storing the data — it's **lazy**; all the cost is at query time.

### Distance, and why scaling is mandatory

Common metrics: Euclidean ($L_2$), Manhattan ($L_1$), Minkowski (general $p$), **Mahalanobis** $\sqrt{(x-x')^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-x')}$ (accounts for feature covariance and scale), and cosine (direction only, for text/embeddings). Euclidean distance is dominated by large-range features: a feature on $[0,500{,}000]$ swamps one on $[0,1]$, so the second is effectively ignored. **You must standardize features before kNN** (z-score or min-max), or use Mahalanobis (which bakes scaling in) or cosine (magnitude-invariant).

### Choosing $k$ is bias-variance

Small $k$ → flexible, low bias, **high variance** ($k=1$ memorizes the training set: zero training error, a jagged boundary). Large $k$ → smoother, **high bias, low variance** ($k=n$ predicts the global majority/mean — maximal underfit). The optimal $k$ is intermediate, chosen by cross-validation, and grows with $n$. A useful framing: kNN regression is **local averaging with an adaptive bandwidth** (the radius to the $k$-th neighbor) — the variable-bandwidth cousin of Nadaraya–Watson kernel regression. Its variance scales like $\sigma^2/k$, its bias grows with $k$.

Right (k=15): the boundary is smooth and ignores small pockets — some training points end up on the 'wrong' side but the classifier generalizes better — high bias. ### How good can it be? Cover–Hart

A classic result (Cover & Hart 1967): as $n\to\infty$, the 1-nearest-neighbor error $R$ is bounded in terms of the Bayes error $R^\star$:

$$R^\star \le R \le R^\star\Bigl(2 - \tfrac{M}{M-1}R^\star\Bigr)$$

for $M$ classes; in the **binary** case this is $R \le 2R^\star(1-R^\star) \le 2R^\star$.

So **1-NN's asymptotic error is at most twice the optimal** — half the achievable information is in the single nearest neighbor. With $k\to\infty$ and $k/n\to0$, kNN becomes Bayes-*consistent* ($R\to R^\star$). For a method with no training, that's remarkable.

### The curse of dimensionality — a probability fact

Here's the catch. In high dimensions, distances **concentrate**: for a query against $n$ points, the ratio of the farthest to the nearest distance tends to 1. Beyer et al. (1999) make this precise — under mild conditions the ratio of farthest to nearest distance converges to 1 (for the formal variance-ratio statement, ask an AI assistant; an interviewer won't ask you to reproduce it).

Intuition: $\|x-x'\|^2=\sum_{j=1}^d(x_j-x_j')^2$ is a sum of $d$ i.i.d. terms, so its mean grows like $d$ but its standard deviation like $\sqrt{d}$ — the coefficient of variation shrinks like $1/\sqrt{d}$, and all pairwise distances bunch together. "Nearest" stops being meaningfully nearest. This is *why* high-dimensional kNN needs either low **intrinsic** dimension (cluster structure) or learned low-dimensional embeddings.

<DistanceConcentration caption="Pairwise distances for 100 uniform points in the unit cube of dimension d, normalized so the mean distance is 1. Each bar is [min, max]/mean. At d=2 the bar spans a wide range — nearest is clearly nearer than farthest. As d grows, the bar collapses: at d=1000 every pair sits within a few percent of the mean, so 'nearest neighbor' loses information — that's Beyer's concentration theorem, made a visual." />

### kNN at scale: approximate nearest neighbors

Exact kNN query is $O(nd)$; KD-trees give $O(\log n)$ in low dimensions but **collapse back to $O(n)$ past ~20 dimensions** (ball-trees do a bit better). Modern embedding retrieval (semantic search, RAG) has millions of high-dimensional vectors, so it uses **approximate nearest neighbor (ANN)**: **HNSW** (hierarchical navigable small-world graphs — the dominant index in Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, pgvector), **FAISS** (IVF + product quantization, GPU, billion-scale), and **ScaNN**. They trade exactness for sub-linear query at high recall — kNN is far from obsolete; it just went approximate.

## Why a "weak baseline" deserves a full lesson

> **Goal — use the simplest generative classifier to nail two ideas that recur everywhere: the generative-vs-discriminative tradeoff, and the gap between classifying and estimating probabilities.** Naive Bayes is dismissed as a toy, but it's the cleanest case study in the course: it's MLE of a joint model (c1.2's "loss = −log p"), it's the generative twin of logistic regression (c1.3), and it's the canonical example of "great AUC, terrible calibration" (c1.2/c1.6). Interviewers love it precisely because the obvious criticism — "the independence assumption is false" — is a *trap*.

> **Vocabulary primer.** The "rusty staff engineer" assumption from c1.1 still holds.
>
> - **Prior / likelihood / posterior** — $p(y)$ / $p(x\mid y)$ / $p(y\mid x)$; Bayes' rule ties them: posterior $\propto$ likelihood $\times$ prior.
> - **Generative model** — models the full joint $p(x,y)$ (can score how likely $x$ itself is). **Discriminative model** — models only $p(y\mid x)$ (the boundary).
> - **Conditional independence** — features are independent *given the class*: $p(x\mid y)=\prod_j p(x_j\mid y)$. (Not marginal independence.)
> - **MLE / MAP** — maximum likelihood (counts) vs maximum a posteriori (counts + a prior = smoothing).
> - **Calibration** — whether a predicted "0.9" actually happens 90% of the time (c1.2).

## Part 1 — Bayes' rule and the "naive" assumption

> **Goal — turn classification into counting by assuming features are independent within each class.**

The Bayes-optimal classifier picks $\hat y(x)=\arg\max_y p(y\mid x)=\arg\max_y \underbrace{p(x\mid y)}_{\text{likelihood}}\,\underbrace{p(y)}_{\text{prior}}$ (the evidence $p(x)$ is constant in $y$). Its error is the irreducible **Bayes error** $R^\star=\mathbb{E}_x[1-\max_y p(y\mid x)]$ — the floor no classifier beats.

The catch: $p(x\mid y)$ over $d$ features is a joint distribution with $O(2^d)$ parameters (binary case) — unestimable. **The naive assumption** is conditional independence *given the class*:

$$p(x\mid y)=\prod_{j=1}^d p(x_j\mid y)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat y=\arg\max_y\;p(y)\prod_{j=1}^d p(x_j\mid y)$$

This collapses the parameter count to $O(d)$ per class — each $p(x_j\mid y)$ is a one-dimensional estimate. Work in **log-space** (a product of thousands of small probabilities underflows to 0; the log-sum is a manageable negative number), recovering normalized posteriors via log-sum-exp (c1.2's numerical-stability point, again):

$$\log p(y\mid x)=\log p(y)+\sum_{j=1}^d \log p(x_j\mid y)-\log p(x)$$

## Part 2 — Why the boundary is linear

> **Goal — show that "counting" produces exactly a logistic-regression-shaped hyperplane.**

The mechanism is worth naming before the algebra: taking the log turns Bayes's *product* over features into a *sum*, and each per-feature summand collapses to a constant plus a weight times $x_j$. Sum those and you get a linear function of $x$ — a hyperplane. That's it; "linear boundary" is a *consequence* of independence + log, not a modeling assumption.

Take binary features $x_j\in\{0,1\}$ with $\theta_{cj}=p(x_j{=}1\mid y{=}c)$, so $\log p(x_j\mid c)=x_j\log\theta_{cj}+(1-x_j)\log(1-\theta_{cj})$ — **affine in $x_j$**. Summing over $j$ and subtracting the $y{=}0$ case gives the log-odds:

$$\log\frac{p(1\mid x)}{p(0\mid x)}=\underbrace{\Bigl[\log\tfrac{p(1)}{p(0)}+\sum_j\log\tfrac{1-\theta_{1j}}{1-\theta_{0j}}\Bigr]}_{b}+\sum_j x_j\underbrace{\log\frac{\theta_{1j}(1-\theta_{0j})}{\theta_{0j}(1-\theta_{1j})}}_{w_j}=w^\top x+b$$

A hyperplane — **NB is a linear classifier**, of the same log-linear family as logistic regression.

The dashed vertical is where the densities cross — the point at which the posteriors flip. The variants differ only in the per-feature likelihood $p(x_j\mid y)$:

- **Gaussian NB** — continuous $x_j\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{cj},\sigma_{cj}^2)$.
- **Multinomial NB** — word counts; score $\log p(c)+\sum_j x_j\log\theta_{cj}$ (text).
- **Bernoulli NB** — word *presence*; uniquely **penalizes non-occurrence** via the $(1-\theta_{cj})(1-x_j)$ term.
- **Complement NB** (Rennie 2003) — estimates from each class's *complement*; fixes Multinomial NB's bias on **imbalanced** text.

## Part 3 — Smoothing is a prior, not a hack

> **Goal — stop a single unseen feature from vetoing an entire class, and recognize the fix as Bayesian shrinkage.**

If a word never appears in class $c$ in training, the MLE $\hat\theta_{cj}=0$, so the whole product $\prod_j p(x_j\mid c)=0$ — one unseen feature sets the class probability to **zero regardless of overwhelming evidence elsewhere** ($\log 0=-\infty$, an absorbing state). **Additive smoothing** adds a pseudo-count: Multinomial NB uses $\hat\theta_{cj}=\frac{N_{cj}+\alpha}{N_c+\alpha d}$ ($\alpha=1$ = Laplace, $0<\alpha<1$ = Lidstone).

This isn't arbitrary. For a multinomial with a symmetric **Dirichlet$(\beta)$** prior, the posterior is Dirichlet$(N_j+\beta)$, whose mode is $\frac{N_j+\beta-1}{N+d(\beta-1)}$ — set $\alpha=\beta-1$ and you recover Laplace smoothing exactly. So **MLE = raw counts; MAP under a Dirichlet prior = smoothed counts** — the identical move to c1.3 (ridge/lasso = Gaussian/Laplace priors *on coefficients*; here a Dirichlet prior *on probabilities*). The penalty is always a negative-log-prior, and smoothing shrinks rare-event frequencies toward uniform — the same **James–Stein** logic from c1.3.

## Part 4 — Generative vs. discriminative: the Ng–Jordan pair

> **Goal — see NB and logistic regression as the same model fit two ways, and know which wins when.**

**They share a functional form.** Assume Gaussian NB with variance *shared across classes*, $p(x_j\mid c)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{cj},\sigma_j^2)$. In the log-odds the quadratic $x_j^2$ terms **cancel** (shared $\sigma_j^2$), leaving

$$\log\frac{p(1\mid x)}{p(0\mid x)}=b+\sum_j\frac{\mu_{1j}-\mu_{0j}}{\sigma_j^2}\,x_j=w^\top x+b\;\Rightarrow\;p(1\mid x)=\sigma(w^\top x+b)$$

— the **logistic form**. (Drop the shared-variance assumption and the $x_j^2$ terms survive → a quadratic boundary = QDA.) So GNB and logistic regression assume the same $p(y\mid x)$ family; they differ only in *how they fit it*: **NB maximizes $\sum_i\log p(x_i,y_i)$** (generative, by counting moments), **logistic maximizes $\sum_i\log p(y_i\mid x_i)$** (discriminative, by gradient descent).

**Which wins (Ng & Jordan 2002).** Logistic has **lower asymptotic error** (it fits $p(y\mid x)$ directly, so it isn't biased by NB's false independence assumption). But NB **converges far faster**: its $O(d)$ parameters are independent univariate estimates each converging at $O(1/\sqrt m)$, so $m=O(\log d)$ examples suffice to approach its (worse) plateau, versus logistic's $m=O(d)$. **Two regimes:** with *few* examples NB reaches its plateau fast and wins; with *many*, logistic overtakes. "Generative is obsolete" is wrong — it's the right tool when labels are scarce relative to dimension.

## Part 5 — Great classifier, terrible probabilities

> **Goal — internalize the course's sharpest calibration lesson: a model can rank perfectly and still lie about probabilities.**

Why does a model built on a *false* assumption work at all? **Domingos & Pazzani (1997):** the assumption damages the *probabilities*, not the *argmax*. Correlated features make NB **double-count evidence** — if $x_2\approx x_1$, NB multiplies their likelihood ratios as if independent, squaring the true evidence and pushing the posterior toward 0 or 1. But the decision only needs the (biased) posterior to stay on the correct side of $\tfrac12$. Their exact result: the region where NB is *classification*-optimal is vastly larger than where it's *probability*-optimal.

*In one line:* double-counting correlated features squares the log-odds, so the sigmoid saturates — but "saturated on the correct side of ½" is still the right label. Accuracy survives; probabilities don't.

<NbReliability caption="Reliability diagram: predicted confidence (x) vs empirical accuracy (y). The dashed diagonal is perfect calibration. Uncalibrated Naive Bayes (red) is S-shaped — pinned near 0 or 1 for most of its predictions because double-counted evidence squares the log-odds. Platt-recalibrated NB (blue) tracks the diagonal. AUC (a rank statistic) is unchanged across both curves — the rankings didn't move, only the scores they were mapped to did." />

So NB's `predict_proba` outputs are **systematically overconfident** — the canonical "high AUC, bad ECE/Brier" case (c1.2, c1.6), and unlike the deep-net overconfidence of Guo 2017 this comes from the model structure itself. **Treat NB scores as rankings, not probabilities**; if you threshold on a probability or feed it into expected-value math, **calibrate first** (Platt/sigmoid or isotonic via `CalibratedClassifierCV`).

> **Stop and think.** You duplicate a feature ($x_2 := x_1$). What happens to NB's log-odds, the true evidence, and the predicted probability — and does the classification change? *Hint: the feature's $w_j x_j$ contribution is counted twice, doubling its log-odds while the true information is unchanged → the posterior is pushed toward 0/1 (overconfident). The argmax usually survives (still on the same side of ½), so accuracy/AUC are fine while calibration degrades — exactly Domingos–Pazzani.*

## The stats / quant-research lens

- **Generative vs discriminative, precisely:** NB models the full joint $p(x,y)$ — so it can sample $x$, flag novelty via $p(x)$, and **handle missing features by marginalizing them out** (a real advantage); logistic spends no capacity on $p(x)$ (which you don't need to classify). Same hypothesis class, different objective.
- **Ng–Jordan is bias-variance at the estimator-class level:** the generative model imposes strong structure → higher bias (worse asymptote) but lower variance (fast convergence); discriminative imposes less → lower bias, higher variance. The quant framing: with a *short backtest / few labeled events*, the high-bias generative estimator can dominate.
- **Smoothing = shrinkage = MAP** (the c1.3 shrinkage thread): smoothed counts dominate raw frequencies when many parameters are estimated from sparse data.


## Worked decision walk-throughs

**Scenario A: text classification, 50k sparse TF-IDF features, 100k docs.** **Linear SVM** (`LinearSVC`). High-dimensional sparse data is where margin maximization shines, and linear SVMs scale; an RBF kernel would be both unnecessary and too slow.

**Scenario B: 5M rows, RBF boundary suspected.** *Not* `SVC(kernel='rbf')` — its $O(n^2)$–$O(n^3)$ training is impractical past tens of thousands of rows. Use `LinearSVC`/`SGDClassifier`, optionally with a **kernel approximation** (`Nystroem` or random Fourier features) to recover RBF-like nonlinearity at scale.

**Scenario C: semantic search over 50M embeddings.** **Approximate kNN** with an HNSW index (FAISS/hnswlib/a vector DB). Exact kNN is infeasible; ANN gives high recall at millisecond latency.

**Scenario D: low-dimensional, clearly nonlinear, few thousand points.** **RBF SVM** — tune $C$ and $\gamma$ by grid-search CV. This is its sweet spot.

**Scenario A: spam filter, a few hundred labeled emails.** **Multinomial NB.** Tiny data, high-dimensional bag-of-words — exactly the regime where NB's fast convergence beats logistic (Ng–Jordan), and it trains in one pass.

**Scenario B: same task, 10M labeled emails.** **Logistic regression** (or a linear model) — with abundant data its lower asymptotic error wins; NB's independence bias now costs you.

**Scenario C: you need the fraud probability for an expected-loss threshold ($p \times \$$).** Don't trust NB's `predict_proba`. **Calibrate** (`CalibratedClassifierCV`, isotonic if you have data, sigmoid if not), then threshold — or use a discriminative model. Verify with a reliability diagram (c1.2).

**Scenario D: imbalanced topic classification.** **Complement NB** — it corrects the majority-class weight bias that skews standard Multinomial NB.


## Production failure modes

1. **Unscaled kNN.** One large-range feature dominates the distance; the model silently ignores everything else. Always standardize.
2. **Kernel SVM on big data.** `SVC(kernel='rbf')` on millions of rows OOMs or runs for days (Gram matrix is $n\times n$). Switch to linear/approximate.
3. **High-dimensional kNN that "works" offline but not live.** Distance concentration makes neighbors arbitrary; reduce dimension (embeddings, PCA) first.
4. **Untuned $C$/$\gamma$.** Defaults rarely fit; the model under- or over-fits dramatically. Joint CV is mandatory.
5. **Needing probabilities from SVC.** SVMs output signed distances, not probabilities; `probability=True` fits Platt scaling via expensive internal CV and can be inconsistent with `decision_function`. If you need calibrated probabilities, prefer logistic regression or calibrate explicitly.

6. **Zero counts with no smoothing.** One unseen feature → class probability 0. Keep $\alpha>0$.
7. **Trusting NB probabilities.** Overconfident by construction; calibrate before any probability-thresholded or EV decision.
8. **Correlated features.** Duplicated/collinear features double-count evidence → worse calibration (and can eventually flip the argmax). De-duplicate or use a discriminative model.
9. **Negative features into Multinomial/Complement NB.** They require non-negative inputs (counts/TF-IDF) and raise otherwise.
10. **The `force_alpha` trap.** In scikit-learn ≥1.4 `force_alpha=True` is the default; to get *unsmoothed* MLE you must set `alpha=0` **and** `force_alpha=True` — and you'll then hit the zero-count problem. (Older versions silently floored tiny `alpha` to `1e-10`.)


## Interview gotchas

- **"Derive the SVM margin."** $2/\|w\|$ from $w^\top x_\pm + b=\pm1$; maximize ⇔ minimize $\tfrac12\|w\|^2$; geometric not functional margin.
- **"Why kernelize the dual, not the primal?"** The dual depends on data only through inner products, and $w=\sum\alpha_i y_i x_i$ is infinite-dimensional for RBF — the dual never materializes $\phi$.
- **"Hinge vs logistic — why is SVM sparse?"** Hinge is exactly zero for margin $\ge1$, so correctly-classified-beyond-margin points have no gradient and drop out; logistic never reaches zero.
- **"Is the RBF feature map finite?"** No — infinite-dimensional (expand the exponential). That's why you can't just precompute $\phi$.
- **"What breaks kNN in high dimensions?"** Distance concentration (Beyer 1999) — nearest ≈ farthest; cite the $1/\sqrt d$ coefficient-of-variation argument.
- **"How good is 1-NN?"** Cover–Hart: asymptotic error $\le 2\times$ Bayes; $k\to\infty,k/n\to0$ gives consistency.
- **"Direction of $C$ and $\gamma$?"** Large $C$ = less regularization (overfit); large $\gamma$ = more local/wiggly (overfit).

- **"The independence assumption is false, so NB is bad."** Misaimed — Domingos–Pazzani: it hurts *probabilities*, not the *argmax*. NB is a strong classifier and a bad probability estimator.
- **"Discriminative always beats generative."** No — Ng–Jordan: NB wins in the low-data regime ($O(\log d)$ vs $O(d)$); logistic wins asymptotically.
- **"NB gives probabilities."** Technically yes, practically overconfident — calibrate.
- **"Derive NB's decision boundary."** Linear in log-space; be ready to show the Bernoulli $w_j, b$.
- **"Why does Gaussian NB relate to logistic regression?"** Equal-variance GNB *is* logistic in form; they differ in objective (joint vs conditional likelihood). Unequal variance → QDA.
- **"What is Laplace smoothing, really?"** MAP under a Dirichlet prior — shrinkage, the c1.3 prior thread.
