**Q1.** You train on a balanced sample ($\bar y=0.5$) but deploy at true prevalence $\tau=0.01$. Derive the additive log-odds correction, and show the corrected model is the *same ranking* as the original — i.e. resampling changed nothing a recalibration couldn't.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The shift.** By Bayes, the posterior odds factor into prior odds × likelihood ratio. Training at $\bar y$ instead of $\tau$ changes only the *prior* term, multiplying the predicted odds by the ratio of prior-odds:

$$\frac{p_{\text{bal}}}{1-p_{\text{bal}}}=\frac{p_{\text{true}}}{1-p_{\text{true}}}\cdot\frac{\bar y/(1-\bar y)}{\tau/(1-\tau)}$$

Taking logs, the balanced model's log-odds is the true log-odds **plus a constant**:

$$\text{logit}(p_{\text{bal}})=\text{logit}(p_{\text{true}})+\ln\!\Bigl[\frac{\bar y/(1-\bar y)}{\tau/(1-\tau)}\Bigr]$$

So to recover true-prevalence probabilities, **subtract** that constant (King & Zeng's intercept correction): $\text{logit}(p_{\text{corr}})=\text{logit}(p_{\text{bal}})-\ln\bigl[\frac{1-\tau}{\tau}\frac{\bar y}{1-\bar y}\bigr]$. With $\bar y=0.5$, $\tau=0.01$: the shift is $\ln\frac{0.99}{0.01}\approx4.6$ — the balanced model's log-odds are inflated by ~4.6 everywhere.

**Same ranking.** The correction is a *constant added to every score's log-odds*, i.e. a strictly monotone transform of the scores. So it preserves their **order** — the AUC and any ranking are identical to the uncorrected balanced model (c1.10: AUC is monotone-invariant), and the corrected probabilities are just a recalibration. Conclusion: **balancing only shifted the probabilities (and hence the implicit threshold); it didn't change what the model knows.** A recalibration step recovers everything.

**Rubric:**
- Bayes: balancing scales odds by the prior-odds ratio → log-odds shifted by a constant
- King–Zeng correction; compute $\ln(0.99/0.01)\approx4.6$ for $\bar y=0.5,\tau=0.01$
- Constant log-odds shift = monotone → ranking/AUC unchanged
- Conclude: resampling = a probability shift recalibration could undo

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q2.** Derive the cost-optimal threshold $t^\star=C_{\text{FP}}/(C_{\text{FP}}+C_{\text{FN}})$ from expected-cost minimization. Then show that oversampling the minority to a ratio $\rho$ is equivalent to a *specific* threshold on the un-resampled model.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Threshold derivation.** With calibrated $p=P(y{=}1\mid x)$ and correct predictions costing 0:
- predict **positive** → pay $C_{\text{FP}}$ iff negative → expected $(1-p)C_{\text{FP}}$;
- predict **negative** → pay $C_{\text{FN}}$ iff positive → expected $p\,C_{\text{FN}}$.

Predict positive iff $(1-p)C_{\text{FP}}<p\,C_{\text{FN}} \iff p>\dfrac{C_{\text{FP}}}{C_{\text{FP}}+C_{\text{FN}}}=t^\star$.

**Oversampling ≡ a threshold.** Oversampling the minority by a factor $k$ (until the training odds are $k\times$ the true odds) makes the model learn shifted log-odds: $\text{logit}(p_{\text{re}})=\text{logit}(p_{\text{true}})+\ln k$ (Q1, with $\ln k$ the log of the prior-odds change). The resampled model then uses the *naive* threshold $p_{\text{re}}\ge0.5$, i.e. $\text{logit}(p_{\text{re}})\ge0$, which in terms of the true probability is $\text{logit}(p_{\text{true}})\ge-\ln k$, i.e.

$$p_{\text{true}}\ge\sigma(-\ln k)=\frac{1}{1+k}$$

So oversampling by $k$ and thresholding at 0.5 is **identical** to leaving the model un-resampled and thresholding the true probability at $t=\frac{1}{1+k}$. (Balancing to 50/50 from prevalence $\tau$ corresponds to $k=(1-\tau)/\tau$, giving $t=\tau$ — you've implicitly set the threshold to the prevalence.) The resampling bought you a threshold move you could have made directly — with the bonus downside of degraded calibration (Q7).

**Rubric:**
- Compare expected action costs → $t^\star=C_{\text{FP}}/(C_{\text{FP}}+C_{\text{FN}})$
- Oversampling by $k$ → log-odds $+\ln k$ (prior shift)
- Naive 0.5 threshold on resampled = threshold $1/(1+k)$ on true $p$
- Conclude: resampling ≡ an implicit (uncalibrated) threshold move

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q3.** Construct a dataset where SMOTE *strictly hurts*, and explain the mechanism in terms of SMOTE's interpolation assumption.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Construction.** Put the minority class in **two separated sub-clusters** with the majority class occupying the region *between* them (an XOR-like / multi-modal layout): minority blobs at $(0,0)$ and $(10,10)$, majority filling the band around $(5,5)$. SMOTE picks a minority point and one of its $k$ minority nearest neighbors and interpolates on the segment between them. When a point in the $(0,0)$ blob has a neighbor in the $(10,10)$ blob (which happens once the blobs are each other's nearest minority neighbors), the synthetic point $x_{\text{new}}=x_i+u(x_i^{(\text{nn})}-x_i)$ lands **in the majority band** around $(5,5)$ — a synthetic "positive" sitting squarely in negative territory.

**Mechanism.** SMOTE assumes the minority class is **locally convex/linear** — that the segment between two minority points stays inside the minority region. When the minority is concave or multi-modal, segments cross into the majority, **injecting synthetic label noise** right on the decision boundary, which drags the boundary into the majority region and *raises* false positives. So SMOTE strictly hurts here versus doing nothing.

(Other valid constructions: high-dimensional data — Blagus & Lusa 2013 show SMOTE leaves class means unchanged and just shrinks variance, so it doesn't help; or categorical features, where interpolating one-hots is meaningless.) The fixes that target this: Borderline-SMOTE/ADASYN (synthesize only near genuine boundaries) or, better, don't SMOTE — threshold + calibrate.

**Rubric:**
- Multi-modal/XOR minority with majority in between
- Interpolation between distant minority blobs → synthetic positive in majority region
- Mechanism: SMOTE's local-convexity assumption violated → injected label noise → boundary dragged
- Note high-dim (Blagus–Lusa) / categorical failure modes as alternatives

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q4.** An engineer SMOTEs the full dataset, then runs 5-fold CV and reports 0.97 PR-AUC; an honest pipeline gives 0.78. Explain the exact leakage mechanism, write the correct ordering, and say why `sklearn.pipeline.Pipeline` can't express it.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The leak.** SMOTE-before-split synthesizes minority points as **interpolations of real minority points**. After the global SMOTE, the CV splitter divides *all* points (real + synthetic) into folds — so a synthetic point in the *validation* fold is a convex combination of real points that are in the *training* fold (and conversely, a validation real point's near-duplicates/neighbors are in training). The model is therefore scored on points it effectively saw in training → train/test leakage (c1.1's information-availability rule). It's the same structural bug as c1.5's non-OOF stacking: information from the held-out set bled into training. Hence the wildly optimistic 0.97; the honest 0.78 is the truth.

**Correct ordering.** Resample **inside each fold, on the training partition only**, after the split:

```python
from imblearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from imblearn.over_sampling import SMOTE
pipe = Pipeline([("smote", SMOTE()), ("clf", model)])
cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5, scoring="average_precision")
```

`imblearn`'s `Pipeline` runs the sampler **only on `fit`/`fit_resample`** and **bypasses it on `transform`/`predict`** — so each fold SMOTEs its own training split and the held-out fold is scored at the true prevalence.

**Why `sklearn.pipeline.Pipeline` can't.** sklearn's pipeline only knows `fit`/`transform`/`predict` steps — it has **no notion of a resampling step** that changes the *number of rows* and must act on `fit` but not on `predict`. So you literally cannot place a resampler in it; imbalanced-learn ships its own `Pipeline` precisely to add that `fit_resample` semantics.

**Rubric:**
- Synthetic validation points are interpolations of training points (and vice versa) → leakage
- Same as c1.1 information-availability / c1.5 OOF
- Fix: SMOTE inside `imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline` (sampler on fit only, bypassed on predict)
- sklearn Pipeline has no resample step (can't change row count on fit-only) → imblearn's exists for this

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q5.** Prove that reweighting and oversampling share the same expected full-batch gradient, then construct the minibatch-SGD regime where they diverge. When does each need learning-rate retuning?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Same expected gradient.** Let the minority have per-example loss gradient $g_+$ and there be $n_+$ minority, $n_-$ majority examples. **Oversampling** the minority $k\times$ makes the (summed) training gradient $\sum_{\text{maj}}g + k\sum_{\text{min}}g_+$. **Reweighting** the minority loss by $k$ gives the same summed gradient $\sum_{\text{maj}}g + k\sum_{\text{min}}g_+$. So over a full pass they produce identical (expected) parameter updates — they're equivalent in expectation.

**Divergence under minibatch SGD.** They differ in *variance and dynamics*:
- **Oversampling** changes the *empirical distribution*: the duplicated minority rows appear in many minibatches, so the model takes *many gradient steps on the exact same points* → it can **memorize/overfit** those duplicates (especially with exact ROS). The per-step gradient has normal magnitude but the *data* is repeated.
- **Reweighting** keeps each row once but multiplies its gradient by $k$: a minibatch that happens to contain minority examples produces a **much larger, higher-variance** update (a $k\times$ spike), which can destabilize training.

**LR retuning.** Reweighting inflates effective gradient magnitudes by up to $k\times$, so it typically needs a **smaller / retuned learning rate** (and the `'mean'`-reduction gotcha from c1.3 changes the effective scale). Oversampling keeps gradient magnitudes normal but changes the effective epoch size and overfitting dynamics, so it interacts with **early stopping / regularization** more than with the LR.

**Rubric:**
- Both give summed gradient $\sum_{\text{maj}}g+k\sum_{\text{min}}g_+$ → equal in expectation
- Oversampling: repeated rows → memorization/overfit (data repeated)
- Reweighting: $k\times$ per-example gradient → high-variance/spiky updates
- Reweighting needs LR retuning (magnitude); oversampling interacts with early-stop/regularization

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q6.** Prove ROC-AUC is invariant to prevalence but PR-AUC is not. For a fixed scorer, compute how precision changes going from 50% to 1% positive while ROC-AUC stays fixed.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**ROC invariance.** TPR $=\text{TP}/(\text{TP+FN})$ and FPR $=\text{FP}/(\text{FP+TN})$ are each computed *within a single true class* (column-normalized). Changing prevalence multiplies the count of one class but cancels in its own rate: 10× the negatives sends $\text{FP}\to10\text{FP}$, $\text{TN}\to10\text{TN}$, so $\text{FPR}=\frac{10\text{FP}}{10\text{FP}+10\text{TN}}$ is unchanged; TPR doesn't involve negatives. So every ROC point — and AUC — depends only on the class-conditional score distributions, **not** the prior. ROC-AUC is prevalence-invariant.

**PR moves.** Precision $=\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP+FP}}$ mixes both classes; via Bayes $P=\frac{\text{TPR}\,\pi}{\text{TPR}\,\pi+\text{FPR}(1-\pi)}$, which depends on the prior $\pi$. So PR-AUC (and the no-skill PR baseline = $\pi$) moves with prevalence.

**Compute** (scorer fixed at TPR $=0.9$, FPR $=0.1$):
- $\pi=0.5$: $P=\frac{0.9(0.5)}{0.9(0.5)+0.1(0.5)}=0.90$.
- $\pi=0.01$: $P=\frac{0.9(0.01)}{0.9(0.01)+0.1(0.99)}=\frac{0.009}{0.108}\approx0.083$.

So precision falls from **0.90 to ~0.083** (and the PR baseline from 0.5 to 0.01) while **ROC-AUC is identical**. That's why imbalanced evaluation must use PR-AUC (c1.10), not ROC-AUC.

**Rubric:**
- TPR/FPR column-normalized → prevalence cancels → ROC invariant
- Precision via Bayes depends on $\pi$
- Compute 0.90 → 0.083 for fixed TPR/FPR
- Conclude: use PR-AUC for imbalanced evaluation

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q7.** A model is perfectly calibrated at $\tau=0.02$. After balancing to 50/50, what happens to its reliability diagram, and what single step undoes the distortion?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**The distortion.** Balancing from $\tau=0.02$ to $\bar y=0.5$ multiplies the predicted odds by $\frac{\bar y/(1-\bar y)}{\tau/(1-\tau)}=\frac{1}{0.02/0.98}=49$ (Q1). So the balanced model's predicted probabilities are **systematically inflated** for the positive class — a case it truly thinks is $p_{\text{true}}=0.1$ now reports $\sigma(\text{logit}(0.1)+\ln 49)\approx\sigma(-2.197+3.892)=\sigma(1.695)\approx0.845$. On a reliability diagram (predicted prob on x, empirical rate on y), the curve falls **well below the diagonal**: at a predicted 0.845 the empirical positive rate is only ~0.1. The model is now badly over-confident in the positive direction — exactly the van den Goorbergh et al. (2022) finding that resampling preserves ROC-AUC (monotone, c1.10) but wrecks calibration.

**The fix (one step).** **Prior correction:** subtract the constant log-odds shift, $\text{logit}(p_{\text{corr}})=\text{logit}(p_{\text{bal}})-\ln\frac{\bar y/(1-\bar y)}{\tau/(1-\tau)}=\text{logit}(p_{\text{bal}})-\ln 49$. This maps the inflated probabilities back onto the diagonal and recovers the original calibrated model (equivalently, refit the intercept, or run `CalibratedClassifierCV` on a held-out set at the true prevalence). Since it's a monotone transform, AUC is unchanged — only the probabilities are repaired.

**Rubric:**
- Balancing inflates odds by $\frac{1}{\tau/(1-\tau)}=49$ → probabilities over-estimated
- Reliability curve sits below the diagonal (over-confident positive); cite van den Goorbergh 2022
- Fix: subtract the $\ln 49$ log-odds shift (prior correction) / recalibrate
- Monotone → AUC unchanged, calibration restored

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q8.** Compute `class_weight='balanced'` for 30 positives / 970 negatives. Then explain the interaction with a loss that uses `'mean'` reduction over weighted examples.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Weights.** sklearn's formula is $w_c=\frac{N}{K\,n_c}$ (n_samples / (n_classes × class count)). With $N=1000$, $K=2$:
- $w_{\text{pos}}=\frac{1000}{2\cdot30}=\frac{1000}{60}\approx16.67$.
- $w_{\text{neg}}=\frac{1000}{2\cdot970}=\frac{1000}{1940}\approx0.515$.

(Ratio $\approx 32.3$, i.e. the minority's IR — each positive counts ~32× a negative, equalizing the two classes' total weight: $30\times16.67\approx970\times0.515\approx500$ each.)

**`'mean'`-reduction interaction.** A weighted loss reduced by `'mean'` typically divides the weighted sum by the **sum of weights present in the batch**, not by the batch size $N$ (the c1.3 `pos_weight` gotcha). So the *effective* per-batch normalizer changes batch-to-batch depending on how many minority examples it contains — a minority-heavy batch has a larger weight sum, shrinking each example's contribution. The practical consequence: the **effective learning rate fluctuates** with batch composition, and a batch with *zero* positives still contributes a full majority gradient with no minority counterweight. Mitigations: use stratified/`WeightedRandomSampler` batches so each minibatch contains minority examples, and retune the LR after adding class weights (or use sum-reduction with an explicit normalizer you control).

**Rubric:**
- $w_{\text{pos}}=1000/60\approx16.7$, $w_{\text{neg}}=1000/1940\approx0.515$ (ratio ≈ IR)
- Equalizes total class weight (~500 each)
- `'mean'` divides by sum-of-weights-present → effective LR varies with batch composition (c1.3 gotcha)
- Fix: stratified batches + LR retune; zero-positive batches are a hazard

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q9.** Given a labeled neighborhood, classify a minority point as safe / danger / noise (Borderline-SMOTE), and compute ADASYN's synthesis allocation. Construct a configuration where ADASYN over-concentrates synthesis on a mislabeled outlier.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Borderline-SMOTE labels.** For a minority point, look at its $m$ nearest neighbors (any class) and let $m'$ = number that are *majority*:
- **safe:** $0\le m'<m/2$ (mostly minority neighbors);
- **danger (borderline):** $m/2\le m'<m$ (mostly majority — near the boundary);
- **noise:** $m'=m$ (all majority — an isolated minority point).

Borderline-SMOTE synthesizes only from the **danger** set (concentrate effort on the boundary). E.g. with $m=10$: $m'=2$ → safe, $m'=7$ → danger, $m'=10$ → noise.

**ADASYN allocation.** For each minority point $i$, $r_i=(\text{# majority among its }k\text{-NN})/k$; normalize $\hat r_i=r_i/\sum_j r_j$; generate $g_i=\hat r_i\cdot G$ synthetic points (G = total to create). So *harder* minority points (more majority neighbors) get *more* synthesis — a continuous version of Borderline's hard threshold.

**The failure (mislabeled outlier).** Consider a single minority point mislabeled deep inside majority territory: all $k$ of its neighbors are majority, so $r_i=1$ (the maximum), and if other minority points are "safe" ($r_j$ small), this outlier's normalized $\hat r_i$ dominates → ADASYN pours a large share of $G$ synthetic positives **around a mislabeled point in the majority region**, manufacturing a cluster of bogus positives that wrecks the boundary. (Borderline-SMOTE would tag it *noise* ($m'=m$) and skip it — which is exactly why the noise category exists.) Lesson: density-adaptive synthesis is sensitive to label noise; clean labels or prefer threshold+calibrate.

**Rubric:**
- safe/danger/noise via $m'$ relative to $m/2$ and $m$; Borderline uses danger only
- ADASYN: $r_i$ = majority fraction in $k$-NN, $g_i=\hat r_i G$ (more for harder points)
- Construct mislabeled outlier with $r_i=1$ dominating $\hat r_i$ → synthesis concentrates on it
- Borderline tags it noise and skips; ADASYN's adaptivity is noise-sensitive

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q10.** At 1:10,000 imbalance, argue from estimation variance why an anomaly-detection framing can beat balanced binary classification. Which method for a *contaminated* training set, and how do you set $\nu$ / `contamination`?

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Why reframe.** At 1:10,000 with, say, 100k rows, you have ~10 positives. You cannot estimate a decision boundary or per-class densities from 10 points — the variance of any minority-class parameter estimate is enormous, and any resampling just duplicates/interpolates those 10 points (manufacturing fake confidence, not information). **Anomaly detection** instead models the *majority/normal* distribution (which you have abundant data for) and flags points that don't fit — turning "learn a boundary from 10 positives" into "learn normality from 100k negatives," a far lower-variance problem.

**Clean vs contaminated.**
- **One-Class SVM** (Schölkopf 2001) learns a boundary around a $\nu$-fraction of the training data — it assumes the training set is essentially **clean** (pure normal); it's a *novelty* detector. Use it when your "normal" training data has (almost) no anomalies.
- **Isolation Forest** (Liu 2008) isolates points via random splits (anomalies need fewer splits → shorter path length); it's robust to **contaminated, multimodal** data — use it when the training set itself contains some anomalies (outlier detection).

So for a **contaminated** training set → **Isolation Forest**.

**Setting the knob.** Both expose a fraction parameter tied to the expected anomaly rate: One-Class SVM's $\nu$ is an upper bound on the fraction of outliers (and a lower bound on the support-vector fraction); Isolation Forest's `contamination` is the expected proportion of anomalies (sets the score threshold). Set it to your **prior anomaly rate** ($\approx10^{-4}$ here), or tune it on a labeled validation subset against the cost-weighted metric.

**Rubric:**
- ~10 positives → huge estimation variance; resampling adds no information
- Reframe: model normal (abundant) + flag outliers → lower-variance problem
- One-Class SVM = clean/novelty; Isolation Forest = contaminated/outlier → pick IForest for contaminated
- $\nu$ / `contamination` ≈ prior anomaly rate (or tune on labeled validation)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q11.** At 1000:1, compare the compute and statistical costs of oversampling-to-balance vs undersampling-to-balance. Show undersampling discards mostly *easy* negatives, and design an ensemble that recovers the hard ones without the memory blowup.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Compute.** Oversampling to balance multiplies the *minority* up to match the majority — at 1000:1 that's ~1000× the minority, roughly **doubling** the dataset to ~2× the majority count (huge if the majority is already large), and training on mostly synthetic/duplicated rows. Undersampling to balance **drops ~99.9% of the majority**, leaving a tiny dataset (2× the minority) that trains in a fraction of the time and memory. So at extreme IR, **undersampling is the only thing that scales**.

**Statistical cost of undersampling.** A *random* undersample keeps a uniform sample of negatives — but the decision-relevant negatives are the **hard ones near the boundary** (easy negatives far from the boundary carry little gradient signal, c1.1's hard-negative point). Random undersampling discards them in proportion, so you throw away most of the *informative* negatives along with the easy ones, raising variance and hurting the boundary.

**Ensemble fix (EasyEnsemble / BalancedRandomForest).** Instead of one undersample, draw **many** independent balanced subsets (each = all minority + a fresh random undersample of the majority), train a classifier on each, and **aggregate** (vote/average). Across the ensemble, *most* of the majority — including the hard negatives — gets seen by *some* member, so collectively you use the full negative set, while each member stays small (memory ≈ one balanced subset at a time). This recovers the hard negatives undersampling would otherwise discard, without the oversampling memory blowup. (Informed undersampling — Tomek links / NearMiss — is the alternative: drop *easy* negatives preferentially.)

**Rubric:**
- Oversample → ~2× majority (compute/memory blowup); undersample → tiny (scales) at extreme IR
- Random undersampling discards informative hard negatives (c1.1)
- EasyEnsemble/BalancedRF: many balanced subsets + aggregate → covers all negatives, small per-member memory
- (or informed undersampling: Tomek/NearMiss drop easy negatives)

</details>

</Quiz>

**Q12.** You're handed a 1000:1 fraud dataset. Specify the full protocol — split, whether/where to resample, metric, and final operating point — and defend *not* using SMOTE.

<details>
<summary>Model answer + rubric</summary>

**Split.** **Stratified** (preserve the 1000:1 ratio in each fold so the rare class appears — c1.1), and if the data is time-ordered and entity-grouped, **temporal forward-chaining + grouped** (no future leakage, no entity straddling — c1.1). At 1000:1, ensure each validation fold has enough positives to estimate recall (or use repeated stratified CV).

**Resample? Where?** Default: **don't resample.** Train a strong probabilistic model (e.g. gradient-boosted trees) with optionally `class_weight`/`scale_pos_weight` for gradient signal. If you do resample (validated to help), put it **strictly inside the CV pipeline** (`imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline`) so only training folds are resampled — never SMOTE-then-split (Q4).

**Metric.** **PR-AUC / average precision** (ROC-AUC hides the precision collapse at 1000:1 — c1.10), plus precision/recall at the chosen operating point; pick the threshold from the **cost matrix**.

**Operating point.** **Calibrate** the model (Platt/isotonic, or prior-correct), then set the threshold $t^\star=C_{\text{FP}}/(C_{\text{FP}}+C_{\text{FN}})$ on the calibrated probability. (At 1000:1 you may also reframe as **anomaly detection** — Q10.)

**Why not SMOTE.** (1) **Elor & Averbuch-Elor 2022:** for strong probabilistic learners with a *tuned threshold*, SMOTE gives **no benefit** — its apparent gains are an artifact of leaving the threshold at 0.5. (2) **Resampling = an implicit threshold shift** (Q1–Q2) that also **breaks calibration** (van den Goorbergh 2022) — so it does nothing a calibrated threshold can't, with worse probabilities. (3) At 1000:1 in any real feature dimension, SMOTE's interpolation assumption is shaky (Blagus–Lusa). So: calibrate + threshold (+ maybe anomaly framing), and reach for SMOTE only if it provably beats that on PR-AUC inside a leakage-safe pipeline.

**Rubric:**
- Stratified (+ temporal/grouped if applicable) split; enough positives per fold
- Default no resample; if any, inside `imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline` (never SMOTE-then-split)
- PR-AUC / AP + cost-based threshold (not ROC-AUC)
- Calibrate + $t^\star=C_{\text{FP}}/(C_{\text{FP}}+C_{\text{FN}})$; consider anomaly framing
- Defend no-SMOTE: Elor 2022 (no benefit with tuned threshold) + resampling=threshold-shift+miscalibration (van den Goorbergh) + Blagus–Lusa

</details>

Show it is exact for a well-specified logistic model and only approximate under misspecification, then say what "correcting the prior" even means for an uncalibrated scorer (an SVM margin, an early-stopped GBM). Calibrate first or prior-correct first — does the order matter, and why?
7. **The hidden label-shift assumption.** The odds-ratio correction $\frac{p_{\text{bal}}}{1-p_{\text{bal}}} = \frac{p_{\text{true}}}{1-p_{\text{true}}}\cdot\frac{1-\tau}{\tau}$ silently assumes $p(x \mid y)$ is unchanged between training and deployment (label shift). Point to the exact step of the Bayes derivation where that assumption enters, then construct a deployment where prevalence moved because the *covariate* distribution moved — and show the correction now returns the wrong posterior.
8. **Estimate the unknown prior.** You deploy where the true prevalence $\tau$ is unknown and unlabeled data is all you have. Derive one method to estimate $\tau$ from unlabeled scores plus a source-calibrated classifier — the Saerens–Latinne–Decaestecker EM fixed point, or the confusion-matrix (BBSE) estimator — and state the identifiability condition each requires.
9. **The geometry of a synthetic point.** With $x_{\text{new}} = x_i + u\,(x_i^{(\text{nn})} - x_i)$, $u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$: derive the mean and covariance of the synthetic-point distribution relative to the real minority class's. Show the class mean is approximately preserved while within-class variance *shrinks* and synthetic points are correlated with their parents — the Blagus–Lusa result. Which classifiers does that shrinkage bias most (LDA, naive Bayes, $k$-NN), and in which direction?
10. **SMOTE's hidden metric.** SMOTE picks neighbors with Euclidean $k$-NN in raw feature space. What happens to the synthetic points when one feature is in dollars and another in log-odds, and why does SMOTE's output change under feature rescaling while a tree ensemble trained on the same data does not? What preprocessing does that force, and in what space would you rather interpolate?
11. **Impossible feature values.** Run plain SMOTE over a row with a count feature (`num_prior_defaults`), a one-hot-encoded `zip_code`, and a binary flag. Enumerate exactly what interpolation manufactures for each, explain why a tree learner may shrug while a distance- or density-based learner is poisoned, and state precisely what SMOTENC does differently — and the failure it still can't fix.
12. **Clean after you synthesize.** `SMOTETomek` and `SMOTEENN` bolt a *cleaning* step onto SMOTE. Specify each cleaner's deletion rule precisely — the Tomek-link condition, and which points edited-nearest-neighbours removes — say which of the two cleans more aggressively, then construct a regime where the cleaning step deletes most of what SMOTE just synthesized. What does that outcome tell you about the class boundary?
13. **SMOTE across time.** Your fraud table is time-ordered and you dutifully SMOTE only inside the training window of a forward-chaining split. Construct the *remaining* failure: under concept drift, what does a synthetic point interpolated between a January and a June transaction represent, and why can it manufacture a "fraud pattern" that never existed at any single point in time?
14. **Quantify the SMOTE-then-split leak.** Don't just name the leakage — bound it. After global SMOTE and a random 5-fold split, what fraction of validation-fold synthetic points have at least one interpolation parent sitting in the training folds, in expectation? Then design the minimal experiment (two pipelines, one dataset) that measures the CV-score inflation directly.
15. **When resampling is NOT a threshold move.** The central claim — resampling is an implicit threshold shift — assumes the model can represent the true posterior. For a *restricted* hypothesis class (linear model, non-linear truth), show reweighting reallocates approximation error and changes the learned boundary's *shape*, not just its offset. Construct a 1-D example where the reweighted model classifies points differently than *any* threshold on the unweighted model's scores.
16. **Two knobs, one confusion.** Focal loss ships two parameters: $\alpha$ (class weighting) and $\gamma$ (the $(1-p_t)^\gamma$ focusing term). Decompose which one addresses class imbalance and which addresses easy-example domination, why they are not interchangeable, and why RetinaNet (Lin et al. 2017) needed both — with the reported best $\alpha = 0.25$ apparently *down*-weighting the rare foreground class. Reconcile that.
17. **Improper but better calibrated.** Focal loss is not a proper scoring rule — its minimizer is not the true posterior — and it suppresses easy *minority* examples exactly as it suppresses easy majority ones. Yet Mukhoti et al. 2020 found focal-trained networks often *better* calibrated than cross-entropy-trained ones. Reconcile: what bias does focal put on the probabilities, why does it partially cancel deep nets' overconfidence, and what must you still do before thresholding?
18. **Derive logit adjustment.** Starting from a model of the standard posterior with logits $f_y(x)$, derive $\arg\max_y\,[f_y(x) - \log\pi_y]$ as the Bayes-optimal rule for *balanced* error. Then explain the loss-time variant (add $\log\pi_y$ to the logits inside the training softmax) and why the post-hoc and loss-time forms stop being equivalent for a misspecified or under-trained model.
19. **Effective number of samples.** Derive the class-balanced weight of Cui et al. 2019: weight by the inverse of $E_n = (1-\beta^n)/(1-\beta)$. What overlap/redundancy assumption generates that formula, how does it interpolate between no reweighting ($\beta \to 0$) and inverse-frequency ($\beta \to 1$), and when does plain inverse-frequency provably over-weight a tiny, noisy class?
20. **Decoupling contradicts the folklore.** Kang et al. 2020: a representation trained with *instance-balanced* (natural, imbalanced) sampling plus a rebalanced classifier head beats end-to-end class-balanced training on long-tailed benchmarks. Reconcile this with "rebalance so the minority gets gradient signal" — which part of the network was imbalance actually hurting, and where does that say resampling belongs (if anywhere) in a deep pipeline?

## Rapid-fire: thresholds, fraud ops & LLM-era pipelines

1. **The threshold isn't one number.** In fraud the false-negative cost is the transaction amount: $C_{\text{FN}}(x) = \text{amount}(x)$ against a fixed friction cost $C_{\text{FP}}$. Derive the per-example rule $t^\star(x)$, prove a single global threshold is suboptimal for any non-degenerate amount distribution, and explain why no class-level resampling or class-weighting scheme can encode this — while a calibrated $p(x)$ handles it trivially. What property must the model's output have for the per-example rule to work at all?
2. **Approve, review, block.** Real fraud systems have three actions, and human review costs money. Derive the *two* thresholds from a cost matrix that includes reviewer cost, state when the review band collapses to zero width, and explain how you'd set the band when the binding constraint is reviewer *capacity* — a fixed-size queue — rather than a per-item cost.
3. **Budget the FPR.** Your trust-and-safety team can review 1,000 flags per day out of 500M posts, and harmful prevalence is about $10^{-4}$. Derive the operating point *from the queue capacity* — what false-positive rate can you afford before the queue is pure noise, why do you end up ranking by score and reviewing top-$k$ anyway, and why is this constraint, not a cost matrix, what actually sets thresholds in integrity systems at Meta scale?
4. **Precision@k vs PR-AUC.** Fraud ops reviews exactly $k$ cases a day; retrieval serves exactly $k$ slots. Argue when precision@$k$ (or recall at fixed precision) is the right metric and PR-AUC is the wrong one — what does PR-AUC average over that your system will never do, and how can two models with equal PR-AUC differ badly at your $k$? Bundle: the no-skill PR baseline is prevalence itself, so what does that do to PR-AUC comparisons across a weekly dashboard where prevalence swings 5× seasonally?
5. **Threshold under drifting prevalence.** Your calibrated model was fit at prevalence $\tau_0$; next month it's $\tau_1$ with class-conditionals unchanged. Derive the corrected posterior and new cost-optimal threshold *without retraining*, and predict which monitored quantities move (precision, alert volume, recall) even though the model itself is fine. What alarm distinguishes this from real model decay?
6. **Platt, isotonic, or neither.** You have 30 confirmed frauds in your calibration fold. Argue which of `CalibratedClassifierCV`'s two methods survives: why does isotonic regression (piecewise-constant, monotone) overfit small calibration sets where Platt's two-parameter sigmoid doesn't, what do isotonic's flat segments do to threshold placement, and when is prior correction alone — no refit at all — the honest choice?
7. **Segment base rates diverge.** Globally your calibrated fraud model sits on the diagonal, but merchant A's base rate is $10\times$ merchant B's. Show why globally calibrated probabilities are miscalibrated per merchant, and design per-segment recalibration when small merchants have almost no positives — why is partial pooling toward the global curve (an empirical-Bayes shrinkage move) the right answer rather than per-segment isotonic?
8. **Calibration you can't see.** At 0.5% prevalence, a 10-bin equal-width ECE reports near-zero error for a badly miscalibrated model, and the constant predictor $p \equiv \tau$ scores a Brier of $\tau(1-\tau) \approx 0.0099$ — nearly perfect-looking. Explain both mechanisms (where the probability mass sits, which bins are empty; which Murphy component the constant predictor nails and which it entirely lacks), then give two diagnostics that survive extreme imbalance and say what each detects that the headline number misses.
9. **Meta downsamples anyway.** The advice is "don't resample" — yet Meta's ads pipeline downsamples negatives at rate $w$ and recalibrates with $q = p/\bigl(p + (1-p)/w\bigr)$ (He et al. 2014), and TikTok-scale CTR systems do the same. Reconcile: what constraint makes resample-then-recalibrate the *right* engineering choice at billions of impressions per day, and what exactly does the recalibration preserve that a naive resampler destroys?
10. **Multi-label resampling is self-defeating.** In multi-label classification, oversampling instances to balance label A necessarily drags the prevalence of co-occurring labels B and C. Construct a two-label example where balancing one label strictly *worsens* the other's imbalance, and argue why per-label threshold-moving on calibrated marginals sidesteps the entire mess.
11. **Two models, forty disagreements.** Your challenger and champion disagree on 40 of the 200 positives in the test set, and on a sliver of the millions of negatives. Which paired test do you run — and why McNemar's on the discordant pairs rather than a two-proportion $z$-test on overall accuracy? Why do the millions of concordant negatives add essentially zero power, and what does that say about how big an imbalanced test set *effectively* is?
12. **Estimate prevalence itself.** Platforms report the *prevalence* of harmful content as a headline metric — but you can label only 10k items out of billions, and a uniform sample at $10^{-4}$ yields about one positive. Design the estimator: stratify by model score, allocate labels across strata (Neyman allocation), and write the importance-weighted prevalence estimate and its variance. Why does uniform sampling fail at any realistic budget?
13. **Positive and unlabeled.** Your fraud labels are "confirmed fraud" versus "everything else" — positive-and-unlabeled, not positive-and-negative. Under the selected-completely-at-random assumption, derive Elkan & Noto's identity $P(y = 1 \mid x) = P(s = 1 \mid x)/c$, say what $c$ is and how to estimate it, and state the direction of the bias if you naively train unlabeled-as-negative.
14. **Labels that arrive late.** Chargebacks land 30–90 days after the transaction, so your freshest training data systematically under-labels fraud. Explain what weekly retraining does to calibration, then design the fix — label-maturation windows, importance weighting by label completeness, or a joint conversion-plus-delay model (Chapelle 2014) — and say when each is the right tool.
15. **The model censors its labels.** Once deployed, the fraud model blocks what it thinks is fraud — so you never observe outcomes for those transactions, and the next training set contains only what it approved (the selective-labels problem). Explain the feedback loop, why naive retraining makes the model blind to exactly the fraud it already catches, and the mitigations: randomized exploration approvals and propensity weighting.
16. **Label where the model looks.** With budget for 10k human labels at $10^{-4}$ prevalence, you use the current model to triage which items get labeled. Explain the bias this injects — the labeled set oversamples the model's own notion of "suspicious" — why the next model trained on it inherits the blind spots, and how propensity-weighted sampling with a random floor corrects both the estimates and the training set.
17. **In-batch negatives are biased.** Two-tower retrieval trains with in-batch softmax negatives. Show that this implicitly samples negatives proportional to item popularity, derive the log-$q$ correction — subtract $\log q(i)$ from the logit (sampled softmax; Yi et al. 2019) — and predict what happens to head versus tail items if you skip it.
18. **Rewards without an anchor.** A reward model is trained on preference *pairs* with a Bradley–Terry loss. Show why that loss identifies only reward *differences* — any per-prompt constant shift is invisible — then explain why underrepresentation of rare harm categories in the pair distribution leaves the reward model unconstrained exactly where the policy will exploit it, and connect this to diagnosing reward hacking in an RLHF pipeline.
19. **Over-refusal as false positives.** Safety examples are a tiny slice of post-training data; upweight them too hard and the model starts refusing benign prompts. Frame over-refusal as this lesson's precision/recall tradeoff operating at the policy level, and design the measurement: what *two* eval sets do you need (harmful prompts *and* benign look-alikes), and what plays the role of moving the threshold?
20. **Synthetic minorities, LLM edition.** Anthropic's Constitutional Classifiers train jailbreak detectors largely on *synthetic* attack data generated from natural-language rules. Reconcile this with the rule that oversampling adds no information: when does generative synthesis genuinely add information that SMOTE-style interpolation cannot, and what is the failure mode when the generator's distribution misses real attacks?

## Rapid-fire: the three sampling primitives — importance · rejection · reservoir

The scenario version of the imbalance question: an interviewer describes a data situation and makes you *arrive at* the sampling method rather than name it. Each of importance, rejection, and reservoir sampling solves a *different* problem — reweight vs. reshape vs. subsample-a-stream — and confusing them is the tell.

1. **Reweight, don't resample (importance sampling).** You want $\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]$ but can only draw from $q$. Derive the identity $\mathbb{E}_{p}[f(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{q}\!\big[f(x)\,\tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}\big]$ and read the weight $w(x)=p(x)/q(x)$ as "how over/under-represented this draw is." Now map it onto imbalance: training on a rebalanced set $q$ but wanting the loss under the true prevalence $p$ *is* importance sampling — show that per-class importance weights $\propto \pi_{\text{true}}/\pi_{\text{train}}$ are exactly the class weights, and that this is the reweighting alternative to physically resampling.
2. **When the weights explode.** Importance sampling is unbiased for *any* valid $q$ — so why does it fail in practice? Derive the variance of the IS estimator, show it blows up when $q$ has thinner tails than $p$ (a few samples carry enormous weight), and define the **effective sample size** $\text{ESS} = (\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$. What does $\text{ESS}=1$ mean, and why is self-normalized IS (weights normalized to sum to 1) the usual fix — at the cost of what?
3. **GOSS is importance sampling.** LightGBM keeps all large-gradient rows, subsamples the small-gradient ones at rate $b$, and multiplies the survivors' contribution by $(1-a)/b$. Derive why that specific constant is the importance weight that keeps the gradient/information-gain estimate **unbiased**, and what goes wrong if you drop it. (Same reweighting logic as He et al. 2014's downsample-then-recalibrate for ads CTR — name what each preserves.)
4. **Reshape the prior (rejection sampling).** You want draws from target $p$ but have a proposal $q$ with $p(x)\le M\,q(x)$. Derive the accept-with-probability $p(x)/(M q(x))$ rule and prove the accepted samples are distributed exactly as $p$. Then reframe *undersampling the majority* as rejection sampling: you accept every minority point and accept a majority point with probability $r<1$ to hit a target ratio — write $r$ as a function of the current and target prevalences.
5. **The acceptance-rate tax.** In rejection sampling the expected fraction accepted is $1/M$. Explain why $M$ must cover the *worst-case* ratio $\sup_x p(x)/q(x)$, why that makes the method catastrophically inefficient in high dimensions (draws rejected almost surely), and contrast with importance sampling, which "accepts" every draw but pays in weight variance instead. State the one-line rule for when you'd reach for reject-and-discard vs. reweight.
6. **Sample a stream you can't store (reservoir sampling).** Impressions arrive as an unbounded stream of unknown length $n$; you can hold only $k$ in memory and need a uniform sample of $k$. Derive Algorithm R: fill the reservoir with the first $k$, then for the $i$-th item ($i>k$) accept it with probability $k/i$ and evict a uniformly random incumbent. **Prove by induction** that after $n$ items every item is retained with probability exactly $k/n$ — and pin down where "the new item must be kept *and* every later item must not evict it" enters the argument.
7. **Reservoir, one pass, zero size known.** Answer the follow-ups an interviewer stacks on Q6: why exactly $k/i$ and not $k/n$ (you don't know $n$)? What's the memory and per-item cost, and why is this the canonical solution for "shuffle / sample a file too big for RAM" or "keep a uniform sample of a Kafka topic"? Give the one-line change for $k=1$.
8. **Balance a stream without knowing its length.** Plain reservoir sampling on a 1:1000 stream yields a reservoir that's still 1:1000 — useless for training a minority-aware model. Design **stratified / per-class reservoirs** (one reservoir per class) to hold a balanced sample from an imbalanced stream in a single pass, and state what breaks if one class is far rarer than its reservoir size (Partitioning Reservoir Sampling, Kim et al. 2020, hits exactly this in continual learning). Then extend to **weighted** reservoir sampling (A-Res, Efraimidis–Spirakis) when you want draws proportional to an importance weight — closing the loop back to Q1.
9. **Pick the primitive.** Rapid discrimination round — for each, name the *one* right tool and why: (a) unbiased offline estimate of a metric under the deployment prevalence, from a model trained on rebalanced data; (b) build a 50/50 training set from a firehose you can't buffer; (c) draw exact samples from an awkward target density with a cheap envelope; (d) correct a two-tower recommender's popularity-biased in-batch negatives. Then the trap: which of these does **oversampling/SMOTE** *not* solve, and why is "just SMOTE it" the wrong reflex for a streaming or estimation problem?
